## 5. Corrections downstream agents must respect

1. Treat "how a group settles on a way of behaving" as four layers, not four rivals: Nash/ESS tests stability, Skyrms and Camerer predict which stable state is reached absent intervention (risk dominance), Gintis names the public signal that aligns conjectures, Bicchieri specifies and measures the expectations inside agents; never present one as the theory (X5).
2. Write "risk dominance wins under random mixing, best-response updating, large groups and no communication; payoff dominance is reachable through correlation (small stable groups, success-visible imitation, public pledges, gradual growth, lower cost of being the lone cooperator)"; never "the efficient equilibrium is the rational one" (X6, C37).
3. Every time local interaction or clustering is invoked, state the condition: it helps cooperation only under success-copying (imitate-the-best), and a dense cluster blocks a norm entering from outside while protecting one adopted inside (X7, C39).
4. Willingness to pay to punish unfair intentions is [E] in the lab; "strong reciprocity is an evolved trait" is [H] (Binmore & Shaked 2010; Guala 2012); real-world sanctioning is mostly cheap and graduated, so design on the cheap version (X4, C57).
5. Do not state cultural group selection as [E]; it is [H] and never the sole basis of a design claim; within-group mechanisms (reciprocity, reputation, punishment) are the [E] part (X2, C2).
6. Do not state the collective-brain population-size effect as [E]; say "connectivity of the learning network, lab-demonstrated, archaeologically contested (Vaesen et al. 2016)"; Tasmania is a live dispute, not a settled example (C1).
7. "Culture drove brain expansion" is the shared Origins hypothesis, contested by diet and social-brain accounts (DeCasien 2017); tag [H] (X3, C7).
8. Distinguish norm adherence (spreads through models, prestige, expectations; explanation is a weak channel) from skill transfer with hidden structure (needs explicit teaching; Morgan et al. 2015, one N=184 study); never collapse Henrich and Laland into "explanation does not work" or "just document it" (X1, X13).
9. Fairness and generosity are [E] as cue- and expectation-dependent behaviour with a distribution of sensitivities; "a taste for fairness of fixed size" and any single social-preference model are not [E]; 06's own verdict is no model wins (X8, C33).
10. Assume every norm is conditional (Bicchieri) until it survives a collapse of expectations; only then call it internalized or moral; the internalization debate (Henrich/Gintis vs Bicchieri) is open (X9, C43).
11. Use Bicchieri's four-way taxonomy (descriptive, convention, social, moral) on every "norm" mentioned; "what most people do" is a descriptive norm, not a social norm (C42).
12. Cheap talk is [E] in the lab for humans in PDs and public goods (Dawes 1977 "roughly doubled", background; Sally 1995) and [E] in models for stag hunts; say it decays without sanctions or repetition where defection dominates (X12, C38).
13. Classify before prescribing: ask whether a member gains by defecting when everyone else cooperates (PD: payoffs, repetition, sanctions) or not (stag hunt: assurance); Skyrms's "the social contract is a stag hunt" is interpretive, and Ostrom's open-access cases are real PDs (X11, C31, C49).
14. Nash equilibrium is a stability test, not a prediction and not an endorsement; with multiple equilibria it predicts nothing; first-contact play is norm- and fairness-driven with one to two steps of reasoning (C25, C32).
15. Repetition permits cooperation as one equilibrium among many; selection and fast detection are separate problems; recommend "nice, retaliatory, forgiving, clear", never tit-for-tat as a rule (C26, C27).
16. Never contrast "rational" with "fair" or "cooperative"; in 04, 06 and 11 rationality means consistent preferences that may include fairness (C28).
17. For Schelling's segregation model say exactly this: it shows that mild preferences plus interdependence *can* produce extreme sorting and that homogeneity is weak evidence of intolerance; it does not show that preferences are the main real-world cause, that preferences are exogenous, or that the outcome is acceptable; empirical tipping is 5-20% minority share (Card, Mas & Rothstein 2008, verified) and the model's fragility to preference shape is disputed (C29).
18. Define tipping point as an unstable equilibrium in a threshold distribution or adoption curve; the scarce resource is low-threshold actors; never the Gladwell "influencers" sense (C30).
19. Name which kind of copying is meant every time: informational cascade (fix: collect private signals first), network effect (fix: cross the tipping point), coordination on a graph (fix: lower q or seed inside clusters); they have different fixes (glossary "cascade").
20. Do not build any recommendation on ego depletion, behavioural priming, power posing, watching eyes, the Stanford Prison Experiment, groupthink as a syndrome, or IAT-based training; the robust list is conformity, obedience, attribution error, dissonance, polarization, loafing, minimal groups, contact (moderate), bystander (low-risk settings) and cooperation-game effects (C11-C21).
21. Quote stereotype threat as "likely real in some populations and high-stakes contexts; corrected d about 0.07 for women's maths (Flore & Wicherts 2015)"; never as a large general effect (C14).
22. Quote Milgram as "65% baseline (background); 70% continued past 150 V in Burger 2009 (verified) vs roughly 82% in Milgram"; the behaviour replicates, the "blind obedience" reading does not; the design lesson is exits, peers and legitimacy of refusal (C21).
23. Bystander effect is [E] only for ambiguous, low-risk situations; in dangerous emergencies someone almost always intervenes; never tell the Kitty Genovese story as fact (C20).
24. Always pair descriptive with injunctive signals and never advertise that misbehaviour is common; descriptive feedback alone boomerangs for people above the norm (C22, X14).
25. Quote ultimatum offers as "about 40% mean, mode 40-50%" (Oosterbeek 2004, verified), rejections of offers below 20% as "about half, background", and small-scale-society offers as "about 26% to 58%" (Henrich 2001, verified); never a universal 50-50 (C34, rows 4.1-4.3).
26. Quote dictator giving as "mean about 28%, about 64% give something" (Engel 2011, verified) with the caveat that it collapses under double-blind anonymity and shifts with take options; call it fragile norm compliance, not altruism (C33, row 4.4).
27. Quote the economics replication figure as "61% of 18 experiments, effects about two-thirds of originals" (Camerer et al. 2016, verified); use lab results for mechanisms and direction, never as field point estimates (C36).
28. Treat positive reciprocity to pay raises as weak and decaying (Gneezy & List 2006, verified) and negative reciprocity to cuts and broken promises as strong (C35).
29. Present Ostrom's eight principles as a diagnostic checklist supported by a systematic review of 91 studies (Cox et al. 2010, verified) with publication-bias and structure-not-process caveats; never as a recipe, never as anti-state, never as evidence of fairness; flag scope when applied to digital or knowledge commons (C47, C48).
30. Cite "first sanctions are small and graduated; monitoring is a by-product of use" as [E] from Ostrom's cases; cite "costly altruistic punishment" only as lab behaviour (rule 4).
31. Present Gintis as a framework and checklist whose durable contributions are the epistemic critique of Nash and the choreographer idea; never write "the unification"; never attribute a review to Binmore; do not claim correlated equilibrium fits data better than Nash (C55, C56, C58).
32. The endowment-effect-as-property-psychology link and "ownership norms work because they are salient, not fair" are [E] as a model and [H] as psychology; commons regimes are rule systems, not possession cues (C57).
33. Say "short paths exist in any large network"; never quote "six degrees" as a fact; quote the structural weak-tie claim (bridges tend to be weak) and not "useful information comes from weak ties" (C50, C51).
34. Distinguish simple from complex contagion every time: information crosses one weak tie; costly behaviour needs several adopting neighbours and wide bridges (C54).
35. Assume selection over influence for any observed similarity among group members unless longitudinal data show otherwise (C52).
36. Write "conformist transmission" for disproportionate majority-copying and "success bias" for copying the successful, and note that only the latter spreads cooperation from clusters (glossary; X7).
37. Do not equate Bicchieri's cue-activation of scripts with behavioural priming; the script story is imported from psychology and more robust than specific priming effects, but still [H] for any specific cue-to-norm mapping (C12, C45).
38. Present norm-based utility as the course's working model of norms with the caveats that its superiority over inequity aversion is argued not shown and that a substantive fairness concept is still needed to say which split people expect (C45).
39. Cite "copying wins because demonstrators pre-filter" together with "it was a simulation" and "populations of pure copiers stagnate; pay for innovation" (C9).
40. Any number not in section 4 may not be quoted as a number; say small, moderate, roughly doubled; mark section-4 "background" figures as approximate in the text.

## 6. Open questions the sources cannot settle

- Whether cultural group selection is a major engine of cooperative norms or a redescription of within-group mechanisms (01 vs 02; Richerson et al. 2016 debate). [O]
- Whether norm-following is a genuinely internalized evolved disposition (01, 11) or a conditional preference that only looks internalized while expectations hold (08). [O/H]
- Whether costly one-shot punishment reflects an evolved strong-reciprocity trait or heuristics tuned for repeated interaction; and how much real-world sanctioning is costly at all (11, 06, 09). [O]
- Which correlated equilibrium a society lands on and how the correlating signal is learned; Skyrms supplies dynamics for simple games, but the general selection problem is moved up a level, not solved (11 Vanderschraaf critique; 07). [O]
- Whether market integration causes fair offers or fair people build markets, and whether game framing is equivalent across cultures (06, 01). [O]
- How large the collective-brain population/connectivity effect is outside the lab, and whether the Tasmanian record supports it (01). [O]
- Whether cultural drive, social-brain or ecological (diet) accounts best explain primate brain size (02). [O]
- Whether language evolved for teaching, gossip, bonding or signalling (02). [O]
- How robust Schelling segregation is to realistic (smooth, survey-estimated) preference functions; Bruch & Mare vs Van de Rijt et al. (05). [O]
- Whether real behaviour is contingent enough for threshold cascades to matter versus being driven by independent preferences or homophily (05, 10). [O]
- Whether Ostrom's principles transfer to large, digital or knowledge commons, and how much durability depends on entrenched inequality (09). [O]
- Whether the payoff-dominant outcome is stable in the long run under noisy best-response dynamics, or only reachable transiently under imitation (07; KMR, Young). [O]
- Whether the BPC/state-dependent-preference reframing of anomalies is falsifiable, and whether construal effects are preferences or beliefs (11, 03). [O]
- Whether gradual growth of coordinated groups (Weber 2006) generalizes beyond one lab lineage (06). [H]
- Whether descriptive/normative expectations can be disentangled in the field, given they are highly correlated in Bicchieri's own sanitation work (08). [O]
- Whether the influence-vs-selection identification problem can be resolved for behaviours that matter to the learner's group without longitudinal data (10). [O]
