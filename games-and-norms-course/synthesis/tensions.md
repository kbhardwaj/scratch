# The tensions: where the authors disagree, what the evidence says, and how to decide

Eleven books, three levels, and a running argument. Henrich and Laland disagree about what drives cultural evolution; Skyrms and Camerer say the safe equilibrium wins while Ostrom's irrigators reach the good one; Camerer writes fairness into the utility function and Bicchieri says it is not there at all; Gintis wants evolved strong reciprocity and Binmore says the lab is fooling us. None of these debates is settled by a winner. Each is settled by conditions: for whom, for what goal, at what point in the group's history, at what cost, and for how long. That is why this file exists. It takes every disagreement the claims ledger recorded (section 1, X1 to X16, and the connections the book files name) and turns it into a decision rule with a switch signal, so that a decision in your cooperation brief is never "Skyrms says" but "under these conditions, this pole; and here is the observation that would tell me I picked wrong".

Both poles are steelmanned from the book files (`books/`), which win over this file wherever they differ. Tags per SPEC: [E] supported by evidence, [H] hypothesis to test in your group, [V] value commitment. Every number is from ledger section 4 and carries its status there ("verified" or "background"); anything the ledger does not quantify is described as small, moderate or roughly doubled. Where a tension turns out to be mostly a false one once a distinction is drawn, the file says so; the disagreement is still worth keeping because the mistake it names is common.

**The list (this order is canonical; ids never change):**

1. t01 — Copying vs teaching (evolved biases vs learning strategies; models vs explanation)
2. t02 — Strong reciprocity as a trait vs conditional behaviour (costly punishment vs cheap sanctions and reputation)
3. t03 — Rational equilibrium vs norm-choreographed order (formal-rational vs social models of the group)
4. t04 — Risk dominance vs payoff dominance (and whether local clustering helps)
5. t05 — Stable social preferences vs cued norm compliance (and internalized vs conditional norms)
6. t06 — Designed rules vs self-governed rules (top-down design vs copy-and-craft, Ostrom)
7. t07 — Assurance and talk vs enforcement and commitment (stag hunt vs prisoner's dilemma; cheap talk vs costly signals)
8. t08 — Group-level selection vs within-group mechanisms (cultural group selection and the origins story)
9. t09 — Closure and homophily vs bridges and diversity (dense clusters vs bridging ties; homogeneity vs heterogeneity)
10. t10 — Broadcasting the majority vs building normative expectations (descriptive vs injunctive signals)
11. t11 — Mis-specified preferences vs unstable beliefs (Gintis's BPC vs construal)
12. t12 — Explicit incentives and metrics vs norm-based motivation (crowding out; measurement and accountability)
13. t13 — Small bounded groups vs open scale (where the learner's values enter)
14. t14 — Individual dispositions vs structure (Schelling; trendsetters vs shifting expectations broadly)

## t01. Copying vs teaching

**Pole A: people adopt behaviour by copying models, not by understanding arguments; route norms through prestige, conformity and credibility-enhancing displays.** Henrich (`01-secret-of-our-success`) argues that humans carry evolved, specifically human learning biases: prestige (Cheng et al. 2013, robust), conformist transmission, self-similarity, and sensitivity to costly displays of belief (CREDs). Over-imitation, replicated in many labs though mostly with one puzzle box, shows people copy opaque practice they cannot justify, and that this is adaptive because the payoff of the practice is delayed or diffuse. The design corollary in the book file is stark: "treat 'we explained it' as zero adoption"; budget for modelling, repetition and enforcement instead.

**Pole B: copying is one general-purpose strategy among several, and for anything with hidden causal structure, explicit teaching beats exposure.** Laland (`02-darwins-unfinished-symphony`) shows copying rules (copy when uncertain, copy the successful, copy the majority, discount stale information) are ecologically shaped strategies present even in nine-spined sticklebacks; what makes humans unusual is fidelity, teaching and language. The social-learning tournament (Rendell et al. 2010, verified; 104 entries; the winner copied almost exclusively and discounted old information) shows copying pays because demonstrators pre-filter, but it was a computer simulation, and populations of pure copiers stagnate (Rogers's paradox). The stone-tool experiment (Morgan et al. 2015, verified; N = 184 adults, five transmission conditions) found imitation added little over reverse engineering while teaching and language produced the largest gains. Laland's design corollary: prefer explanation over exposure for skills with hidden structure.

**What the evidence says.** The two authors agree on far more than they dispute: cumulative culture is the human distinction, copying is selective, demonstrators filter, and cooperation itself spreads as a copied behaviour. The ledger resolves X1 as "mostly compatible": use Henrich's bias list as the levers (whom and what people copy) and Laland's tournament logic as the reason copying pays (ledger #8, #39). The narrow real disagreement, X13, is about what is being transmitted. Once that is read properly this is mostly a false tension. Norm adherence (who contributes, who reviews, who speaks in meetings) spreads through visible models, prestige and expectations; explanation is a weak channel for it, and Henrich is right. Skill with hidden causal structure (why the deploy checklist has that step, how to size a queue) needs explicit teaching, and Laland's single modest study plus the fidelity models are right. The studies were run by Henrich's and Laland's own labs and collaborators; neither ran a horse race against the other's claim, and the Morgan result is one experiment on modern adults with one task. Two further caveats from the book files: over-imitation's developmental universality is unclear (Aka forager children emulated rather than over-imitated, Berl and Hewlett 2015), and the tournament's "copying wins" result must always be paired with "someone must be paid to innovate". [E for both mechanisms in their scope; H for the weight of any bias in your group; V for preferring prestige to dominance as the copying channel.]

**Rule.** IF the target is a norm (a behaviour whose value depends on others doing it too, or a practice with diffuse payoff) THEN put a prestigious, self-similar model visibly doing it and visibly succeeding, make leaders' CREDs point the same way, and do not count a memo or a training as adoption. IF the target is a skill with non-obvious causal structure THEN budget for explicit teaching (reasons, not just demonstrations) and put dates on the resulting playbooks so stale exemplars expire. IF the group is young with little accumulated practice THEN heavy documentation is overhead; make the best behaviour visible first and add fidelity mechanisms when there is something hard to lose. Never collapse the two into "explanation does not work" or "just document it" (ledger #8).

**Switch signal.** Toward teaching: people reproduce the surface of a practice (the ritual, the template) while the outcomes it exists for do not move, or the practice breaks the first time conditions change; that is copied form without transmitted structure. Toward modelling: the practice is well documented and explained, everyone can state the reason, and adoption is still low or confined to the people who wrote the document; that is a norm problem wearing a knowledge costume. A count that helps in both directions: how many people can explain the practice versus how many do it, measured separately.

**Common mistake.** Treating adoption as a knowledge gap and running another explanation session (the software-team default). Its mirror: assuming a copied practice carries its own reasons, so that when the prestigious model leaves or the context shifts the group keeps the ritual and loses the point; Henrich's cargo-cult side of over-imitation.

## t02. Strong reciprocity as a trait vs conditional behaviour

**Pole A: humans have an evolved disposition to cooperate conditionally and to punish defectors at personal cost, even in one-shot encounters; design can rely on it.** Gintis (`11-bounds-of-reason`, with Bowles, Boyd and Fehr) argues strong reciprocity evolved by gene-culture coevolution and group selection, and that norm-following is internalized (shame, guilt, the willingness to pay to enforce). Henrich (`01-secret-of-our-success`) supplies the norm psychology and costly third-party punishment. Camerer (`06-behavioral-game-theory`) supplies the lab base: rejection of low ultimatum offers is among the best-replicated results in behavioural economics (mean offer about 40%, mode 40-50%, Oosterbeek et al. 2004, verified; about half of offers below 20% rejected in Western student samples, background), and Blount (1995) showed people punish intended unfairness far more than unlucky outcomes.

**Pole B: one-shot lab punishment reflects heuristics tuned for repeated interaction, and real-world sanctioning is mostly cheap; design on the cheap, graduated version.** This is the critic's position and it comes from Gintis's own evidence section. Binmore and Shaked (2010, background) argue that one-shot lab behaviour is consistent with subjects misapplying rules for repeated play and that learning erodes cooperation in many designs. Guala (2012, background) argues field evidence for costly punishment in small-scale societies is thin: sanctioning in the wild is gossip and ostracism. Camerer's own file adds that rejection rates fall substantially at high stakes (Andersen et al. 2011, background) and that positive reciprocity to a gift decays within hours in the field (Gneezy and List 2006, verified) while negative reciprocity is strong. Ostrom (`09-governing-the-commons`) documents that durable commons use graduated sanctions, small and quick and public first, escalating only for repeat offenders, applied by participants at low cost; the systematic review of 91 studies (Cox et al. 2010, verified) finds the principle well supported.

**What the evidence says.** Everyone agrees on the behaviour: people will pay something to punish an unfair intention, contributions in public-goods games decay over rounds and recover when punishment is available, and pure self-interest is rejected about as securely as anything in behavioural science (the 11 file's words). The narrow disagreement is about origin and magnitude: is this an evolved trait that will show up unconditionally, or a conditional disposition that shrinks with stakes, experience and anonymity? The ledger splits the claim (X4, ledger #4, #30): willingness to pay to punish unfair intentions in the lab is [E]; "evolved strong reciprocity" is [H], the 11 file's own verdict being "contested; the evidence supports a weaker version"; "real-world sanctioning is mostly cheap and graduated" is [E] from Ostrom's cases and Guala. The studies on the trait side were run by the strong-reciprocity school itself (Fehr, Gächter, Henrich, Gintis); the critics are Binmore, Shaked and Guala; the field evidence is Ostrom's and the Cox review. Note the replication frame from Camerer et al. 2016 (verified): 61% of 18 top-journal experiments replicated with effects about two-thirds of originals, so even the robust lab effects are smaller than first reported. Note also that this is also a tension between punishment and reputation as levers: the trait reading makes punishment the engine, the conditional reading makes observation, reputation and cheap disapproval the engine, with formal penalty as the rare backstop. [E for lab punishment and for cheap field sanctions; H for the evolved trait and for how much costly punishment your members will do; V for constraining peer punishment with a legitimate process.]

**Rule.** IF you are budgeting enforcement THEN assume members will sanction free riders only when the channel is cheap, fast and proportionate: gossip-grade disapproval, a public log, a quiet word, a graduated escalation with a known ladder; and make monitoring a by-product of participation rather than a police function (Ostrom). IF the violation is an unfair intention rather than an unlucky outcome THEN expect costly retaliation and pre-empt it with procedurally impartial allocation (rules, rotation, lottery), since people forgive bad luck and punish bad intent. IF stakes are high or the group is experienced with the game THEN discount the lab punishment rate further and rely on reputation and repeated interaction, not on spontaneous costly enforcement. Never build a design that only works if members reliably punish at personal cost with no institutional support, and never let peer punishment run without a legitimate process, because antisocial punishment (retaliation against cooperators) is real (ledger #4, `01` design implications).

**Switch signal.** Toward the trait pole: members actually pay observable costs to sanction (time, relationships, standing) with no expectation of future dealings, repeatedly, and the sanctioned behaviour falls. Toward the conditional pole: enforcement that was supposed to be spontaneous is not happening (violations accumulate with nobody acting), or punishment is happening but going underground into feuds and retaliation against the wrong targets, or sanctioning collapses when stakes rise or anonymity increases. The count: violations observed versus violations sanctioned, and by whom.

**Common mistake.** Designing for heroes: assuming the group will self-police at cost, then discovering that everyone waited for someone else (the bystander structure from `03-social-psychology`, [E] in low-risk ambiguous settings). Its mirror: concluding that because punishment is not an evolved reflex it does not matter, and removing all sanctions, after which contributions decay exactly as Camerer's public-goods curves predict.

## t03. Rational equilibrium vs norm-choreographed order

**Pole A: a group settles where no one gains by deviating alone; model the group as rational players, find the Nash equilibria, and patch selection with focal points.** Dixit, Skeath and McAdams (`04-games-of-strategy`) give the formal-rational model: write the matrix, find the equilibria, use rollback for sequential moves, use the folk theorem for repeated play; where equilibria are multiple, focal points, learning and evolutionary dynamics are added on, and the book admits the seam. Rationality here is consistent pursuit of whatever the payoffs contain, fairness included (ledger #16). Skyrms (`07-stag-hunt`) supplies the dynamic version: which stable pattern a population reaches is set by basins of attraction and correlation, with no beliefs at all.

**Pole B: Nash cannot be derived from rationality alone; groups reach order because a social signal (a norm) aligns everyone's conjectures, and the signal has to be inside the agents as expectations.** Gintis (`11-bounds-of-reason`) proves, via Aumann and Brandenburger, that common knowledge of rationality does not yield Nash; what Bayesian rationality plus a common prior yields is correlated equilibrium, and the correlating device in human groups is the norm, the choreographer. Bicchieri (`08-grammar-of-society`) specifies what must be true inside the agents for the signal to work: a conditional preference keyed to empirical expectations (what others do) and normative expectations (what others think one should do), with the game changing only while those expectations are live. Camerer (`06`) adds that first-contact play is norm- and fairness-driven with one to two steps of reasoning (beauty-contest first-round average about 35-36, Nagel 1995, verified; cognitive-hierarchy mean about 1.5 steps, Camerer, Ho and Chong 2004, verified).

**What the evidence says.** This is the course's central reconciliation and the ledger makes it binding (X5, ledger #1): the four accounts are layers, not rivals. Nash or ESS is the stability test for any candidate pattern; Skyrms and Camerer predict which stable pattern a group reaches absent intervention (risk dominance, t04); Gintis names what a norm does (supply a public signal that aligns conjectures; the theorem-backed part of his book); Bicchieri specifies and measures the expectations that make the signal bite. Where they genuinely disagree is narrower: whether an explicit choreographer is needed or history plus a focal point suffices (Schelling's `05` position, "focal points are choreographers without an author"), and whether order arrives by learning (Camerer's path-dependent learning models) or by norms (Gintis). The 11 file's own assessment concedes the choreographer is weaker than Bicchieri on diagnosis, Ostrom on authorship and Skyrms on origins, and Vanderschraaf's review presses that Gintis moved the equilibrium-selection gap up one level without closing it. The formal results are theorems (Aumann 1974/1987; Aumann and Brandenburger 1995); the empirical claim that correlated equilibrium fits data better than Nash is not made by the book and must not be made here (ledger #31, C58). Nash is a stability test, not a prediction and not an endorsement (ledger #14). [E for the formal relations and for first-contact behaviour being norm-driven; H for which layer bites in a given group; V for whether a designed choreographer is preferable to an emergent one.]

**Rule.** IF you are checking whether a proposed pattern can last THEN apply the formal test (does any member gain by deviating alone given the others hold?) and treat failure as fatal regardless of how good the pattern is. IF the pattern passes but the group is not there THEN stop reasoning people toward it and name the signal: a public cue (rota, written rule, calendar, default, dashboard) that tells each person which equilibrium is in play; ambiguity, not incentives, is the usual failure (`11` design implications). IF the signal exists and compliance is still low THEN measure the expectations (do people believe others comply; do they believe others expect them to) because a choreographer nobody believes is a poster. Never present one of the four as "the" theory of norms, and never contrast "rational" with "fair" (ledger #1, #16).

**Switch signal.** Toward the rational-equilibrium pole: members' behaviour is stable across framings and labels and responds only to payoff changes; you are dealing with experienced players in a familiar game, where Camerer says play approaches equilibrium. Toward the norm pole: the same payoff matrix produces different behaviour under different labels (the "Community Game" label roughly doubled prisoner's-dilemma cooperation versus "Wall Street Game", Liberman, Samuels and Ross 2004, background) or when observation changes; or a meeting has derived the "obviously rational" plan and behaviour has not moved. The observation: does behaviour track the payoffs or the cues?

**Common mistake.** Reading Nash as a forecast and building an incentive scheme that assumes the group will find and hold the equilibrium the designer computed (the product-manager default). Its mirror: concluding from Gintis that rational choice is refuted and dropping the stability test, so that the norm you install is one everyone would gain by defecting from and it dies the first week.

## t04. Risk dominance vs payoff dominance

**Pole A: under uncertainty the safe equilibrium wins; expect groups to settle on the low-effort, low-trust outcome and treat that as the structural default.** Skyrms (`07-stag-hunt`) shows that under replicator dynamics with random mixing the risk-dominant equilibrium (best reply against a 50/50 opponent) wins because its basin is larger; the file's illustrative watershed is p* = 3/4 stag hunters needed with payoffs Stag/Stag 4, Stag/Hare 0, Hare 3 (the file's own numbers). Under best-response updating local interaction accelerates risk dominance (Ellison 1993), and with persistent noise the risk-dominant outcome is the long-run stochastically stable one (Kandori, Mailath and Rob 1993; Young 1993). Camerer (`06`) supplies the lab: without communication, play converges to the risk-dominant or minimum-effort outcome (Cooper et al. 1990/92), and weak-link groups of 14-16 fell to effort 1 within about ten rounds while fixed pairs reached 7 (Van Huyck, Battalio and Beil 1990; group-size result verified, round counts background).

**Pole B: the payoff-dominant outcome is reachable, because correlation (structure, talk, history, partner choice) expands the good basin.** Skyrms Parts I-III: local interaction with imitate-the-best, cheap-talk handshakes and reinforcement-based partner choice let stag hunting and fair division spread from small clusters (fairness contagion in tens of generations under local imitation versus hundreds under random mixing, Alexander and Skyrms 1999, background). Camerer's post-2003 evidence: two-way cheap talk, a leader publicly naming the target, gradual growth from a small core with visible history (Weber 2006, verified, single-lab lineage), forward induction and a lowered payoff to the safe action all select the efficient equilibrium. Easley and Kleinberg (`10-networks-crowds-markets`) give the network version: adoption spreads when a node's adopting-neighbour fraction exceeds q = b/(a+b) (theorem), so lowering q or seeding inside a cluster spreads costly behaviour; Centola's experiment found clustered networks spread behaviour faster than random ones (background).

**What the evidence says.** The poles agree completely on the game: in a stag hunt the payoffs already favour cooperation, so coordination failure is not a payoff problem but strategic uncertainty plus history (ledger C37; `06` misreadings). They agree on the default: absent intervention, risk dominance. The real disagreement is whether the payoff-dominant outcome is a stable destination or a transient that noise eventually erodes, and it hinges on the learning rule: Skyrms's optimism rests on imitation dynamics and transient analysis; the pessimistic theorems assume best response with noise (`07` evidence section; open question in ledger section 6). The ledger's binding wording (rule #2): risk dominance wins under random mixing, best-response updating, large groups and no communication; payoff dominance is reachable through correlation (small stable groups, success-visible imitation, public pledges, gradual growth, lower cost of being the lone cooperator). "Sweeten the joint payoff" is the weakest lever (`07` ch 1; raising Stag/Hare from 0 to 2 in the file's example moves the watershed from 3/4 to 1/2, illustrative). Clustering is the same conditional in network form (X7, ledger #3): local structure helps only under success-copying, and a dense cluster (density greater than 1 − q) blocks a norm entering from outside while protecting one adopted inside. The lab work is Cooper's, Van Huyck's and Weber's; the models are Skyrms's, Ellison's, Young's and Kleinberg's. [E for the models and the lab; H for which regime your group is in and for whether the good outcome is stable long-run.]

**Rule.** IF matching is near-random, the group is large, people best-respond to what they last saw and there is no channel for talk or history THEN plan for risk dominance: cap the group whose minimum matters at a handful, or change the aggregation rule from minimum to median or average (`06`). IF you can create correlation THEN do the cheap things first and in this order: lower the cost of being the lone cooperator (the Stag/Hare payoff), have a leader publicly name the target equilibrium, take public opt-in pledges, grow from a small core with a visible success record, keep neighbourhoods small and stable while the norm is young. IF you are relying on local clusters THEN check the learning rule: make cooperators' success visible so imitate-the-best can operate; if people copy the majority instead, clusters will entrench the safe outcome. Never write "the efficient equilibrium is the rational one" or treat its absence as irrationality (ledger #2), and never launch a coordination-dependent practice at full size without history.

**Switch signal.** Toward the risk-dominance pole (stop pushing, restructure): effort or participation falls round on round toward the minimum in a group above roughly a dozen; the people who tried first are visibly worse off than those who waited. Toward payoff dominance (the levers are working): a small core's success is visible and the adoption fraction among any member's neighbours is rising past the point where switching pays. Watch the lone-cooperator payoff directly: if trying alone costs more than not trying, no amount of exhortation will move the watershed.

**Common mistake.** Raising the reward for joint success (bonuses, recognition for the whole team) when the problem is the cost of being the one who trusted; this moves the watershed least. Its mirror: assuming that because the group is small and talks, the payoff-dominant outcome is secured for good, and then rotating people randomly across teams, which Skyrms's dynamics say re-randomizes matching and hands the basin back to risk dominance.

## t05. Stable social preferences vs cued norm compliance

**Pole A: fairness and reciprocity are stable preferences; put them in the payoffs and model members as types.** Camerer (`06-behavioral-game-theory`) models social preferences as utility parameters (Fehr-Schmidt inequity aversion, Charness-Rabin, intention-based models), and Dixit et al. (`04`) and Gintis (`11`) say the same in different words: "put fairness in the payoffs" (BPC: beliefs, preferences, constraints). The type-distribution summary (roughly 30% self-interested, about 50% conditional cooperators, a small tail of altruists, background; Bruhin, Fehr and Schunk 2019 estimate about 40% strongly other-regarding, background) is a design tool: let conditional cooperators see others cooperating. Henrich and Gintis add that norm-following is genuinely internalized: shame, guilt and the willingness to punish are inside the person, not conditional on expectations.

**Pole B: fair behaviour is norm compliance activated by cues and expectations, not a taste of fixed size; and every norm is conditional until proved otherwise.** Bicchieri (`08-grammar-of-society`) argues fixed preferences cannot explain the variance of fair behaviour with anonymity, entitlement, moral wiggle room (Dana, Weber and Kuang 2007) and information. Dictator giving (mean about 28%, about 64% give something, Engel 2011, verified) collapses under double-blind anonymity, falls with earned endowments and shifts when a take option is added (List 2007; Bardsley 2008); that is the signature of a cued norm, not a stable parameter. Bicchieri and Xiao (2009, verified) found empirical expectations of selfishness reduced giving even when normative messages favoured giving. Henrich's own cross-cultural data (mean ultimatum offers about 26% to about 58% across 15 small-scale societies, verified) show the disposition to have norms is universal but the content is not.

**What the evidence says.** The agreement is broad: people are neither selfish nor saintly; behaviour is heterogeneous; reciprocity and intention-sensitivity dominate over unconditional altruism (`06` misreadings; ledger C33). The narrow disagreements are two. First, X8: are the parameters stable? Camerer's own verdict is that no single social-preference model wins and estimated parameters are unstable across games, which is close to conceding Bicchieri's point; the ledger rules (ledger #9) that fairness is [E] as cue- and expectation-dependent behaviour with a distribution of sensitivities, and that "a taste for fairness of fixed size" is not [E]. Second, X9: is anything internalized? Bicchieri confines unconditional compliance to the "moral norm" bin; Henrich and Gintis think the bin is large. The ledger's operational test (ledger #10) is one all files accept: assume the norm is conditional until it survives a collapse of expectations; only if compliance persists when people believe others no longer comply or care, call it internalized. Caveats: Bicchieri's superiority over inequity aversion is argued from fit, not shown in a horse race, and critics note a substantive fairness concept still sneaks back in to say which split people expect (ledger #38, C45); her taxonomy is cleaner than her own field data, where empirical and normative expectations are highly correlated. Camerer's corpus is student-lab, WEIRD, one-shot; Bicchieri's later field work is in sanitation and child-marriage programmes. [E for cue-dependence and for type heterogeneity as a qualitative fact; H for any parametric model and for the internalized share; V for treating process fairness as a good in itself, which `06` tags as a value.]

**Rule.** IF you are deciding what fairness baseline to design to THEN elicit it from the group (what do most people here do; what do most think you should do), never import a lab or a 50-50 default (ledger #25). IF you want generosity or contribution to persist THEN design the cues and the observation (identifiability, visible consequences for others, no exits that let people dodge unseen), because that is what the fragility of dictator giving says moves it. IF you want to know whether a norm is safe to lean on THEN run the collapse test: tell a subgroup, truthfully, that compliance elsewhere has fallen, and watch whether they hold; design for the conditional case because it is the cheaper one to lose (ledger X9). Never state a member type share as a fact about your group, and never run values workshops for a norm you have not first diagnosed as moral rather than social (ledger #10, C43).

**Switch signal.** Toward the preference pole: behaviour holds across anonymity, framing and reported changes in others' behaviour; the same people are generous in every context; that is either a moral norm or a stable type and either way it can be leaned on. Toward the norm pole: contribution tracks what people believe others do more closely than what they say they value; anonymity or a new exit option changes it; the two-step elicitation shows a gap between attitude and expectation. The observation: does the behaviour move when the expectation moves?

**Common mistake.** Attributing free-riding to "selfish types" and hiring, firing or screening for character, when the behaviour is a cue and expectation problem that the same people would not show under observation. Its mirror: treating every behaviour as infinitely cue-dependent and forgetting the 30% or so who, in every lab, do not cooperate no matter what others do; those are the reason t02's sanctions exist.

## t06. Designed rules vs self-governed rules

**Pole A: institutions are designed; a competent designer with the right toolkit writes rules that make contribution a best response.** Dixit et al. (`04-games-of-strategy`) supply the mechanism-design view: choose decision rules on purpose, set sanctions to be subgame-perfect, randomize monitoring at probability roughly gain divided by penalty, sort types at the boundary with costly signals. Easley and Kleinberg (`10`) tag "prefer institutional edits over exhortation" as a value they endorse. The stronger version of Pole A is external authorship: a central authority or platform owner writes and enforces the rules, which is the standard policy prescription Ostrom's book was written against.

**Pole B: durable rules are crafted incrementally by the people bound by them, and mostly copied from neighbours rather than invented.** Ostrom (`09-governing-the-commons`) shows appropriators in Törbel (the 1517 cap: no more cows on the alp than one can winter), the Spanish huertas, the Philippine zanjeras and the California basins deliberately crafted, monitored and revised rules, and that institutional change was incremental and self-transforming, using courts and enabling law as backstop rather than author. Principle 3 (collective-choice arrangements: those affected can change the rules) is the core; the 91-study review (Cox et al. 2010, verified) finds the principles well supported, with the caveat that cases were selected on the dependent variable and the coding is qualitative. Henrich (`01`) goes further in the anti-design direction: institutions were mostly copied from visibly successful neighbours and modified under group competition, with designers rationalizing afterwards; Cleaver's "institutional bricolage" (in `09`'s critiques) says rules are assembled from existing practice.

**What the evidence says.** The ledger resolves X10 as "conditional and mostly compatible": Ostrom's own cases show incremental crafting rather than a constitutional act, and Henrich's copy-and-modify includes the modification. The compatible reading: copy the norm package of a visibly successful peer group, then craft incrementally with the people bound by the rules. Neither "design from scratch" nor "just copy" is [E]. The real disagreement is about authorship and legitimacy: Ostrom's principle 7 (external recognition of the right to organize) and her lab and field evidence that weakly enforced external regulation can crowd out communication-driven cooperation (Cardenas, background) say imposed rules are followed only while enforced; the mechanism-design toolkit is silent on who may change the rules, and Dixit's file concedes that with more than two players "who punishes whom" is hard and Ostrom's monitoring principles are the empirical answer. The critiques in the 09 file matter for the learner: the principles describe structure, not the processes (trust, legitimacy, transparency) that make structure work; Agrawal counted around 30 or more success conditions across the literature; Ostrom herself warned against panaceas; durable cases lean on courts and enabling law and several are inegalitarian. This is also one of the two tensions where the learner's own values enter explicitly: "those bound by a rule may change it" is tagged [E] for durability in `09` and [V] for polycentric arrangements; a designer who prefers control is making a value choice, not an evidence-based one. [E for participant authorship and graduated sanctions as features of durable institutions; H for transfer to teams and digital commons (ledger #29, C48); V for who ought to hold the pen.]

**Rule.** IF the group already communicates, observes itself and has any capacity to change its rules THEN fix those channels before writing rules, then let the members craft operational rules against a diagnostic checklist (the eight principles as questions, not features) and reserve external authority for recognition, backstop and data. IF the group is new, large or the problem is one members cannot see (an accounting constraint, a sorting externality) THEN design the structure (boundaries, monitoring by-product, a graduated ladder, a cheap dispute forum) and copy the package from a visibly successful peer group, but hand the operational rules to the members within weeks and put a date on the first revision. IF an external authority insists on writing the rules THEN expect compliance only while enforced and budget for enforcement accordingly. Never present the principles as a recipe, as anti-state, or as evidence of fairness (ledger #29); never design from scratch what a neighbouring group has already made work.

**Switch signal.** Toward design: members cannot agree, cannot observe each other, or the problem involves an externality nobody experiences directly; the "consult on voluntary adoption" route has produced a half-launch. Toward self-governance: rules imposed from above are complied with only when someone is watching, sanctions are appealed over the group's head, or the group is already quietly running its own workarounds (bricolage) that work better than the official rule. The observation: who changed the last rule, and did compliance change when enforcement attention moved elsewhere?

**Common mistake.** The platform default: writing a complete rulebook and a moderation policy centrally and being surprised that the community treats it as someone else's law. Its mirror: romanticizing self-governance and skipping external recognition, so that the first serious dispute is appealed to management or the platform owner, who has never agreed the group may govern this domain.

## t07. Assurance and talk vs enforcement and commitment

**Pole A: most cooperation problems are trust problems; once repetition and reputation are included they are stag hunts, and what they need is assurance, which cheap talk provides.** Skyrms (`07`) argues the social contract is a stag hunt rather than a prisoner's dilemma: the shadow of the future turns temptation into trust, and cheap talk works as a transient handshake that expands the stag basin. Dixit et al. (`04`) agree most cooperation problems are not PDs and that visible pledges and thresholds ("we start when ten sign up") reach the payoff-dominant outcome cheaply. Bicchieri (`08`) supplies the strongest empirical claim: relevant face-to-face discussion is the single strongest lever on public-goods and PD cooperation, raising it from roughly 30% to roughly 70% in an eight-person dilemma (Dawes, McTavish and Shaklee 1977, background; quote as "roughly doubled"), with Sally 1995 and Balliet 2010 meta-analyses behind it; Gilovich et al. (`03`) and Ostrom's lab work with Walker and Gardner agree.

**Pole B: many group problems are genuine multi-person prisoner's dilemmas, talk does not fix them, and what they need is a payoff change, repetition with fast detection, sanctions, or a costly commitment.** Ostrom (`09`) shows open-access commons and the California pumping race were real PDs, refuting the PD as a law but keeping it for open access (ledger C49). Schelling (`05`) built the binary-choice diagram precisely to tell MPDs from coordination problems and warns that communication will not fix an MPD. Dixit et al. say cheap talk informs only when interests are aligned, and give the alternatives: costly signals that separate committed from opportunistic types because the cost is asymmetric, commitment devices, credible subgame-perfect sanctions. Skyrms concedes that in a true PD mimics invade the talkers and the handshake decays; Camerer (`06`) shows public-goods contributions decay over rounds and recover with punishment.

**What the evidence says.** The poles agree on the diagnostic, and the ledger makes it binding (X11, ledger #13): ask whether a member gains by defecting when everyone else cooperates. If yes, it is a PD and needs payoff change, repetition or sanctions; if no, it is a stag hunt and needs assurance. Skyrms's thesis that the social contract is a stag hunt is interpretive and untested (`07` evidence section); some commons are PDs at the margin. On cheap talk the disagreement is narrower than it looks (X12, ledger #12): Skyrms's agents have no normative term, so in his models talk is transient in a PD; Bicchieri's account, that relevant talk creates empirical and normative expectations and so transforms the PD into a coordination game in human subjects, explains the lab effect. Course rule: cheap talk is [E] in the lab for humans in PDs and public goods, [E] in models for stag hunts, and expected to decay without sanctions or repetition where defection dominates. The lab studies are Dawes's, Sally's meta-analysis, Ostrom-Walker-Gardner's and Camerer's; the decay curves are from public-goods experiments across many labs. Costly signals and commitment are theorems in `04` with the [H] caveat that any given ritual has the required cost asymmetry. [E for the diagnostic and the lab effect of talk; H for persistence without sanctions and for any group's classification; V for preferring assurance to enforcement where both would work.]

**Rule.** IF nobody gains by defecting when everyone else cooperates (stag hunt) THEN build assurance and stop building enforcement: public intent statements, opt-in pledges, visible sign-ups, a threshold launch, a leader naming the target; sanctions here are dead weight. IF a member does gain by defecting against full cooperation (PD or MPD) THEN talk still helps and should be held before the mixed-motive decision (extract explicit promises, make people state what they expect), but pair it with the things that stop decay: repetition with defection detected within a round or two, graduated sanctions, or a payoff change; and where membership is the lever, screen with a costly signal (probationary contribution, onboarding effort) whose cost is higher for opportunists. IF you cannot tell which game it is THEN run the talk first because it is cheap, and watch whether cooperation decays over the following rounds; decay is the PD's signature. Never treat "we discussed it and agreed" as a durable fix for a behaviour where defection pays (ledger #12), and never build sanctions for a trust problem.

**Switch signal.** Toward enforcement: cooperation after a good discussion decays round on round; the people who kept their promise are worse off than those who broke it; free riders are increasing, not just persisting. Toward assurance: the group cooperates whenever it believes others will, defections come in waves triggered by doubts about others rather than by opportunity, and sanctions when tried have hit people who were merely uncertain. The observation: measure cooperation at the meeting and again several rounds later; a stag hunt holds, a PD sags.

**Common mistake.** Treating a PD as a communication problem and running another alignment offsite (`05` misreadings). Its mirror: treating a stag hunt as a PD and installing monitoring and penalties on people who were only waiting for assurance, which signals distrust, lowers empirical expectations and can manufacture the defection it feared.

## t08. Group-level selection vs within-group mechanisms

**Pole A: cooperative norms exist because groups carrying them out-competed, out-grew or were imitated over groups without them; the unit of design is the group's norm package and its identity.** Henrich (`01-secret-of-our-success`) argues cultural group selection (war, migration, imitation, differential growth) selected cooperation-enhancing norm packages; the Nuer expansion and the Ilahita cult are the cases. Gintis (`11`) leans on group selection for strong reciprocity. The same Origins books share a second hypothesis: culture drove brain expansion (Henrich: the payoff of learning from others; Laland: a fidelity threshold plus teaching), with Street et al. 2017 (verified, correlational) as support.

**Pole B: cooperation is scaffolded by socially learned norms and within-group mechanisms (reciprocity, reputation, punishment), which suffice; group selection is a redescription with weak evidence, and the brain story is contested.** Laland (`02-darwins-unfinished-symphony`) is explicitly sceptical of cultural group selection; the 2016 Richerson et al. target article drew many critical commentaries asking whether between-group variation is large and stable enough and whether "imitation of successful groups" is selection at all. On brains, DeCasien et al. 2017 found diet predicts primate brain size better than sociality, and the social-brain account remains live.

**What the evidence says.** The poles agree on the within-group mechanisms and on their being the well-evidenced part; they agree culture and brains co-evolved in some direction; they even agree Nuer and Ilahita are illustrative rather than statistical (the 01 file says so). The ledger settles the design consequence firmly (X2, X3; ledger #5, #7): within-group mechanisms are [E]; cultural group selection is [H] and never the load-bearing explanation for a design recommendation; "culture drove brains" is the shared Origins hypothesis, contested, [H]. The tension is worth keeping because it changes what a designer reaches for. A group-selection lens suggests inter-group competition, a strong superordinate identity and copying whole norm packages from winning groups; a within-group lens suggests reciprocity, observation, reputation and sanctions inside the group. The book files give partial support to both at the design level: minimal-group in-group favouritism and intergroup contact (reliable moderate effect across 500+ studies, Pettigrew and Tropp 2006, background; rigorous field experiments fewer and smaller) are robust in `03`; Robbers Cave is a demonstration, not an estimate (ledger C19); and copying a successful peer group's package is [H] in t06. The Origins evidence is Henrich's, Boyd's and Richerson's on one side, Laland's lab and DeCasien on the other; none of it was designed to test team-level interventions. [H for group selection and for culture-drove-brains; E for within-group reciprocity, reputation and sanctions; H for identity-based interventions in your group.]

**Rule.** IF you are choosing between a group-identity intervention (rivalry, superordinate identity, "our way") and a within-group mechanism (observation, reciprocity, graduated sanctions) THEN build the within-group mechanism first, because it is the evidenced part, and treat identity work as an accelerant tagged [H]. IF you use inter-group competition as a motivator THEN pair it with structured contact and a superordinate goal across the boundary, because the same psychology that binds the in-group generates hostility across it (`03`; contact is [E] moderate, competition-as-cure is a demonstration). IF you want to import a norm package THEN copy from a visibly successful peer group and then craft locally (t06). Never justify a design decision by "groups with this norm out-compete groups without it" (ledger #5).

**Switch signal.** Toward the group-level pole: cooperation inside the group is already reciprocal and monitored and the residual problem is commitment to the collective as such (turnover, indifference, no shared story); or a rival group's visible success is producing spontaneous imitation. Toward the within-group pole: strong identity coexists with free-riding (people are proud members who do not contribute), or identity work has produced hostility to adjacent teams without raising contribution. The observation: contribution rates versus identification measures, tracked separately.

**Common mistake.** Reaching for identity, ritual and team-versus-team competition (ritual bonding is [H], ledger C5) as the primary cooperation lever because they are visible and cheap to announce. Its mirror: dismissing identity entirely and running a group as a reciprocity machine, which the minimal-group literature says forgoes a real, if moderate, effect.

## t09. Closure and homophily vs bridges and diversity

**Pole A: dense, closed, similar groups are where trust, enforcement and complex contagion work; protect closure and bounded membership.** Easley and Kleinberg (`10`) show closure gives trust and enforcement (ch 3), that costly behaviour needs several adopting neighbours and so does not jump a single weak tie (complex contagion, Centola and Macy 2007; ledger #34), and that a cluster of density greater than 1 − q both blocks an outside norm and protects an inside one. Skyrms (`07`) makes correlation, any departure from random mixing that makes like meet like, his master variable for rescuing cooperation. Ostrom (`09`) finds the design principles hold in small, dense, bounded groups. Homophily is a pervasive regularity (McPherson, Smith-Lovin and Cook 2001), and the 2pq test (fewer cross-boundary edges than 2pq indicates homophily, theorem) is how you measure it.

**Pole B: connectivity, bridges and heterogeneity are where information, innovation and skill retention come from; silos lose skills and homogeneous groups polarize.** Henrich and Laland (`01`, `02`) argue larger, better-connected learning networks retain and innovate more; isolation loses skills (Tasmania, contested; lab transmission chains, small scale). Easley and Kleinberg show bridges tend to be weak ties (Onnela et al. 2007, robust) and carry novelty. Gilovich et al. (`03`) show homogeneous groups polarize and prescribe injecting outside views before decisions and guaranteeing a credible dissenter. Laland's tournament adds that populations of pure copiers stagnate: someone must be paid to innovate.

**What the evidence says.** The ledger's resolution of X15 is "both": connectivity for information and skill retention; closure and bounded membership for enforcement and complex contagion. The files agree on the mechanism-level facts and on two corrections that discipline the debate: the collective-brain population-size claim is contested (Vaesen et al. 2016) and reframed as network connectivity, not head-count (ledger #6); and "useful information comes from weak ties" is not robust (Gee et al. 2017 found most jobs via strong ties, background), only the structural claim that bridges tend to be weak (ledger #33). The real disagreement is a design trade-off, not a factual one: every unit of closure you add to make enforcement and costly-norm spread work is a unit of bridging you forgo for information, and vice versa. The homogeneity-diversity axis maps onto it: homophily produces the clusters that hold norms and block them (X7), and it produces the polarization `03` warns about; diversity produces the dissent and novelty and weakens complex contagion. Influence versus selection is unresolved for any observed similarity (Shalizi and Thomas 2011; ledger #35), so "the team converged because they influence each other" should be assumed false until longitudinal data say otherwise. Studies: Granovetter, Onnela, Centola, McPherson on the network side; Henrich's and Laland's transmission-chain and comparative work on connectivity; the polarization literature in `03`. [E for the trade-off and each mechanism; H for the right mix in any group; V for how much diversity of view you want at the cost of speed of alignment.]

**Rule.** IF the job is to hold a costly norm, enforce it and spread it neighbour by neighbour THEN keep the working units small, dense and bounded, and when spreading to a resisting cluster build wide bridges (several parallel ties) rather than a single liaison, or seed inside the cluster. IF the job is information, innovation or retaining rare skills THEN find and protect the local bridges (usually weak ties, which decay unattended), rotate demonstrators into siloed teams, and pay explicitly for the innovators a copying population will not fund. IF the group is homogeneous and about to decide THEN inject outside views before, not after, and have leaders speak last. Never say "clusters are bad" or "more connection is always better" (ledger #3, X15), and never infer influence from similarity.

**Switch signal.** Toward closure: a norm you seeded keeps failing to take because nobody's neighbours have adopted it (adoption below q everywhere), or enforcement is failing because violators have no repeated dealings with anyone who saw them. Toward bridges: the same solutions keep being reinvented in different subteams, a rare skill sits with one person, or decisions are unanimous fast and wrong (polarization). The counts: the 2pq cross-boundary ratio for the traits you care about; the number of parallel ties between any two subgroups; how many people hold each critical skill.

**Common mistake.** Reorganizing for cross-pollination (open floor plans, rotating squads, matrix reporting) while a young cooperative norm is still being held by a few dense pockets, and dissolving the pockets. Its mirror: protecting closure until the group is a silo with one point of failure per skill and a comfortable consensus nobody outside shares.

## t10. Broadcasting the majority vs building normative expectations

**Pole A: make the target behaviour look like majority behaviour; people copy the majority under uncertainty.** Henrich (`01`) grounds this in conformist transmission: publish counts where adoption is high and avoid advertising low adoption because "most people don't do this" is itself transmitted. Gilovich et al. (`03`) supply the field evidence: descriptive-norm feedback on energy use produces about a 2% reduction, small but reliable at scale (Allcott 2011, building on Schultz et al. 2007, background); Asch conformity is robust across 133 studies in 17 countries (Bond and Smith 1996, verified), with about a third of critical trials and about 75% of participants conforming at least once in the original (background), and conformity rising to a plateau at about three or four others (background).

**Pole B: empirical expectations alone do not make a social norm; without the normative channel descriptive messaging misfires and can boomerang.** Bicchieri (`08`) defines the social norm by both expectations (what others do, and what others think one should do), and reads the mixed results of social-norms marketing, including boomerangs, as confirmation that the descriptive channel alone is not enough. Gilovich et al. agree from the other side: Schultz et al. 2007 found below-average users increased consumption when told the average, unless an injunctive smiley was added; the Petrified Forest sign that advertised widespread theft raised theft.

**What the evidence says.** This is the file's clearest case of a false tension once the taxonomy is read properly. The ledger records X14 as "agreement once stated carefully" (ledger #24): descriptive and injunctive signals must point the same way, and the injunctive channel is required for mixed-motive behaviour. Henrich's "use conformity deliberately" and Bicchieri's "empirical expectations are necessary but not sufficient" are the same rule stated from different levels. The genuine residue is small: Henrich's conformist bias operates on behaviour with uncertain payoffs, where matching the majority is informative; Bicchieri's social norms operate on mixed-motive behaviour, where matching the majority is not enough because one gains by deviating and needs to believe others disapprove of deviating. Pluralistic ignorance (`08` ch 5, `03`) is the failure mode both sides recognize: most privately reject a norm, believe others accept it, and comply; disapproval has to become common knowledge to matter (ledger C44). This also bears on how norm change starts (see t14): publishing a measured expectation gap is the broad-shift route, trendsetting is the seeded route; both need the normative channel. Studies: Cialdini's and Schultz's on the boomerang; Allcott's field trial; Asch and the Bond-Smith meta-analysis; Prentice and Miller on campus drinking; Bicchieri and Xiao 2009 (verified), where expectations of selfishness beat pro-giving messages. [E for the pairing rule and the boomerang; H for the size of the effect in your group, which at scale is about 2% in the one field number the ledger allows; V for whether nudging by counts is a legitimate way to treat members.]

**Rule.** IF the target behaviour is already the majority's THEN publish the count and pair it with a visible approval signal (who is recognized, what leaders say), so the descriptive and injunctive channels align. IF the target behaviour is a minority's THEN do not publish prevalence at all; instead measure the expectation gap (what do you do; what do most do; what do most think you should do; what happens if you don't) and, if private attitudes are better than perceived norms, make that gap common knowledge with a credible source. IF the behaviour is mixed-motive (someone gains by deviating) THEN never rely on the descriptive channel alone; add the normative one, backed where necessary by the sanctions of t02. Never advertise how common misbehaviour is in order to shame it (ledger #24).

**Switch signal.** Toward broadcasting: private attitudes and actual behaviour are both good and only perceived prevalence is wrong (the elicitation shows people underestimate compliance). Toward the normative channel: after publishing a favourable count the above-norm members regress toward it (the boomerang), or people can state the count and still do not believe anyone expects them to comply. The count: the four-question elicitation before and after any messaging.

**Common mistake.** The dashboard reflex: publishing adoption metrics for a practice that is at 20% and calling it transparency, thereby teaching the 80% that not doing it is normal. Its mirror: running a "why this matters" campaign (injunctive only) while the visible behaviour of prestigious members contradicts it; the empirical channel wins that contest.

## t11. Mis-specified preferences vs unstable beliefs

**Pole A: behavioural anomalies (loss aversion, hyperbolic discounting, social preferences) are state-dependent consistent preferences; write down beliefs, preferences and constraints and the anomalies disappear into the model.** Gintis (`11-bounds-of-reason`) keeps the rational actor and widens the utility function: the BPC model. Its design payoff, tagged [E] in the 11 file, is a discipline: for any failure, ask what members believe about each other, what they value (status and fairness included) and what constrains them; most "incentive" fixes change constraints while beliefs about what others will do stay the same. Camerer's parametric programme (`06`) is the empirical cousin.

**Pole B: many anomalies are shifts in belief with presentation, mood and motive, not preferences of any shape; and learning is path-dependent rather than a fixed point.** Gilovich et al. (`03`) document construal, framing, naive realism and motivated reasoning: the same payoff matrix produces roughly double the cooperation under a "Community Game" label (Liberman, Samuels and Ross 2004, background, listed as robust in `03`); people see their own construal as reality. Camerer's learning chapter shows convergence is path-dependent; the qualitative points (feedback and early rounds matter) are robust while EWA's parameters are not (ledger C64).

**What the evidence says.** The 11 file's own verdict on the reframing is "partially holds up; overstated", and the ledger follows it (X16, ledger under C55-C58 and X16): use BPC as bookkeeping, do not claim it explains construal effects, and note the falsifiability worry (once preferences may be state-dependent and norm-inclusive, what observation refutes the model?). The poles agree that rationality is not selfishness and that the anomalies are real; the disagreement is whether calling them preferences adds anything. The design stakes are real: if a behaviour is a preference, you change payoffs; if it is a belief, you change information, framing and the first rounds. The replication record is asymmetric here. The construal effect on PD cooperation is on `03`'s robust list, and Nagel's beauty-contest first-round average of about 35-36 (verified) with convergence toward zero under repetition is the cleanest demonstration that first-contact behaviour is belief-driven and learnable. But `03`'s broader belief-manipulation literature includes failures the course must not build on (behavioural priming, ego depletion at d = 0.04, power posing; ledger #20), so "it's all framing" is not a licence either. Gintis wrote no experiments for this claim; the framing evidence is Ross's and Liberman's; the learning evidence is Camerer's. [H for the BPC reframing as explanation; E for framing and first-round belief effects; E for BPC as a bookkeeping discipline.]

**Rule.** IF a behaviour persists across framings, labels and experience THEN treat it as a preference and change the payoffs or constraints. IF it moves with the label, the order of information or the first few rounds THEN treat it as a belief: relabel the game, seed the first rounds deliberately (first splits and first review interactions set the attractor, `08`, [H]), show forgone payoffs, and expect convergence with feedback. IF you do not know THEN write the BPC table (beliefs, preferences, constraints) for the members whose behaviour puzzles you and check which column your intended fix touches; the usual error is changing a constraint when the problem is a belief about others. Never build a belief intervention on priming, depletion or posture (ledger #20).

**Switch signal.** Toward preferences: relabelling and re-sequencing have been tried and behaviour is unchanged; experienced members behave the same as novices. Toward beliefs: behaviour differs between two teams facing identical incentives, or shifts when a respected member's public stance shifts, or converges over repeated rounds toward what the payoff model predicts. The observation: run the same decision under two labels or two orderings and compare.

**Common mistake.** Absorbing every anomaly into a richer utility function and then declaring the design "incentive-compatible" when it has merely renamed the problem (the falsifiability worry made practical). Its mirror: treating members as pure construal machines and reframing endlessly while the payoff structure that actually punishes contribution stays untouched.

## t12. Explicit incentives and metrics vs norm-based motivation

**Pole A: make contribution a best response with explicit incentives and measurable accountability; what is rewarded and measured gets done.** Dixit et al. (`04`) give the mechanism-design tools: prices, penalties, monitoring at a probability roughly equal to gain divided by penalty, screening. Easley and Kleinberg (`10`) endorse institutional edits over exhortation. Ostrom (`09`) makes monitoring a design principle: it is a by-product of participation in every durable case, and sanctions are graduated but real. Camerer (`06`) shows contributions decay without punishment and recover with it.

**Pole B: norms exploit an existing disposition, are cheaper to maintain, and explicit payment and scorekeeping can crowd them out.** Gintis (`11`) tags "prefer the norm to the incentive when both are available" as [H], noting crowding out is real but context-dependent. Gilovich et al. (`03`) supply the psychology: overjustification, and the warning tagged [E] for the mechanism that introducing scorekeeping into a communal team damages it; the design line is "use small, voluntary, public commitments and modest rewards to produce internalized norms; avoid paying heavily for behaviour you want members to own". Ostrom's field evidence (Cardenas, background) is that weakly enforced external regulation crowds out communication-driven cooperation. Camerer's field gift-exchange result cuts the same way for positive incentives: above-market pay boosted effort for the first few hours only and cost more than it returned (Gneezy and List 2006, verified), while cuts and non-monetary gestures have larger and more persistent effects (Kube et al. 2012, background; ledger #28). Dixit et al.'s own caveat: partial-metric incentives displace unmeasured effort; with noisy output, lean on norms and reputation.

**What the evidence says.** This is the measurement and accountability tension, and the files agree more than the poles suggest. Everyone accepts that some observation is necessary (t02: sanctions need monitoring; t05: dictator giving needs identifiability; Ostrom: monitoring is principle 4). Everyone accepts that money is a weak and decaying lever for positive reciprocity and a strong one for negative. The narrow disagreement is about form: whether accountability should be an explicit metric with attached reward, or a by-product visibility (public logs, turn-taking that exposes cheaters, visible queues) that keeps empirical expectations accurate without turning a communal relationship into an exchange one. The ledger's resolutions all lean to the by-product form (ledger #28, #30) while keeping the credible-sanction requirement from `04`. Caveats: the crowding-out literature is context-dependent and the 11 file tags the preference for norms [H]; the `03` scorekeeping warning is [E] for the mechanism and [H] for any group; the measurement-displacement claim in `04` is theory plus [H] for the setting. The studies: Gneezy and List's field experiments; Kube's; Cardenas's field games in Colombia; the overjustification paradigms in `03`; the Cox review for monitoring as by-product. [E for monitoring and cheap sanctions being necessary; E for pay as a weak positive lever; H for crowding out in your group; V for whether members should be treated as exchange partners or as a community, which is a choice about the kind of group you want.]

**Rule.** IF the behaviour is a genuine PD contribution that people will not make on trust (t07) THEN attach real accountability, but make the monitoring a by-product of the work (visible commits, public rotas, shared logs) and the first sanction cheap and graduated; reserve explicit financial incentives for measurable outputs with low noise. IF the behaviour is one you want members to own (review quality, mentoring, speaking up) THEN use small public commitments, modest recognition and norms, avoid heavy pay for it, and never introduce individual scorekeeping into a communal team without expecting damage; if a metric is needed, make it collective. IF you must use a metric THEN ask what unmeasured effort it will displace and monitor that too, and treat any wage or reward cut, or any broken promise, as a strong negative-reciprocity event to be handled explicitly. Never buy contribution with a raise and expect it to last (ledger #28), and never leave a PD to run on norms alone without any observation.

**Switch signal.** Toward explicit incentives: contribution is decaying despite talk and visibility, the free riders are stable and identifiable, and the output is measurable with low noise. Toward norms: after a metric or reward was introduced, the measured behaviour rose and an unmeasured one fell, or members began asking "what do I get" for things they used to do; or the effort boost from a reward faded within days. The counts: the measured behaviour, at least one unmeasured behaviour that shares the same effort budget, and the trend in each after the change.

**Common mistake.** The product default: instrument everything, attach a leaderboard, and turn a community into a market whose members now expect to be paid for what they used to give. Its mirror: refusing all measurement in the name of trust, so that free-riding is invisible, empirical expectations drift, and the conditional cooperators (t05) stop.

## t13. Small bounded groups vs open scale

**Pole A: cooperation is a small-group phenomenon; the evidence lives in bounded, dense, stable groups, and scale should be handled by nesting rather than by growing.** Ostrom (`09`): clear boundaries are principle 1 (ambiguous membership is the commonest structural cause of decay, [E]); nested enterprises are principle 8; her cases are small, place-based and bounded, and outsiders are excluded, which is one reason several durable systems are inegalitarian. Camerer (`06`): weak-link groups of 14-16 collapsed to minimum effort while fixed pairs reached the top (Van Huyck et al. 1990), and the design advice is to cap the group whose minimum matters at a handful. Gilovich et al. (`03`): working units of 2-5 to limit loafing (a rule of thumb, not a measured optimum, [H]). Skyrms (`07`): small stable neighbourhoods are the correlation device. Dixit et al. (`04`): with more than two players, who punishes whom is hard.

**Pole B: larger, connected populations retain more knowledge and innovate more; open membership is where growth, information and legitimacy come from, and gradual growth can carry coordination to scale.** Henrich and Laland (`01`, `02`): cumulative culture depends on the size and connectivity of the learning network (contested as head-count, ledger #6). Camerer: large groups grown from a small core with visible history sustain high effort (Weber 2006, verified, single-lab lineage). Easley and Kleinberg (`10`): short paths exist in any large network; network effects mean a practice is worth more the more people use it, and adoption above the tipping point self-sustains. Ostrom's own scope note: the book concerns subtractable resources with costly exclusion; knowledge commons and open source found partial fit with principles 1 and 2 reinterpreted (ledger C48), which is exactly the learner's platform case.

**What the evidence says.** The agreement: every mechanism in this course (observation, reputation, sanctions, complex contagion, assurance) gets weaker as the group grows and interaction becomes anonymous, and every knowledge-and-innovation benefit gets stronger. Nobody in the files claims Ostrom's principles are proven at scale; the 09 file lists scale as where they are least tested and the ledger lists transfer to large, digital and knowledge commons as an open question. Nobody claims small groups innovate. The narrow disagreements are two. First, whether scale is reached by nesting (Ostrom: subgroups handle local problems, a higher layer handles inter-subgroup conflict) or by growth from a core (Weber), which the files treat as compatible sequencing rather than rivals. Second, the boundary itself, and this is where the learner's values enter most directly: a boundary that makes monitoring and sanctions work also excludes; durability and fairness are different criteria (`09` misreadings); Ostrom's durable cases include hereditary shares and exclusion of outsiders; open platforms trade enforcement for reach. The course cannot settle how much exclusion a learner should accept for how much cooperation; it can only insist the trade be named and tagged [V]. Studies: Van Huyck's and Weber's lab lineages; the Cox review; Kline and Boyd's Oceania correlation (clean but single-region) for the connectivity side. [E for the small-group results and the boundary principle; H for nesting and gradual growth beyond one lab; V for who is in and who is out.]

**Rule.** IF a behaviour depends on the minimum contributor, on mutual observation or on sanctions THEN keep the unit small (a handful; 2-5 is `03`'s rule of thumb, [H]), bounded and stable, and scale by nesting: local units govern local problems, a higher layer handles conflict between units and shared infrastructure. IF the goal is knowledge, innovation or a practice with network effects THEN grow, but grow from a small core with a visible success record rather than launching at full size, and estimate whether adoption is above the tipping point before you open the doors. IF you are applying Ostrom to a digital or knowledge commons THEN flag the scope: reinterpret boundaries as membership and contribution roles, not resource limits, and expect principles 1 and 2 to fit only partly. Never scale a weak-link process by adding people to the unit whose minimum matters, and never define a boundary without writing down, as a value, whom it excludes and why that is acceptable.

**Switch signal.** Toward small and bounded: minimum-effort outcomes are appearing as the unit grows past a dozen; violations are going unsanctioned because nobody has repeated dealings with the violator; membership is ambiguous and decay is tracking that ambiguity. Toward scale: rare skills sit with single people, the same problems are solved repeatedly in isolation, or a network-effect practice is stuck below its tipping point because the unit is too small to reach it. The counts: unit size against the minimum-effort trend; fraction of violations sanctioned; number of holders per critical skill.

**Common mistake.** Treating the platform or the whole organization as "the group" and expecting community-level cooperation from thousands of anonymous members. Its mirror: keeping the cooperative unit small and closed until it is a guild, durable and unfair, with the learner never having decided that exclusion was the price they meant to pay.

## t14. Individual dispositions vs structure

**Pole A: outcomes are explained by what individuals are like; change attitudes, values and the people, and the group changes.** This is the dispositional default that `03-social-psychology` documents as the fundamental attribution error, robust across decades and paradigms, and that every manager's first diagnosis ("we have free riders", "they don't care about quality") expresses. The steelman is not a book file's thesis but a residue all the files keep: attitudes set thresholds and tolerance schedules (Card, Mas and Rothstein 2008, verified, found tipping points higher where measured tolerance is higher), type heterogeneity is a real finding (t05), and the norm-change literature does rely on particular people: Bicchieri's trendsetters (low sensitivity to the bad norm, high credibility in the reference network, [H], case studies), Henrich's prestigious models, `03`'s credible dissenter.

**Pole B: aggregate outcomes are properties of structure (interdependence, thresholds, accounting constraints, equilibria), and mild individual motives can produce extreme collective results; change the structure.** Schelling (`05-micromotives-and-macrobehavior`) shows that mild preferences plus interdependence can produce complete sorting, that a tipping point is an unstable equilibrium in a threshold distribution rather than a property of charismatic people (ledger #18), and that homogeneity is weak evidence of intolerance. Gilovich et al. prescribe diagnosing situationally before dispositionally: map workload, information, incentives and ambiguity before concluding "free riders" ([E]). Easley and Kleinberg (`10`) reduce norm change to a seed set, a threshold q and cluster density. Bicchieri's pluralistic ignorance is a structure of beliefs, not a set of bad people.

**What the evidence says.** The files agree that dispositions are underestimated less often than situations are (`03` misreadings: effect sizes are moderate; the claim is that situations are underestimated, not that people are infinitely malleable, ledger C23). They agree that Schelling's model is a possibility proof: it shows what mild preferences can do, not that preferences are the main real-world cause of segregation or that the outcome is acceptable (ledger #17; Massey and Denton on institutional drivers); its fragility to preference shape is disputed (Bruch and Mare 2006 versus Van de Rijt et al. 2009); empirical tipping is roughly 5-20% minority share (verified). The narrow disagreement is about where to intervene for norm change, and the two candidate routes look like the two poles but are both structural once read properly: trendsetters are chosen by their position in the threshold distribution and the reference network (low threshold, high credibility), not by charisma (ledger #18, C30); and shifting expectations broadly (publishing a measured gap, t10) is the other route, for when the norm is held up by pluralistic ignorance rather than by a missing seed. Schelling's "structure explains most group failures" is itself a hypothesis in any given group (`05` evidence section). Studies: Schelling's demonstrations and the Card-Mas-Rothstein tract data; Granovetter's threshold model; Bicchieri's sanitation and child-marriage case studies; the attribution literature in `03`. [E for the mechanisms and for the attribution error; H for structure explaining your case and for trendsetter interventions; V for whether the equilibrium you find is one you accept.]

**Rule.** IF a group outcome looks like a character problem THEN model the interaction first: is behaviour contingent on others' behaviour, does an accounting constraint force the outcome, which equilibrium is the group in, and what is the threshold distribution; only if the structural model fails to reproduce the outcome, treat it as dispositional. IF the norm you want to change is disliked privately but believed to be endorsed (pluralistic ignorance) THEN shift expectations broadly: measure and publish the gap through a credible source (t10). IF the norm is genuinely held by most and you need it to fall THEN find low-threshold, high-credibility members and let them move first in a cluster where their move is visible to each other, then grow (t04, t13); do not pick them for charisma. IF the problem is mixing or sorting THEN act on thresholds and neighbourhood definitions (cohorts not tokens, visible allies) rather than exhortation, knowing that attitude work moves the tipping point but does not remove the dynamic. Never write "the situation determines behaviour" (ledger C23) and never use the Gladwell sense of "influencer".

**Switch signal.** Toward structure: the same "bad" behaviour appears in whoever holds the role; people who are cooperative elsewhere defect here; the outcome reproduces after the people change. Toward dispositions: the structural model has been changed (thresholds lowered, expectations published, seeds placed) and the outcome persists with the same individuals, or moves when those individuals do; a small identifiable set behaves the same regardless of others. The observation: swap the people, keep the structure, and see whether the pattern survives; then the reverse.

**Common mistake.** The dispositional diagnosis and its remedies (hiring for culture fit, values workshops, removing the "toxic" person), after which the structure produces a new toxic person. Its mirror: concluding that because outcomes are structural, individual attitudes do not matter, and skipping the trendsetter and dissenter work that Card-Mas-Rothstein's tolerance result and Bicchieri's case studies both say moves the threshold.

## How to use this file

Before a contested decision in your cooperation brief: find the tension that names it, state which pole you are leaning to and why, apply the rule's IF branch that fits your group, and write the switch signal into the Cooperation brief next to the decision as its [H] test, so that a future observation can tell you that you chose wrong. A decision that fits no tension is either a pure [V] (say so, and name who in the reading list would dissent) or a pure [E] (cite the mechanism and its slug); if it is neither, it is not yet a decision, it is a preference. When two tensions apply, they usually nest: t07 (which game is it) comes before t04 (which equilibrium will it reach) which comes before t09 and t13 (what structure holds it) which come before t12 (what accountability it needs); t05, t10 and t14 are diagnostic tools you run at any point; t01, t02, t03, t06, t08 and t11 are about which model of the people you are using. The critic's tensions (t02, t03, t11) are there to keep the whole brief honest about what the evidence does not show.
