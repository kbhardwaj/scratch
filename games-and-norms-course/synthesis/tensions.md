# The tensions: where the authors disagree, what the evidence says, and how to decide

Eleven books and a running argument. Henrich and Laland disagree about what drives cultural evolution; Skyrms and Camerer say the safe equilibrium wins while Ostrom's irrigators reach the good one; Camerer writes fairness into the utility function and Bicchieri says it is not there; Gintis wants evolved strong reciprocity and Binmore says the lab is fooling us. None of these debates is settled by a winner. Each is settled by conditions: for whom, for what goal, at what point in the group's history, at what cost, for how long. This file turns every disagreement the claims ledger recorded (section 1, X1 to X16, plus the disagreements the book files name under "Connections") into a decision rule with a switch signal, so that a decision in your Cooperation brief is never "Skyrms says" but "under these conditions, this pole; and here is the observation that would tell me I chose wrong".

Both poles are steelmanned from the book files (`books/`), which win over this file wherever they differ. Tags per SPEC: [E] supported by evidence, [H] hypothesis to test in your group, [V] value commitment. Every number is from ledger section 4 with its status there ("verified" or "background"); anything the ledger does not quantify is described as small, moderate or roughly doubled. Where a tension is mostly false once a distinction is drawn, the file says so; it is kept because the mistake it names is common.

**The list (this order is canonical; ids never change):**

## t01. Copying vs teaching

**Pole A: people adopt behaviour by copying models, not by understanding arguments; route norms through prestige, conformity and credibility-enhancing displays.** Henrich (`01-secret-of-our-success`): humans carry evolved learning biases, prestige (Cheng et al. 2013, robust), conformist transmission, self-similarity and sensitivity to costly displays of belief. Over-imitation, replicated in many labs though mostly with one puzzle box, shows people copy opaque practice they cannot justify, adaptively. The file's design line: treat "we explained it" as zero adoption.

**Pole B: copying is one general-purpose strategy among several, and for anything with hidden causal structure explicit teaching beats exposure.** Laland (`02-darwins-unfinished-symphony`): copying rules (copy when uncertain, copy the successful, copy the majority, discount stale information) are ecologically shaped strategies found even in sticklebacks; humans differ in fidelity, teaching and language. The social-learning tournament (Rendell et al. 2010, verified; 104 entries; the winner copied almost exclusively and discounted old information) shows copying pays because demonstrators pre-filter, but it was a simulation and pure-copier populations stagnate. The stone-tool experiment (Morgan et al. 2015, verified; N = 184) found imitation added little over reverse engineering while teaching and language produced the largest gains.

**What the evidence says.** The two agree on far more than they dispute: cumulative culture is the human distinction, copying is selective, demonstrators filter, cooperation itself spreads as a copied behaviour. The ledger resolves X1 as mostly compatible: Henrich's biases are the levers, Laland's tournament is the reason copying pays (ledger #8, #39). The narrow disagreement, X13, is about what is transmitted, and once that is read properly this is mostly a false tension. Norm adherence spreads through visible models, prestige and expectations; explanation is a weak channel for it, and Henrich is right. Skill with hidden causal structure needs explicit teaching, and Laland is right, on one modest study of modern adults plus his lab's fidelity models. Neither ran a horse race against the other; over-imitation's developmental universality is unclear (Aka children emulated, Berl and Hewlett 2015); and "copying wins" must be paired with "pay someone to innovate". [E for both mechanisms in their scope; H for the weight of any bias in your group; V for preferring prestige to dominance as the channel.]

**Rule.** IF the target is a norm (its value depends on others doing it, or its payoff is diffuse) THEN put a prestigious, self-similar model visibly doing it and succeeding, make leaders' costly displays point the same way, and do not count a memo or training as adoption. IF the target is a skill with non-obvious causal structure THEN budget for explicit teaching of reasons and put expiry dates on the playbooks. IF the group is young with little accumulated practice THEN documentation is overhead; make the best behaviour visible first. Never collapse the two into "explanation does not work" or "just document it" (ledger #8).

**Switch signal.** Toward teaching: people reproduce the surface of a practice while the outcome it exists for does not move, or it breaks when conditions change. Toward modelling: the practice is documented, everyone can state the reason, and adoption is still confined to whoever wrote the document. Count separately how many can explain the practice and how many do it.

**Common mistake.** Treating adoption as a knowledge gap and running another explanation session. Its mirror: assuming a copied practice carries its own reasons, so when the model leaves the group keeps the ritual and loses the point.

## t02. Strong reciprocity as a trait vs conditional behaviour

**Pole A: humans have an evolved disposition to cooperate conditionally and punish defectors at personal cost, even one-shot; design can rely on it.** Gintis (`11-bounds-of-reason`, with Bowles, Boyd and Fehr): strong reciprocity evolved by gene-culture coevolution and group selection; norms are internalized. Henrich (`01`) supplies the norm psychology and costly third-party punishment. Camerer (`06-behavioral-game-theory`) supplies the lab: rejection of low ultimatum offers is among the best-replicated results in the field (mean offer about 40%, mode 40-50%, Oosterbeek et al. 2004, verified; about half of offers below 20% rejected, background), and people punish intended unfairness far more than unlucky outcomes (Blount 1995).

**Pole B: one-shot lab punishment reflects heuristics tuned for repeated play, and real-world sanctioning is mostly cheap; design on the cheap, graduated version.** The critic's position, from Gintis's own evidence section. Binmore and Shaked (2010, background): the lab data fit misapplied repeated-play rules and learning erodes cooperation. Guala (2012, background): field evidence for costly punishment in small-scale societies is thin; sanctioning in the wild is gossip and ostracism. Camerer adds that rejections fall substantially at high stakes (Andersen et al. 2011, background) and positive reciprocity to a gift decayed within hours in the field (Gneezy and List 2006, verified) while negative reciprocity is strong. Ostrom (`09-governing-the-commons`): durable commons use graduated sanctions, small and quick and public first, applied by participants; well supported in the 91-study review (Cox et al. 2010, verified).

**What the evidence says.** Everyone agrees on the behaviour: people pay something to punish unfair intentions, public-goods contributions decay over rounds and recover with punishment, and pure self-interest is rejected as securely as anything in behavioural science. The narrow disagreement is origin and magnitude: an unconditional evolved trait, or a conditional disposition that shrinks with stakes, experience and anonymity? The ledger splits the claim (X4, ledger #4, #30): willingness to pay to punish unfair intentions in the lab is [E]; evolved strong reciprocity is [H] (the 11 file: contested, a weaker version is supported); real-world sanctioning is mostly cheap and graduated, [E]. The trait side's studies were run by the strong-reciprocity school itself (Fehr, Gächter, Henrich, Gintis); the field evidence is Ostrom's and the Cox review. Even robust lab effects are smaller than first reported: 61% of 18 top-journal experiments replicated at about two-thirds of original size (Camerer et al. 2016, verified). This is also the punishment-versus-reputation choice: the trait reading makes punishment the engine; the conditional reading makes observation, reputation and cheap disapproval the engine with formal penalty as backstop. [E for lab punishment and cheap field sanctions; H for the evolved trait and how much costly punishment your members will do; V for constraining peer punishment with a legitimate process.]

**Rule.** IF you are budgeting enforcement THEN assume members sanction only when the channel is cheap, fast and proportionate (a quiet word, a public log, a known escalation ladder), and make monitoring a by-product of participation rather than a police function. IF the violation is an unfair intention rather than an unlucky outcome THEN expect costly retaliation and pre-empt it with procedurally impartial allocation (rules, rotation, lottery). IF stakes are high or the group is experienced THEN discount the lab punishment rate further and rely on reputation and repetition. Never build a design that only works if members reliably punish at personal cost without institutional support, and never let peer punishment run without a legitimate process, because antisocial punishment is real.

**Switch signal.** Toward the trait pole: members pay observable costs to sanction with no expectation of future dealings, repeatedly, and the behaviour falls. Toward the conditional pole: violations accumulate with nobody acting, punishment goes underground into feuds, or sanctioning collapses when stakes rise or anonymity increases. Count violations observed against violations sanctioned, and by whom.

**Common mistake.** Designing for heroes: assuming the group will self-police at cost, then finding everyone waited for someone else (the bystander structure, [E] in low-risk ambiguous settings, `03`). Its mirror: concluding that because punishment is not a reflex it does not matter, removing all sanctions, and watching contributions decay as Camerer's curves predict.

## t03. Rational equilibrium vs norm-choreographed order

**Pole A: a group settles where no one gains by deviating alone; model members as rational players, find the Nash equilibria, patch selection with focal points.** Dixit, Skeath and McAdams (`04-games-of-strategy`): write the matrix, find the equilibria, use rollback and the folk theorem; where equilibria are multiple, add focal points or dynamics, a seam the book admits. Rationality is consistent pursuit of whatever the payoffs contain, fairness included (ledger #16). Skyrms (`07-stag-hunt`) gives the dynamic version: basins and correlation select the pattern, with no beliefs at all.

**Pole B: Nash cannot be derived from rationality alone; groups reach order because a social signal aligns conjectures, and the signal must live inside the agents as expectations.** Gintis (`11`) proves, via Aumann and Brandenburger, that common knowledge of rationality yields correlated equilibrium, not Nash; the correlating device is the norm, the choreographer. Bicchieri (`08-grammar-of-society`) specifies what must be true inside agents: a conditional preference keyed to empirical expectations (what others do) and normative expectations (what others think one should do). Camerer (`06`): first-contact play is norm-driven with one to two steps of reasoning (beauty-contest first-round average about 35-36, Nagel 1995, verified; mean thinking steps about 1.5, Camerer, Ho and Chong 2004, verified).

**What the evidence says.** The course's central reconciliation, binding in the ledger (X5, #1): four layers, not four rivals. Nash or ESS is the stability test for any candidate pattern; Skyrms and Camerer predict which stable pattern is reached absent intervention (t04); Gintis names what a norm does (a public signal aligning conjectures, the theorem-backed part of his book); Bicchieri specifies and measures the expectations that make the signal bite. The real disagreement is narrower: whether an explicit choreographer is needed or history plus a focal point suffices (Schelling, `05`: focal points are choreographers without an author), and whether order arrives by learning (Camerer) or by norms (Gintis). The 11 file concedes the choreographer is weaker than Bicchieri on diagnosis, Ostrom on authorship and Skyrms on origins; Vanderschraaf's review notes the selection gap was moved up a level, not closed. The formal results are theorems; the claim that correlated equilibrium fits data better than Nash is not made by the book and must not be made here (ledger #31). Nash is a stability test, not a prediction or an endorsement (ledger #14). [E for the formal relations and norm-driven first-contact play; H for which layer bites in your group; V for preferring a designed choreographer to an emergent one.]

**Rule.** IF you are checking whether a proposed pattern can last THEN apply the stability test (does any member gain by deviating alone?) and treat failure as fatal however good the pattern is. IF the pattern passes but the group is not there THEN stop reasoning people toward it and name the signal: a public cue (rota, written rule, default, dashboard) that tells each person which equilibrium is in play; ambiguity, not incentives, is the usual failure. IF the signal exists and compliance is still low THEN measure the expectations; a choreographer nobody believes is a poster. Never present one layer as "the" theory of norms, and never contrast "rational" with "fair".

**Switch signal.** Toward the rational pole: behaviour is stable across labels and framings and responds only to payoff changes; these are experienced players in a familiar game. Toward the norm pole: the same matrix produces different behaviour under different labels (a "Community Game" label roughly doubled prisoner's-dilemma cooperation against "Wall Street Game", Liberman, Samuels and Ross 2004, background) or under different observation; or a meeting derived the "obviously rational" plan and behaviour did not move.

**Common mistake.** Reading Nash as a forecast and building an incentive scheme that assumes the group will find and hold the computed equilibrium. Its mirror: taking Gintis to have refuted rational choice, dropping the stability test, and installing a norm everyone gains by defecting from.

## t04. Risk dominance vs payoff dominance

**Pole A: under uncertainty the safe equilibrium wins; expect the low-effort, low-trust outcome as the structural default.** Skyrms (`07`): under replicator dynamics with random mixing the risk-dominant equilibrium wins because its basin is larger (the file's illustration: p* = 3/4 stag hunters needed with payoffs Stag/Stag 4, Stag/Hare 0, Hare 3). Under best-response updating local interaction accelerates risk dominance (Ellison 1993); with persistent noise it is stochastically stable (Kandori, Mailath and Rob 1993; Young 1993). Camerer (`06`): without communication play converges to the risk-dominant or minimum-effort outcome (Cooper et al. 1990/92); weak-link groups of 14-16 fell to effort 1 within about ten rounds while fixed pairs reached 7 (Van Huyck, Battalio and Beil 1990; group-size result verified, round counts background).

**Pole B: the payoff-dominant outcome is reachable, because correlation (structure, talk, history, partner choice) expands the good basin.** Skyrms Parts I-III: local interaction with imitate-the-best, cheap-talk handshakes and partner choice let stag hunting spread from small clusters (fairness contagion in tens of generations under local imitation against hundreds under random mixing, Alexander and Skyrms 1999, background). Camerer's post-2003 evidence: two-way talk, a leader naming the target, gradual growth from a small core with visible history (Weber 2006, verified, single-lab lineage) and a lower payoff to the safe action select the efficient equilibrium. Easley and Kleinberg (`10-networks-crowds-markets`): adoption spreads when a node's adopting-neighbour fraction exceeds q = b/(a+b) (theorem), so lowering q or seeding inside a cluster spreads costly behaviour.

**What the evidence says.** The poles agree on the game: in a stag hunt payoffs already favour cooperation, so failure is strategic uncertainty plus history (ledger C37), and the default absent intervention is risk dominance. The real disagreement is whether the payoff-dominant outcome is a stable destination or a transient that noise erodes, and it hinges on the learning rule: Skyrms's optimism rests on imitation dynamics and transient analysis; the pessimistic theorems assume best response with noise (open, ledger section 6). The binding wording (ledger #2): risk dominance wins under random mixing, best-response updating, large groups and no communication; payoff dominance is reachable through correlation (small stable groups, success-visible imitation, public pledges, gradual growth, lower cost of being the lone cooperator). Sweetening the joint payoff is the weakest lever; lowering the lone-cooperator loss moves the watershed most (Stag/Hare from 0 to 2 moves p* from 3/4 to 1/2 in the illustration). Clustering is the same conditional in network form (X7, ledger #3): local structure helps only under success-copying, and a cluster of density above 1 − q blocks a norm entering from outside while protecting one adopted inside. Lab work is Cooper's, Van Huyck's and Weber's; models are Skyrms's, Ellison's, Young's and Kleinberg's. [E for models and lab; H for which regime your group is in and whether the good outcome is stable long-run.]

**Rule.** IF matching is near-random, the group is large, people best-respond to what they last saw and there is no channel for talk or history THEN plan for risk dominance: cap the group whose minimum matters at a handful, or change the aggregation rule from minimum to median or average. IF you can create correlation THEN do the cheap things first, in order: lower the cost of being the lone cooperator, have a leader publicly name the target, take public opt-in pledges, grow from a small core with a visible success record, keep neighbourhoods small and stable while the norm is young. IF you rely on clusters THEN check the learning rule and make cooperators' success visible; if people copy the majority instead, clusters entrench the safe outcome. Never write "the efficient equilibrium is the rational one" or treat its absence as irrationality (ledger #2).

**Switch signal.** Toward risk dominance (stop pushing, restructure): effort falls round on round toward the minimum in a group above roughly a dozen; those who tried first are visibly worse off than those who waited. Toward payoff dominance (the levers are working): a small core's success is visible and the adopting fraction among members' neighbours is rising past the point where switching pays. Watch the lone-cooperator payoff directly.

**Common mistake.** Raising the reward for joint success when the problem is the cost of being the one who trusted. Its mirror: assuming a small talking group has secured the good outcome for good, then rotating people randomly across teams, which hands the basin back to risk dominance.

## t05. Stable social preferences vs cued norm compliance

**Pole A: fairness and reciprocity are stable preferences; put them in the payoffs and model members as types.** Camerer (`06`) models social preferences as utility parameters (Fehr-Schmidt, Charness-Rabin, intention-based models); Dixit et al. (`04`) and Gintis (`11`, BPC) say "put fairness in the payoffs". The type summary (roughly 30% self-interested, about 50% conditional cooperators, a small tail of altruists, background; Bruhin, Fehr and Schunk 2019 put about 40% strongly other-regarding, background) is a design tool: let conditional cooperators see others cooperating. Henrich and Gintis add that norms are genuinely internalized.

**Pole B: fair behaviour is norm compliance activated by cues and expectations, not a taste of fixed size; every norm is conditional until proved otherwise.** Bicchieri (`08`): fixed preferences cannot explain how fair behaviour varies with anonymity, entitlement, moral wiggle room (Dana, Weber and Kuang 2007) and information. Dictator giving (mean about 28%, about 64% give something, Engel 2011, verified) collapses under double-blind anonymity, falls with earned endowments and shifts when a take option is added (List 2007; Bardsley 2008): the signature of a cued norm. Bicchieri and Xiao (2009, verified) found expectations of selfishness reduced giving even under pro-giving messages. Henrich's cross-cultural data (mean ultimatum offers about 26% to 58% across 15 societies, verified) show the disposition to have norms is universal but the content is not.

**What the evidence says.** Broad agreement: people are neither selfish nor saintly, behaviour is heterogeneous, reciprocity and intention-sensitivity dominate over unconditional altruism (ledger C33). Two narrow disagreements. X8: are the parameters stable? Camerer's own verdict is that no social-preference model wins and parameters are unstable across games, which nearly concedes Bicchieri's point; the ledger rules (#9) that fairness is [E] as cue- and expectation-dependent behaviour with a distribution of sensitivities, and "a taste for fairness of fixed size" is not [E]. X9: is anything internalized? Bicchieri confines unconditional compliance to the moral-norm bin; Henrich and Gintis think the bin is large. The operational test all files accept (ledger #10): assume a norm is conditional until it survives a collapse of expectations. Caveats: Bicchieri's superiority over inequity aversion is argued from fit, not shown, and critics say a substantive fairness concept sneaks back in to say which split people expect (ledger #38); her taxonomy is cleaner than her own field data. Camerer's corpus is student-lab and one-shot; Bicchieri's later field work is in sanitation programmes. [E for cue-dependence and type heterogeneity as qualitative facts; H for any parametric model and the internalized share; V for process fairness as a good in itself.]

**Rule.** IF you are choosing a fairness baseline THEN elicit it from the group (what do most people here do; what do most think you should do), never a lab or 50-50 default (ledger #25). IF you want contribution to persist THEN design the cues and the observation: identifiability, visible consequences for others, no exits that let people dodge unseen. IF you want to know whether a norm can be leaned on THEN run the collapse test: tell a subgroup, truthfully, that compliance elsewhere has fallen, and watch whether they hold; design for the conditional case because it is cheaper to lose. Never state a member-type share as a fact about your group, and never run values workshops for a norm you have not first diagnosed as moral rather than social (ledger #10).

**Switch signal.** Toward preferences: behaviour holds across anonymity, framing and reported changes in others' behaviour; the same people are generous in every context. Toward norms: contribution tracks what people believe others do more closely than what they say they value; anonymity or a new exit changes it; the elicitation shows an attitude-expectation gap.

**Common mistake.** Attributing free-riding to selfish types and screening for character when the same people would contribute under observation. Its mirror: treating everyone as cue-driven and forgetting the roughly 30% who in every lab do not cooperate whatever others do; they are why t02's sanctions exist.

## t06. Designed rules vs self-governed rules

**Pole A: institutions are designed; a competent designer with the right toolkit writes rules that make contribution a best response.** Dixit et al. (`04`): choose decision rules on purpose, make sanctions subgame-perfect, randomize monitoring at a probability roughly gain divided by penalty, sort types with costly signals. Easley and Kleinberg (`10`) endorse institutional edits over exhortation. The strong form is external authorship: a central authority or platform owner writes and enforces the rules, the prescription Ostrom's book was written against.

**Pole B: durable rules are crafted incrementally by those bound by them, and mostly copied from neighbours rather than invented.** Ostrom (`09`): appropriators in Törbel (the 1517 cap: no more cows on the alp than one can winter), the huertas, the zanjeras and the California basins crafted, monitored and revised their rules; change was incremental, with courts and enabling law as backstop, not author. Principle 3 (those affected can change the rules) is the core; the Cox review (91 studies, verified) supports the principles, with the caveat that cases were selected on the dependent variable. Henrich (`01`) goes further: institutions were mostly copied from successful neighbours and modified under group competition, with designers rationalizing afterwards; Cleaver's institutional bricolage (in `09`'s critiques) says rules are assembled from existing practice.

**What the evidence says.** The ledger resolves X10 as conditional and mostly compatible: Ostrom's cases show incremental crafting rather than a constitutional act, and Henrich's copy-and-modify includes the modification. Copy the norm package of a visibly successful peer group, then craft with the people bound by the rules; neither "design from scratch" nor "just copy" is [E]. The real disagreement is authorship and legitimacy: Ostrom's principle 7 (external recognition of the right to organize) and her field evidence that weakly enforced external regulation crowds out communication-driven cooperation (Cardenas, background) say imposed rules are followed only while enforced; the mechanism-design toolkit is silent on who may change the rules, and Dixit's file concedes that with many players "who punishes whom" is hard and Ostrom's monitoring principles are the empirical answer. The 09 critiques matter: the principles describe structure, not the processes (trust, legitimacy) that make it work; Agrawal counted around 30 or more success conditions across the literature; Ostrom warned against panaceas; durable cases lean on courts and several are inegalitarian. This is one of two tensions where the learner's values enter explicitly: "those bound by a rule may change it" is [E] for durability and [V] for polycentric arrangements; a designer who prefers control is making a value choice. [E for participant authorship and graduated sanctions in durable institutions; H for transfer to teams and digital commons (ledger #29); V for who holds the pen.]

**Rule.** IF the group already communicates, observes itself and can change its rules THEN fix those channels first, then let members craft operational rules against the eight principles used as questions, with external authority as recognition, backstop and data. IF the group is new, large, or the problem is one members cannot see (an accounting constraint, a sorting externality) THEN design the structure (boundaries, by-product monitoring, a graduated ladder, a cheap dispute forum), copy the package from a successful peer group, but hand operational rules to members within weeks and date the first revision. IF an external authority insists on writing the rules THEN expect compliance only while enforced and budget accordingly. Never present the principles as a recipe, as anti-state or as evidence of fairness (ledger #29).

**Switch signal.** Toward design: members cannot agree or observe each other, or the problem is an externality nobody experiences directly; consultation has produced a half-launch. Toward self-governance: imposed rules are complied with only under watch, sanctions are appealed over the group's head, or members already run workarounds that work better than the official rule. Ask who changed the last rule, and whether compliance changed when enforcement attention moved.

**Common mistake.** The platform default: a complete rulebook written centrally, treated by the community as someone else's law. Its mirror: romanticizing self-governance and skipping external recognition, so the first serious dispute is appealed to an owner who never agreed the group may govern this domain.

## t07. Assurance and talk vs enforcement and commitment

**Pole A: most cooperation problems are trust problems; with repetition and reputation they are stag hunts, and what they need is assurance, which cheap talk provides.** Skyrms (`07`): the social contract is a stag hunt, not a prisoner's dilemma; talk is a transient handshake that expands the stag basin. Dixit et al. (`04`): most cooperation problems are not PDs; visible pledges and thresholds ("we start when ten sign up") reach the good outcome cheaply. Bicchieri (`08`): relevant face-to-face discussion is the strongest lever on public-goods and PD cooperation, from roughly 30% to roughly 70% in an eight-person dilemma (Dawes, McTavish and Shaklee 1977, background; quote as "roughly doubled"), with Sally 1995 and Balliet 2010 behind it; Gilovich et al. (`03`) and Ostrom's lab work agree.

**Pole B: many group problems are genuine multi-person PDs, talk does not fix them, and what they need is a payoff change, repetition with fast detection, sanctions or a costly commitment.** Ostrom (`09`): open-access commons and the California pumping race were real PDs; she refuted the PD as a law, not as the open-access case (ledger C49). Schelling (`05`) built the binary-choice diagram to tell MPDs from coordination and warns communication will not fix an MPD. Dixit et al.: cheap talk informs only when interests align; the alternatives are costly signals that separate committed from opportunistic types, commitment devices and credible sanctions. Skyrms concedes mimics invade talkers in a true PD; Camerer (`06`) shows contributions decay over rounds and recover with punishment.

**What the evidence says.** The poles agree on the diagnostic, binding in the ledger (X11, #13): does a member gain by defecting when everyone else cooperates? Yes: PD, needing payoff change, repetition or sanctions. No: stag hunt, needing assurance. Skyrms's thesis that the social contract is a stag hunt is interpretive and untested; some commons are PDs at the margin. On talk the disagreement is narrower than it looks (X12, ledger #12): Skyrms's agents have no normative term, so talk is transient in his PD models; Bicchieri's account, that relevant talk creates empirical and normative expectations and turns the PD into a coordination game for human subjects, explains the lab effect. Cheap talk is [E] in the lab for humans in PDs and public goods, [E] in models for stag hunts, and expected to decay without sanctions or repetition where defection dominates. Costly signals and commitment are theorems in `04` with the [H] caveat that any given ritual has the required cost asymmetry. Studies: Dawes, Sally's meta-analysis, Ostrom-Walker-Gardner, Camerer's decay curves. [E for the diagnostic and the lab effect of talk; H for persistence and any group's classification; V for preferring assurance to enforcement where both would work.]

**Rule.** IF nobody gains by defecting against full cooperation THEN build assurance and stop building enforcement: public intent statements, opt-in pledges, threshold launches, a leader naming the target. IF a member does gain THEN still hold relevant talk before the mixed-motive decision (extract explicit promises), but pair it with what stops decay: repetition with defection detected within a round or two, graduated sanctions, or a payoff change; where membership is the lever, screen with a costly signal whose cost is higher for opportunists. IF you cannot tell THEN run the talk first because it is cheap and watch whether cooperation decays over the following rounds; decay is the PD's signature. Never treat "we discussed it and agreed" as durable where defection pays (ledger #12), and never build sanctions for a trust problem.

**Switch signal.** Toward enforcement: cooperation after a good discussion sags round on round; promise-keepers end up worse off than promise-breakers; free riders increase rather than persist. Toward assurance: defections come in waves triggered by doubt about others rather than by opportunity, and sanctions when tried hit people who were merely uncertain. Measure cooperation at the meeting and again several rounds later.

**Common mistake.** Treating a PD as a communication problem and running another alignment offsite. Its mirror: installing monitoring and penalties on people who were only waiting for assurance, which signals distrust, lowers empirical expectations and can manufacture the defection it feared.

## t08. Group-level selection vs within-group mechanisms

**Pole A: cooperative norms exist because groups carrying them out-competed, out-grew or were imitated over groups without them; the unit of design is the group's norm package and identity.** Henrich (`01`): cultural group selection (war, migration, imitation, differential growth) selected cooperation-enhancing norm packages; the Nuer and Ilahita are the cases. Gintis (`11`) leans on group selection for strong reciprocity. The Origins books share a second hypothesis, that culture drove brain expansion (Henrich: the payoff of learning from others; Laland: a fidelity threshold plus teaching; Street et al. 2017, verified, correlational).

**Pole B: cooperation is scaffolded by socially learned norms and within-group mechanisms (reciprocity, reputation, punishment), which suffice; group selection is weakly evidenced and the brain story is contested.** Laland (`02`) is explicitly sceptical of cultural group selection; the 2016 Richerson et al. target article drew many critical commentaries asking whether between-group variation is large and stable enough and whether imitating successful groups is selection at all. On brains, DeCasien et al. 2017 found diet predicts primate brain size better than sociality.

**What the evidence says.** The poles agree on within-group mechanisms and on their being the evidenced part; they agree culture and brains co-evolved in some direction; they agree Nuer and Ilahita are illustrative, not statistical (the 01 file says so). The ledger fixes the design consequence (X2, X3; ledger #5, #7): within-group mechanisms are [E]; cultural group selection is [H] and never the load-bearing explanation for a design recommendation; culture-drove-brains is [H]. The tension is kept because it changes what a designer reaches for: a group-selection lens suggests inter-group competition, a superordinate identity and copying whole norm packages; a within-group lens suggests reciprocity, observation, reputation and sanctions. The files support both partially at the design level: minimal-group favouritism and intergroup contact (reliable moderate effect across 500+ studies, Pettigrew and Tropp 2006, background; rigorous field experiments fewer and smaller) are robust in `03`; Robbers Cave is a demonstration, not an estimate (ledger C19); copying a peer group's package is [H] (t06). The Origins evidence is Henrich's, Boyd's and Richerson's against Laland's and DeCasien's; none of it tests team-level interventions. [H for group selection and culture-drove-brains; E for within-group reciprocity, reputation and sanctions; H for identity interventions in your group.]

**Rule.** IF you are choosing between an identity intervention (rivalry, superordinate identity, "our way") and a within-group mechanism THEN build the within-group mechanism first and treat identity work as an accelerant tagged [H]. IF you use inter-group competition as a motivator THEN pair it with structured contact and a superordinate goal across the boundary, because the psychology that binds the in-group generates hostility across it. IF you import a norm package THEN copy from a visibly successful peer group and craft locally (t06). Never justify a design by "groups with this norm out-compete groups without it" (ledger #5).

**Switch signal.** Toward the group-level pole: cooperation inside is already reciprocal and monitored and the residual problem is commitment to the collective as such (turnover, indifference, no shared story). Toward the within-group pole: strong identity coexists with free-riding, or identity work has produced hostility to adjacent teams without raising contribution. Track contribution and identification separately.

**Common mistake.** Reaching for identity, ritual (ritual bonding is [H], ledger C5) and team-versus-team competition as the primary lever because they are cheap to announce. Its mirror: running a group as a pure reciprocity machine and forgoing a real, if moderate, identity effect.

## t09. Closure and homophily vs bridges and diversity

**Pole A: dense, closed, similar groups are where trust, enforcement and complex contagion work; protect closure and bounded membership.** Easley and Kleinberg (`10`): closure gives trust and enforcement (ch 3); costly behaviour needs several adopting neighbours and does not jump a single weak tie (complex contagion, ledger #34); a cluster of density above 1 − q blocks an outside norm and protects an inside one; homophily is pervasive (McPherson, Smith-Lovin and Cook 2001) and measurable (fewer cross-boundary edges than 2pq, theorem). Skyrms (`07`) makes correlation his master variable. Ostrom (`09`) finds the principles hold in small, dense, bounded groups.

**Pole B: connectivity, bridges and heterogeneity are where information, innovation and skill retention come from; silos lose skills and homogeneous groups polarize.** Henrich and Laland (`01`, `02`): better-connected learning networks retain and innovate more; isolation loses skills (Tasmania, contested; lab transmission chains, small scale). Easley and Kleinberg: bridges tend to be weak ties (Onnela et al. 2007, robust) and carry novelty. Gilovich et al. (`03`): homogeneous groups polarize; inject outside views before decisions and guarantee a credible dissenter. Laland's tournament: pure-copier populations stagnate; pay for innovation.

**What the evidence says.** The ledger's resolution of X15 is "both": connectivity for information and skill retention; closure and bounded membership for enforcement and complex contagion. Two corrections discipline it: the population-size claim is contested (Vaesen et al. 2016) and reframed as connectivity, not head-count (ledger #6); and "useful information comes from weak ties" is not robust (Gee et al. 2017 found most jobs via strong ties, background), only the structural claim that bridges tend to be weak (ledger #33). The disagreement is a design trade-off, not a factual one: every unit of closure added for enforcement and costly-norm spread is a unit of bridging forgone for information. Homogeneity and diversity map onto it: homophily produces the clusters that hold norms and block them (X7) and the polarization `03` warns about; diversity produces dissent and novelty and weakens complex contagion. Influence versus selection is unresolved for any observed similarity (Shalizi and Thomas 2011; ledger #35). Studies: Granovetter, Onnela, Centola, McPherson; Henrich's and Laland's transmission work; the polarization literature. [E for the trade-off and each mechanism; H for the right mix; V for how much diversity of view you want at the cost of speed of alignment.]

**Rule.** IF the job is to hold a costly norm, enforce it and spread it neighbour by neighbour THEN keep units small, dense and bounded, and when spreading to a resisting cluster build wide bridges (several parallel ties) or seed inside it. IF the job is information, innovation or retaining rare skills THEN find and protect the local bridges (usually weak ties, which decay unattended), rotate demonstrators into silos, and pay explicitly for innovators. IF a homogeneous group is about to decide THEN inject outside views before, not after, and have leaders speak last. Never say "clusters are bad" or "more connection is always better" (ledger #3, X15), and never infer influence from similarity.

**Switch signal.** Toward closure: a seeded norm keeps failing because nobody's neighbours have adopted it, or enforcement fails because violators have no repeated dealings with anyone who saw them. Toward bridges: the same solutions are reinvented in different subteams, a rare skill sits with one person, or decisions are unanimous fast and wrong. Counts: the 2pq ratio for the traits you care about; parallel ties between subgroups; holders per critical skill.

**Common mistake.** Reorganizing for cross-pollination while a young norm is still held by a few dense pockets, and dissolving the pockets. Its mirror: protecting closure until the group is a silo with one point of failure per skill and a consensus nobody outside shares.

## t10. Broadcasting the majority vs building normative expectations

**Pole A: make the target behaviour look like majority behaviour; people copy the majority under uncertainty.** Henrich (`01`): conformist transmission; publish counts where adoption is high and never advertise low adoption, because "most people don't do this" is itself transmitted. Gilovich et al. (`03`): descriptive-norm feedback on energy use gives about a 2% reduction, small but reliable at scale (Allcott 2011, background); Asch conformity holds across 133 studies in 17 countries (Bond and Smith 1996, verified), with about a third of critical trials and about 75% of participants conforming at least once in the original (background), plateauing at about three or four others (background).

**Pole B: empirical expectations alone do not make a social norm; without the normative channel descriptive messaging misfires and can boomerang.** Bicchieri (`08`) defines the social norm by both expectations and reads the mixed results of social-norms marketing, boomerangs included, as confirmation. Gilovich et al. agree from the other side: Schultz et al. 2007 found below-average users increased consumption when told the average unless an injunctive smiley was added; the Petrified Forest sign advertising theft raised theft.

**What the evidence says.** The file's clearest false tension once the taxonomy is read properly. The ledger records X14 as agreement once stated carefully (ledger #24): descriptive and injunctive signals must point the same way, and the injunctive channel is required for mixed-motive behaviour. Henrich's "use conformity deliberately" and Bicchieri's "empirical expectations are necessary, not sufficient" are one rule stated from two levels. The residue is small: Henrich's conformist bias operates where payoffs are uncertain and matching the majority is informative; Bicchieri's social norms operate where one gains by deviating and must believe others disapprove. Pluralistic ignorance (`08` ch 5, `03`) is the failure mode both recognize: most privately reject a norm, believe others accept it, and comply; disapproval must become common knowledge (ledger C44). Studies: Cialdini and Schultz on the boomerang; Allcott's field trial; Asch and Bond-Smith; Prentice and Miller on campus drinking; Bicchieri and Xiao 2009 (verified). [E for the pairing rule and the boomerang; H for the effect size in your group; V for whether nudging by counts is a legitimate way to treat members.]

**Rule.** IF the target behaviour is already the majority's THEN publish the count and pair it with a visible approval signal so the two channels align. IF it is a minority's THEN publish no prevalence; measure the expectation gap (what do you do; what do most do; what do most think you should do; what happens if you don't) and, where private attitudes are better than perceived norms, make the gap common knowledge through a credible source. IF the behaviour is mixed-motive THEN never rely on the descriptive channel alone; add the normative one, backed where needed by t02's sanctions. Never advertise how common misbehaviour is in order to shame it (ledger #24).

**Switch signal.** Toward broadcasting: private attitudes and behaviour are both good and only perceived prevalence is wrong. Toward the normative channel: after a favourable count is published, above-norm members regress toward it, or people can state the count and still do not believe anyone expects them to comply. Run the four-question elicitation before and after any messaging.

**Common mistake.** The dashboard reflex: publishing adoption metrics for a practice a minority follows and calling it transparency, thereby teaching the majority that not doing it is normal. Its mirror: an injunctive-only "why this matters" campaign while prestigious members visibly do the opposite; the empirical channel wins that contest.

## t11. Mis-specified preferences vs unstable beliefs

**Pole A: behavioural anomalies (loss aversion, hyperbolic discounting, social preferences) are state-dependent consistent preferences; write down beliefs, preferences and constraints and the anomalies dissolve into the model.** Gintis (`11`) keeps the rational actor and widens the utility function (BPC). Its design payoff, tagged [E] in the 11 file, is a discipline: for any failure ask what members believe about each other, what they value (status and fairness included) and what constrains them; most "incentive" fixes change constraints while beliefs about others stay put. Camerer's parametric programme (`06`) is the empirical cousin.

**Pole B: many anomalies are shifts in belief with presentation, mood and motive, not preferences of any shape; and learning is path-dependent rather than a fixed point.** Gilovich et al. (`03`): construal, framing, naive realism, motivated reasoning; the same matrix roughly doubles cooperation under a "Community Game" label (Liberman, Samuels and Ross 2004, background, on `03`'s robust list). Camerer's learning chapter: convergence is path-dependent; feedback and early rounds matter, while EWA's parameters do not carry the load (ledger C64).

**What the evidence says.** The 11 file's own verdict on the reframing is "partially holds up; overstated", and the ledger follows (X16): use BPC as bookkeeping, do not claim it explains construal effects, and note the falsifiability worry (once preferences may be state-dependent and norm-inclusive, what observation refutes the model?). The poles agree rationality is not selfishness and the anomalies are real; they disagree whether calling them preferences adds anything. The stakes are practical: a preference means change payoffs; a belief means change information, framing and the first rounds. The record is asymmetric. The construal effect and Nagel's beauty-contest result (first-round average about 35-36, verified, converging toward zero with repetition) are clean demonstrations that first-contact behaviour is belief-driven and learnable. But `03`'s broader belief-manipulation literature contains failures the course must not build on (priming; ego depletion at d = 0.04 with CI including zero, Hagger et al. 2016, verified; power posing; ledger #20), so "it's all framing" is not a licence either. Gintis ran no experiments for this claim; the framing evidence is Ross's; the learning evidence is Camerer's. [H for BPC as explanation; E for framing and first-round belief effects; E for BPC as a bookkeeping discipline.]

**Rule.** IF a behaviour persists across labels, framings and experience THEN treat it as a preference and change payoffs or constraints. IF it moves with the label, the order of information or the first rounds THEN treat it as a belief: relabel the game, seed the first rounds deliberately (first splits set the attractor, `08`, [H]), show forgone payoffs, expect convergence with feedback. IF you do not know THEN write the BPC table for the puzzling members and check which column your intended fix touches; the usual error is changing a constraint when the problem is a belief about others. Never build a belief intervention on priming, depletion or posture (ledger #20).

**Switch signal.** Toward preferences: relabelling and re-sequencing have been tried and behaviour is unchanged; experienced members behave like novices. Toward beliefs: behaviour differs between two teams under identical incentives, shifts when a respected member's public stance shifts, or converges over rounds toward what the payoff model predicts. Run the same decision under two labels and compare.

**Common mistake.** Absorbing every anomaly into a richer utility function and declaring the design incentive-compatible when it has merely renamed the problem. Its mirror: reframing endlessly while the payoff structure that punishes contribution stays untouched.

## t12. Explicit incentives and metrics vs norm-based motivation

**Pole A: make contribution a best response with explicit incentives and measurable accountability.** Dixit et al. (`04`): prices, penalties, monitoring at a probability roughly gain divided by penalty, screening. Easley and Kleinberg (`10`): institutional edits over exhortation. Ostrom (`09`): monitoring is a design principle, a by-product of participation in every durable case, and sanctions are graduated but real. Camerer (`06`): contributions decay without punishment and recover with it.

**Pole B: norms exploit an existing disposition, are cheaper to maintain, and explicit payment and scorekeeping can crowd them out.** Gintis (`11`) tags "prefer the norm to the incentive when both are available" [H]: crowding out is real but context-dependent. Gilovich et al. (`03`): overjustification; introducing scorekeeping into a communal team damages it ([E] for the mechanism); use small voluntary public commitments and modest rewards, and avoid paying heavily for behaviour you want members to own. Ostrom's field evidence (Cardenas, background): weakly enforced external regulation crowds out communication-driven cooperation. Camerer's field result: above-market pay boosted effort for the first few hours only and cost more than it returned (Gneezy and List 2006, verified), while cuts and non-monetary gestures have larger, more persistent effects (Kube et al. 2012, background; ledger #28). Dixit's own caveat: partial-metric incentives displace unmeasured effort; with noisy output, lean on norms and reputation.

**What the evidence says.** This is the measurement-and-accountability tension, and the files agree more than the poles suggest. Everyone accepts that some observation is necessary (t02 needs monitoring, t05 needs identifiability, Ostrom's principle 4) and that money is a weak, decaying lever for positive reciprocity and a strong one for negative. The narrow disagreement is form: accountability as an explicit metric with attached reward, or as by-product visibility (public logs, turn-taking that exposes cheaters) that keeps empirical expectations accurate without turning a communal relationship into an exchange one. The ledger's rules lean to the by-product form (#28, #30) while keeping `04`'s credible-sanction requirement. Caveats: crowding out is context-dependent ([H] in `11`); the scorekeeping warning is [E] for the mechanism and [H] for your group; measurement displacement is theory plus [H]. No single study pits a metric against a norm in a team; this paragraph is assembled from five files' side remarks. Studies: Gneezy and List, Kube, Cardenas, the overjustification paradigms, the Cox review. [E for monitoring and cheap sanctions being necessary and for pay as a weak positive lever; H for crowding out in your group; V for whether members are exchange partners or a community.]

**Rule.** IF the behaviour is a genuine PD contribution people will not make on trust (t07) THEN attach real accountability, but make monitoring a by-product of the work (visible commits, public rotas) and the first sanction cheap and graduated; reserve financial incentives for measurable, low-noise outputs. IF the behaviour is one you want members to own (review quality, mentoring, speaking up) THEN use small public commitments, modest recognition and norms; never introduce individual scorekeeping into a communal team without expecting damage, and if a metric is needed make it collective. IF you must use a metric THEN name the unmeasured effort it will displace and monitor that too, and treat any reward cut or broken promise as a strong negative-reciprocity event. Never buy contribution with a raise and expect it to last (ledger #28), and never leave a PD to run on norms alone without observation.

**Switch signal.** Toward incentives: contribution decays despite talk and visibility, free riders are stable and identifiable, output is measurable with low noise. Toward norms: after a metric or reward appeared, the measured behaviour rose and an unmeasured one fell, members began asking what they get for things they used to do, or the boost faded within days. Track the measured behaviour and at least one unmeasured behaviour that shares its effort budget.

**Common mistake.** The product default: instrument everything, add a leaderboard, and turn a community into a market whose members now expect payment for what they used to give. Its mirror: refusing all measurement in the name of trust, so free-riding is invisible, expectations drift, and the conditional cooperators stop.

## t13. Shifting expectations vs changing structure

**Pole A: change a norm by changing what people expect of each other; the levers are measurement, publicity, talk and trendsetters, inside one reference network.** Bicchieri (`08-grammar-of-society`): a norm lives in empirical and normative expectations, so the intervention is to elicit them (what do you do; what do most do; what do most think you should do; what happens if you don't), publish the gap where private attitudes are better than perceived norms, hold relevant talk that creates new expectations, and recruit trendsetters (low sensitivity to the bad norm, high credibility in the reference network; [H], case studies from sanitation and child-marriage programmes). Bicchieri and Xiao (2009, verified) showed expectations of selfishness reduced giving even under pro-giving messages, which is why expectations, not attitudes, are the target. Gilovich et al. (`03`) supply pluralistic ignorance and the Community Game relabelling (roughly doubled cooperation, Liberman, Samuels and Ross 2004, background).

**Pole B: change the structure that decides who sees the signal and whether switching pays; the levers are thresholds, payoffs, seeding, clusters, bridges and rules.** Schelling (`05-micromotives-and-macrobehavior`): aggregate outcomes are properties of threshold distributions and payoff curves; a tipping point is an unstable equilibrium, and the scarce resource is low-threshold actors, not charismatic ones (ledger #18, C30); attitudes move the tipping point but do not remove the dynamic (Card, Mas and Rothstein 2008, verified: tipping at roughly 5-20% minority share, higher where measured tolerance is higher). Easley and Kleinberg (`10-networks-crowds-markets`): adoption spreads when the adopting-neighbour fraction exceeds q = b/(a+b) (theorem); a cluster of density above 1 − q blocks it; the fixes are lower q, seed inside the cluster, build wide bridges. Skyrms (`07`): correlation, not persuasion, moves a population between basins. Ostrom (`09`): change the rules, boundaries and monitoring, and expectations follow because monitoring keeps them accurate. The `10` file names the tension directly: Bicchieri targets expectations; Easley and Kleinberg target structure and payoffs.

**What the evidence says.** The poles agree on more than they admit. Bicchieri's trendsetters are Easley and Kleinberg's seed set and Schelling's low-threshold nodes (glossary; `10` connections); Schelling's thresholds are Bicchieri's conditional preferences in population form (`05` connections); Ostrom's monitoring is how empirical expectations stay accurate (`09` connections). Both sides reject the dispositional reading that the brief's Schelling candidate warns against: the fundamental attribution error is robust (`03`), Schelling's model shows mild preferences can produce extreme sorting, and the course's rule is to diagnose situationally before dispositionally (ledger #17, C23). The narrow disagreement is scope and sequence. Expectation work assumes everyone is in one reference network and can see the same signal; the `03` file adds that Bicchieri is more optimistic about correcting beliefs alone than the identity and dissonance literature warrants, and social-norms marketing has mixed results including boomerangs (t10). Structure work assumes the payoff and network conditions are what block adoption; it says nothing about what a norm is to the agent, and `07`'s dynamics have no beliefs in them at all. The seed list's resolution is the course's: expectations where the group is one reference network; structure where clusters and bridges decide who sees the signal. Schelling's claim that structure explains most group failures is a hypothesis in any given group (`05` evidence section), and the model's fragility to preference shape is disputed (Bruch and Mare 2006 against Van de Rijt et al. 2009). Studies: Bicchieri's field programmes and elicitation experiments; Schelling's demonstrations and the Card-Mas-Rothstein tract data; Granovetter's threshold model; Kleinberg's theorems; Centola's clustered-network experiment (direction only). [E for each mechanism; H for which one blocks your norm and for trendsetter interventions; V for whether the equilibrium you are steering toward is one the members would choose.]

**Rule.** IF the group is one reference network (members see each other, the same signal reaches everyone) and the elicitation shows a gap between private attitudes and perceived norms THEN shift expectations: publish the gap through a credible source, hold relevant talk, extract public commitments, and seed with low-threshold high-credibility members chosen for position, not charisma. IF the group is several clusters, adoption is stuck below q in the cluster that matters, or the lone adopter pays a real cost THEN change structure first: lower q (tooling, defaults, compatibility), seed inside the resisting cluster, build wide bridges, change the rule or the monitoring, and let expectations follow. IF the outcome looks like a character problem THEN model the interaction before blaming anyone: is behaviour contingent, does an accounting constraint force the outcome, where is the threshold. Never run an expectations campaign across clusters that cannot see each other, and never use the Gladwell sense of "influencer" (ledger #18).

**Switch signal.** Toward structure: expectations have been measured and published, people can state the correct count and the norm, and adoption is still stuck in specific subgroups, or those who switched first are visibly worse off. Toward expectations: the structure has been changed (q lowered, seeds placed, rules rewritten) and the same behaviour persists with people who can see each other every day and still believe the others do not care. The test in both directions: swap the people and keep the structure, then the reverse, and see which the pattern follows.

**Common mistake.** The dispositional diagnosis that both poles reject: hiring for fit, values workshops or removing the "toxic" person, after which the structure produces a new one. Its mirror: treating every norm as a threshold problem and skipping the elicitation, so that a norm held up only by pluralistic ignorance, which a single credible publication would have collapsed, gets an expensive reorganization instead.

## t14. Small bounded groups vs open scale

**Pole A: cooperation is a small-group phenomenon; the evidence lives in bounded, dense, stable groups, and scale is handled by nesting, not growing.** Ostrom (`09`): clear boundaries are principle 1 (ambiguous membership is the commonest structural cause of decay, [E]) and nested enterprises principle 8; her cases are small and bounded, outsiders are excluded, and several durable systems are inegalitarian. Camerer (`06`): weak-link groups of 14-16 collapsed while fixed pairs reached the top (Van Huyck et al. 1990); cap the group whose minimum matters. Gilovich et al. (`03`): working units of 2-5 to limit loafing (a rule of thumb, [H]). Skyrms (`07`): small stable neighbourhoods are the correlation device. Dixit et al. (`04`): with many players, who punishes whom is hard.

**Pole B: larger, connected populations retain more knowledge and innovate more; open membership is where growth, information and legitimacy come from, and gradual growth can carry coordination to scale.** Henrich and Laland (`01`, `02`): cumulative culture depends on the size and connectivity of the learning network (contested as head-count, ledger #6). Camerer: large groups grown from a small core with visible history sustain high effort (Weber 2006, verified, single-lab lineage). Easley and Kleinberg (`10`): short paths exist in any large network; a practice with network effects is worth more the more use it, and adoption above the tipping point self-sustains. Ostrom's own scope note: the book concerns subtractable resources with costly exclusion; knowledge commons and open source found partial fit with principles 1 and 2 reinterpreted (ledger C48), which is the learner's platform case.

**What the evidence says.** Agreement: every mechanism in this course (observation, reputation, sanctions, complex contagion, assurance) weakens as the group grows and interaction becomes anonymous, and every knowledge-and-innovation benefit strengthens. Nobody claims Ostrom's principles are proven at scale (the 09 file names scale as where they are least tested; transfer to digital commons is an open question in the ledger). Nobody claims small groups innovate. Two narrow disagreements. First, whether scale is reached by nesting (Ostrom) or by growth from a core (Weber); treating these as compatible sequencing is this file's reading, not a book's claim. Second, the boundary itself, where the learner's values enter most directly: a boundary that makes monitoring and sanctions work also excludes; durability and fairness are different criteria (`09` misreadings); open platforms trade enforcement for reach. The course cannot settle how much exclusion a learner should accept for how much cooperation; it insists the trade be named and tagged [V]. Studies: Van Huyck's and Weber's lab lineages; the Cox review; Kline and Boyd's Oceania correlation (clean, single-region). [E for the small-group results and the boundary principle; H for nesting and gradual growth beyond one lab; V for who is in and who is out.]

**Rule.** IF a behaviour depends on the minimum contributor, on mutual observation or on sanctions THEN keep the unit small (a handful; 2-5 is `03`'s rule of thumb, [H]), bounded and stable, and scale by nesting: local units govern local problems, a higher layer handles conflict between units and shared infrastructure. IF the goal is knowledge, innovation or a network-effect practice THEN grow, but from a small core with a visible success record, and estimate whether adoption is above the tipping point before opening the doors. IF you apply Ostrom to a digital or knowledge commons THEN flag the scope and reinterpret boundaries as membership and contribution roles. Never scale a weak-link process by adding people to the unit whose minimum matters, and never define a boundary without writing down, as a value, whom it excludes and why that is acceptable.

**Switch signal.** Toward small and bounded: minimum-effort outcomes appear as the unit passes a dozen; violations go unsanctioned because nobody has repeated dealings with the violator; membership is ambiguous and decay tracks it. Toward scale: rare skills sit with single people, the same problems are solved repeatedly in isolation, or a network-effect practice is stuck below its tipping point. Counts: unit size against the minimum-effort trend; fraction of violations sanctioned; holders per critical skill.

**Common mistake.** Treating the whole platform or organization as "the group" and expecting community-level cooperation from thousands of anonymous members. Its mirror: keeping the unit small and closed until it is a guild, durable and unfair, without ever deciding that exclusion was the price.

## How to use this file

Before a contested decision in your Cooperation brief: find the tension that names it, state which pole you lean to and why, apply the IF branch that fits your group, and write the switch signal into the Cooperation brief next to the decision as its [H] test, so that a future observation can tell you that you chose wrong. A decision that fits no tension is either a pure [V] (say so, and name who in the reading list would dissent) or a pure [E] (cite the mechanism and its slug); if it is neither, it is not yet a decision. When several tensions apply they usually nest: t07 (which game) before t04 (which equilibrium) before t09 and t14 (what structure holds it) before t12 (what accountability it needs); t05, t10 and t13 are diagnostics you run at any point; t01, t02, t03, t06, t08 and t11 are about which model of the people you are using. The critic's tensions (t02, t03, t11) keep the brief honest about what the evidence does not show.
