# The Grammar of Society: The Nature and Dynamics of Social Norms — Cristina Bicchieri (Cambridge University Press, 2006)
Level: Order  ·  Priority: Core
Est. reading time saved: ~9h  ·  Your time with this file: ~35 min

## The book in one sentence
A social norm is not a value people hold or a habit they have; it is a rule people prefer to follow *on condition that* they expect enough others to follow it and believe enough others expect them to follow it — and once you see norms this way, you can explain why they persist when nobody likes them, why they switch on and off with tiny changes in framing, and how they change.

## The book in one paragraph
Bicchieri, a philosopher working with game theory and experimental economics, argues that most accounts of norms fail because they treat norm-following either as pure self-interest (norms as equilibria enforced by sanctions) or as internalized values (norms as preferences). Her alternative is a precise, testable definition: a behavioral rule R is a social norm in a population when a sufficient share of people (a) know the rule applies to a situation, and (b) prefer to conform to it *conditional on* two beliefs — an empirical expectation that enough others conform, and a normative expectation that enough others think they ought to conform (possibly backed by willingness to sanction). This conditional structure separates social norms from descriptive norms and conventions (which need only empirical expectations) and from moral norms (which need neither). Because norms are activated by cues, not by deliberation, she draws on cognitive psychology — scripts, schemata, categorization — to explain why the same person is a generous sharer in one framing and a calculating maximizer in another, and reinterprets the ultimatum, dictator, trust and social-dilemma experiments through this lens. Norms transform mixed-motive games into coordination games by changing the payoffs conditional on expectations. Finally, she models how unpopular norms survive through pluralistic ignorance and informational cascades, and how a fairness norm can evolve among boundedly rational agents.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
Read it with this question: *when my group "has a norm," which of Bicchieri's four things does it actually have — a habit, a convention, a social norm or a moral commitment — and which expectations would I have to shift to change it?* Henrich and Laland (Origins) explain why humans are norm-acquiring animals; Camerer and Dixit et al. (Mechanisms) give you the games and the anomalies; Skyrms shows how conventions emerge dynamically. Bicchieri supplies the missing middle layer: a diagnostic definition of a norm that tells you what to measure and what lever to pull. It is also the theoretical foundation of the applied toolkit (*Norms in the Wild*, 2017) that UNICEF and development agencies now use to attack practices like open defecation and child marriage.

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

### Introduction — what a theory of norms must do
- Core claims: Existing accounts either reduce norms to sanction-backed equilibria (economics, rational-choice sociology) or to internalized values (Parsons-style sociology, some psychology). Both fail: the first cannot explain conformity without monitoring, the second cannot explain how quickly norm-following collapses when expectations change. A good theory must explain conformity *and* its fragility with the same machinery.
- Bicchieri's stance is deliberately deflationary about motives: you do not need to assume people value fairness in order to explain fair behavior; conditional preferences plus expectations do the work.
- So-what: Before intervening in a group, stop asking "what do these people value?" and ask "what do they expect of each other, and what do they think is expected of them?"

### Ch 1 — The Rules We Live By
- Core claims:
  1. **The definition.** Let R be a behavioral rule for situations of type S. R is a *social norm* in population P if there is a sufficiently large subset of P such that each member (i) knows R exists and applies to S (contingency), and (ii) prefers to conform to R in S *on condition that* (a) she believes a sufficiently large subset of P conforms (empirical expectation) and (b) she believes a sufficiently large subset of P expects her to conform, and may be willing to sanction deviation (normative expectation). The norm is *followed* when these conditions are actually met; a norm can exist while being widely violated.
  2. **Four kinds of rule, distinguished by the expectations they require.** A *descriptive norm* (e.g., "most people here leave work at 6") is followed because you expect others to follow it and prefer to match — empirical expectations only. A *convention* (drive on the right; dress code) is a descriptive norm that is also a coordination equilibrium: everyone prefers to conform given others do, and nobody has an incentive to deviate unilaterally. A *social norm* (tipping, reciprocity, share fairly) runs *against* what you would do absent the norm — the underlying game is mixed-motive (a prisoner's dilemma, a trust game, an ultimatum game) — so you need normative expectations, not just empirical ones, to get compliance. A *moral norm* is followed unconditionally: you would not steal even if everyone did and nobody cared.
  3. **Norms transform games.** A social norm does not change the material payoffs; it changes the *utility* players attach to outcomes given their expectations. Bicchieri's norm-based utility function: a player's utility is her material payoff minus a term proportional to (her sensitivity to the norm) times (the largest loss any player suffers from her deviation from the norm, relative to what the norm prescribed). When expectations that the norm applies are present, the mixed-motive game becomes a coordination game with a norm-following equilibrium; when expectations are absent, it reverts to the original game.
  4. **Preference, not value.** Conditional preference is the key concept. A person who "supports" the norm in the abstract but expects no one else to comply will not comply — and this is rational within her utility function, not hypocrisy.
- Key model / worked example: Take a two-person PD with cooperate/defect payoffs (3,3), (0,5), (5,0), (1,1). Suppose a norm of cooperation and a sensitivity k. If you defect while your partner cooperates, the norm-deviation penalty is proportional to the partner's loss (3 − 0 = 3). With k ≥ 2/3, defecting yields 5 − 3k ≤ 3, so cooperate-cooperate becomes an equilibrium. Crucially, it only becomes one *if you expect the other to follow the norm* — otherwise the penalty term does not bite and defection is again dominant. The game now has two equilibria (mutual cooperation, mutual defection): a stag hunt rather than a PD.
- So-what: The four-way taxonomy is the single most useful diagnostic tool in this curriculum. A "no-blame post-mortem" that actually happens is a social norm only if people would blame in its absence *and* comply because they expect others to comply and to disapprove of blaming. If people simply do not want to blame, it is a moral norm and needs no maintenance. If they copy what the loudest person does, it is a descriptive norm and will flip when that person changes.

### Ch 2 — Habits of the Mind
- Core claims:
  1. Norm-following is mostly *automatic*, not deliberated. People do not consult their expectations and run a cost-benefit analysis; they categorize the situation, retrieve a **script** (a stored sequence of expected roles and actions) or **schema** (a structured knowledge representation), and the script carries default expectations and the norm with it.
  2. Therefore norms are *cued*. Whether a norm is activated depends on how the situation is interpreted, which depends on salience, priming, labeling, the presence of others, and small features of the environment. The same objective situation can trigger different scripts and hence different norms — or no norm at all.
  3. This explains the apparent inconsistency of behavior across contexts that so troubles both rational-choice and value-internalization theories. Nothing about the person changed; the categorization did.
  4. It also explains why people are poor at predicting their own norm-following: asked in the abstract, they report values; in the situation, the script fires.
- Key studies (from background knowledge of the chapter, not re-verified): scripts from Schank & Abelson (1977); situationism such as Darley & Batson's (1973) "Good Samaritan" study; Cialdini, Reno & Kallgren's (1990) injunctive/descriptive norm distinction, which maps roughly onto normative/empirical expectations — though Bicchieri insists both are needed for a *social* norm.
- So-what: Norm design is largely *cue* design. If you want the review norm followed, the pull-request template, the channel name and the first comment in the thread matter more than the policy document. Ask which script your group's artifacts summon.

### Ch 3 — A Taste for Fairness
- Core claims:
  1. Ultimatum-game results (proposers offer ~40-50%; responders reject offers below ~20-30%) are usually explained by a "taste for fairness" or inequity aversion (Fehr & Schmidt 1999; Bolton & Ockenfels 2000). Bicchieri argues fixed-preference models cannot explain the *variability* of fair behavior across framings; an expectation-conditional norm model can.
  2. Evidence marshalled: offers fall when proposers "earn" the role via a quiz (Hoffman, McCabe, Shachat & Smith 1994); dictator giving collapses under double-blind anonymity (Hoffman, McCabe & Smith 1996); many dictators pay to *exit* rather than share (Dana, Cain & Dawes 2006) and take "moral wiggle room" when the link between choice and recipient outcome is hidden (Dana, Weber & Kuang 2007); offers track information about what other proposers offered; and offers vary across the 15 small-scale societies of Henrich et al. (2001, 2004) in line with local norms of gift exchange and market integration.
  3. Interpretation: the "taste" is conformity to a fairness norm activated by cues of observation, entitlement and expectation. Remove the cues and the taste evaporates. Dictators are not being fair; they comply with a norm they expect to be judged by, and take any exit that avoids it without visible violation.
  4. The responder's rejection is the sanction that makes normative expectations credible; proposers' offers track their beliefs about what responders expect and will punish.
- Key model: Bicchieri applies the norm-based utility function to the ultimatum game and shows that a distribution of norm-sensitivities k across proposers, plus beliefs about responders' expectations, reproduces the observed offer and rejection distributions, including the modal fair offer and the rejections of low offers.
- Later validation (extension, not in the 2006 book): Bicchieri & Xiao (2009) manipulated expectations in a dictator game with messages about what earlier participants did or said should be done; empirical expectations of selfishness reduced giving even when normative messages endorsed giving. Bicchieri & Chavez (2010) found ultimatum proposers picked an option (a coin flip) that let them look fair while keeping more.
- So-what: If "we share credit" only happens when the manager is watching, you have a social norm with weak normative expectations, not a value. Making outcomes visible (removing wiggle room) is a cheaper lever than changing hearts.

### Ch 4 — Covenants Without Swords
- Core claims:
  1. Hobbes's line — covenants without the sword are but words — predicts that promises and talk cannot sustain cooperation in social dilemmas absent enforcement. Yet the most robust finding in experimental public-goods and PD research is that *cheap talk* (face-to-face communication, pre-play discussion) dramatically increases cooperation (Dawes, McTavish & Shaklee 1977; Isaac & Walker 1988; Sally 1995 meta-analysis; Ostrom, Walker & Gardner 1992). Why?
  2. Standard explanations — communication creates group identity (Dawes, van de Kragt & Orbell), or lets players commit — are incomplete. Bicchieri's account: communication *creates or makes salient the expectations that a norm requires*. Discussion lets people (a) discover that a norm of cooperation applies to this situation (categorization), (b) form empirical expectations that others will cooperate, and (c) form normative expectations by hearing others express what ought to be done and by making promises that generate an obligation others will hold them to.
  3. Evidence she uses: communication works only when *relevant* to the dilemma; unenforceable promises still matter; partial communication yields partial effects. Bicchieri & Lev-On (2007, later) found face-to-face beats computer-mediated talk because it generates richer expectations.
  4. In norm-based utility terms: talk raises the chance that a critical mass expects the norm to apply, flipping the game from PD to coordination, where cooperation is self-enforcing among norm-sensitive players. The sword is the anticipated disapproval of the people you just talked to.
- Key study: Dawes, McTavish & Shaklee (1977) — 8-person social dilemma; ten minutes of relevant discussion raised cooperation from roughly 30% to roughly 70%; irrelevant discussion did almost nothing. (Figures approximate, from background knowledge; not re-verified.)
- So-what: *What happens before the interaction* — the kickoff, the retro, the thread where people say what they think should happen — is the mechanism by which the game gets reclassified. Schedule it, and make it about the actual dilemma.

### Ch 5 — Informational Cascades and Unpopular Norms
- Core claims:
  1. Many norms persist although most people privately dislike them: hazing, drinking norms on campus, foot-binding historically, "always look busy" cultures. The mechanism is **pluralistic ignorance**: each person privately rejects the norm but believes most others accept it, because she infers others' attitudes from their public behavior — and their behavior is itself conformity driven by the same mistaken inference. Everyone complies with a norm nobody supports.
  2. Pluralistic ignorance is an *informational* failure about normative expectations, and it is stable because the very behavior it produces confirms the false belief. Prentice & Miller (1993) on Princeton undergraduates and alcohol is the canonical study: students overestimated peers' comfort with campus drinking, and the gap predicted conformity and, for men, drift toward the perceived norm.
  3. Bicchieri connects this to **informational cascades** (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch 1992): when people act sequentially and see others' choices but not their private signals, early choices swamp later private information and everyone falls in line even though pooled information points the other way. Cascades explain how an unpopular norm arises quickly and, because it rests on little information, how a credible public signal (a survey revealing true attitudes, an undismissable defector) can collapse it.
  4. Norm change therefore has two routes: shift empirical expectations (show that others already deviate) or normative expectations (show that others do not approve). This chapter seeds the "trendsetter" and "measure expectations" ideas of *Norms in the Wild*.
- Key model: A sequential-choice cascade: each agent gets a private binary signal about whether norm X is good and observes predecessors' actions; once two consecutive agents choose the same way, all rational followers ignore their own signal. Add a normative-expectation term and you get conformity to a norm that a majority privately judges wrong.
- So-what: Before concluding "this team likes crunch," run an anonymous expectation survey: what do you do, what do you think others do, what do you think others think you should do? A large gap between private attitude and perceived norm is a cheap, high-leverage intervention point. Publish the results.

### Ch 6 — The Evolution of a Fairness Norm (with appendix)
- Core claims:
  1. Where does a fairness norm come from if nobody "chooses" it? Bicchieri builds an evolutionary model of the ultimatum game among boundedly rational agents who learn (via a reinforcement or replicator-style dynamic) which demands and which acceptance thresholds do well, and who hold beliefs about what others will do.
  2. Result in plain words: populations do not converge to the subgame-perfect equilibrium (demand almost everything, accept anything). Because responders who reject low offers survive when proposers learn to avoid rejection, and because agents condition on expectations, the dynamics can settle on a range of "fair" divisions — but *which* division depends on initial conditions and on the population's belief structure. The norm is an equilibrium selected by history, not by justice; Bicchieri's point is that the *existence* of a fairness norm is explicable without assuming a taste for fairness, while its *content* is path-dependent.
  3. She contrasts this with Skyrms's (1996, 2004) replicator analyses of divide-the-dollar, where 50-50 emerges as the only evolutionarily stable equilibrium with equal populations, and with Young's (1993) stochastic learning models; her agents are more cognitively realistic (they form and update expectations) and the conclusion is correspondingly less deterministic.
  4. The appendix formalizes the norm-based utility function and the learning dynamics.
- So-what: Fairness norms in a group are locally emergent and sticky. The early splits, the first credit-allocations, the first few code-review tones set attractors. Deliberately seeding the first rounds matters more than writing the policy later.

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. **A social norm = conditional preference + empirical expectation + normative expectation.** If any leg is missing, you have something else (a habit, a convention, a moral conviction, or a dead rule).
2. **Diagnose by the expectations, not the behavior.** The same observed behavior can be a descriptive norm, a convention, a social norm or a moral norm, and each responds to different levers.
3. **Norms change games, not payoffs.** A social norm turns a PD or ultimatum game into a coordination game with a norm-following equilibrium — *only while expectations that the norm applies are live.*
4. **Norm activation is cognitive and cue-driven.** Scripts and schemata select the norm; framing, observation, and salience switch it on or off without changing anyone's values.
5. **"Taste for fairness" is mostly norm compliance.** Fair behavior in experiments varies with anonymity, entitlement, moral wiggle room and information about others; a fixed preference cannot explain that variance.
6. **Cheap talk works because it manufactures expectations.** Relevant discussion and promises make the cooperation norm salient and credible — covenants need no sword when everyone has just said out loud what they expect.
7. **Unpopular norms live on pluralistic ignorance.** People infer others' approval from others' compliance, which is itself driven by the same inference. The norm is a shared misperception.
8. **Cascades cut both ways.** Because a cascade rests on little information, it is fragile; a credible public signal or a visible trendsetter can collapse an unpopular norm fast.
9. **Fairness norms are history-dependent equilibria.** They can evolve among boundedly rational learners without anyone valuing fairness, and which split becomes the norm is path-dependent.
10. **To change a norm, change what people believe about each other** — measure expectations, reveal them, and coordinate deviation.

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **Empirical expectation** — belief about what others *do* — bites when a team copies visible behavior (e.g., how late people stay).
- **Normative expectation** — belief about what others think one *ought* to do, possibly backed by sanctions — bites in credit-sharing, honesty in stand-ups, and any mixed-motive situation.
- **Conditional preference** — wanting to follow a rule *if* the expectations hold — explains why "buy-in" surveys mispredict behavior.
- **Descriptive norm** — regularity followed on empirical expectations alone — fragile; flips when the visible majority changes.
- **Convention** — a descriptive norm that is a coordination equilibrium — self-enforcing; needs no monitoring (formats, naming, meeting times).
- **Social norm (Bicchieri's sense)** — rule requiring normative expectations because it runs against private incentives — needs maintenance; collapses when people stop believing others expect it.
- **Moral norm** — unconditional rule — does not depend on the group; cannot be engineered by changing expectations.
- **Norm-based utility** — material payoff minus k × (largest loss imposed by deviation) when the norm is expected to apply — a way to compute when a norm is enough to flip a game.
- **Script / schema** — stored situational template carrying roles, expected actions and the associated norm — norms live here, not in the handbook.
- **Norm activation / salience** — whether the cue set triggers the script — most of the practical leverage.
- **Moral wiggle room** — a situational feature that lets someone avoid a norm without visibly violating it — remove it if you want compliance.
- **Pluralistic ignorance** — most privately reject a norm but believe most others accept it — the diagnosis for "nobody likes this but we all do it."
- **Informational cascade** — sequential imitation that swamps private signals — explains fast rise and fast collapse of practices.
- **Trendsetter** (from *Norms in the Wild*) — a first-mover with low sensitivity to the norm, high autonomy, and enough social visibility to be credible — the lever for collapsing a bad norm.
- **Reference network** (from *Norms in the Wild*) — the set of people whose expectations actually matter to a given person — the unit at which norms should be measured and changed.

## Evidence strength & limits
- **Robust.** The regularities she builds on are among the best-replicated in behavioral economics: fair ultimatum offers and rejection of low offers (dozens of countries, including Henrich et al.'s cross-cultural work); the collapse of dictator giving under double-blind anonymity; the communication effect in social dilemmas (Sally 1995 and Balliet 2010 meta-analyses); moral wiggle room (Dana, Weber & Kuang 2007, replicated with varying effect sizes). Pluralistic ignorance about campus drinking is well documented, though "social norms marketing" interventions have mixed results, including boomerang effects — itself consistent with her claim that empirical expectations alone do not make a social norm.
- **Later direct tests (verified via search snippets).** Bicchieri & Xiao (2009) and the two-step norm-elicitation method are widely used; independent work (a 2022 SSRN paper on social-desirability bias, a 2024 pre-registered trial of procedure variants) finds it usable but sensitive to design details. Krupka & Weber (2013) offer a competing coordination-game elicitation.
- **Argued rather than shown.** That norm-based utility beats inequity-aversion models is an argument from fit and parsimony, not a decisive horse race; the norm model's free parameters (k, thresholds) accommodate much. The Ch 6 model is a possibility proof, not history. The script story is imported from psychology and asserted more than tested.
- **Known critiques.**
  - *Fairness is still needed.* A paper in *Philosophy of Science* ("Fairness and Social Norms," surfaced via search) argues that her theory cannot account for ultimatum results without an independent notion of fairness: expectations alone do not tell you *which* division people expect, so a substantive fairness concept sneaks back in.
  - *Too narrow a concept of norm.* Brennan, Eriksson, Goodin & Southwood, *Explaining Norms* (2013), argue that Bicchieri defines only one species (social norms) and that her requirement of normative *expectations* (beliefs about others' beliefs) misses cases where a norm exists as a shared normative attitude without anyone expecting anyone to expect anything; they offer an attitude-based account instead.
  - *Conditional preferences vs. internalization.* Critics from the Henrich/Boyd/Gintis tradition (see 01-secret-of-our-success, 11-bounds-of-reason) argue that norm internalization is real and evolutionarily explicable, and that Bicchieri's deflationary "no taste for fairness needed" underweights the third-party-punishment and strong-reciprocity evidence in which people pay to enforce norms on strangers with no expectations at stake. Bicchieri's reply is that moral norms exist too; the dispute is about how much of observed behavior falls in each bin.
  - *Circularity worries.* Sugden and others have noted that identifying a "sufficiently large subset" and the relevant "situation type S" is done ex post; the theory risks defining the norm by the behavior it is meant to explain. The 2017 measurement program is in part a response to this.
  - *Descriptive/normative dichotomy is fuzzy in practice.* Field studies (Bicchieri's own later Bangladesh and India sanitation work) show empirical and normative expectations are highly correlated and hard to disentangle; the clean taxonomy is a model, not a field observation.
- **What she never claims.** She does not say sanctions are unnecessary — normative expectations are often backed by anticipated sanctions — only that internalized sanction-fear and explicit enforcement are neither necessary nor sufficient. She does not claim norms are always efficient; Ch 5 is about bad ones.

## Design implications for cooperation in real groups
- **Classify every "norm" by which expectations sustain it** (descriptive, convention, social, moral) before choosing a lever. [E]
- **Measure expectations, not attitudes.** Ask: what do you do; what do most people here do; what do most people here think you should do; what happens if you don't. [E] — the two-step elicitation is validated, with caveats.
- **When private attitudes and perceived norms diverge, publish the gap.** [E] for the diagnosis; [H] for the intervention in *your* group — social-norms marketing has mixed results and depends on source credibility.
- **Design the cues, not just the rules.** Templates, channel names, meeting openers determine which script fires. [H] — the specific cue-to-norm mapping must be tested locally.
- **Hold relevant discussion before mixed-motive decisions** (resource splits, on-call rotas, credit). Make people say what they expect and what should happen; extract explicit promises. [E]
- **Close moral wiggle room.** Make consequences for others visible and unavoidable; remove exits that let people dodge the norm unseen. [E] in the lab; [H] in organizations.
- **Seed early rounds deliberately.** First splits and first review interactions set the fairness attractor. [H] — path dependence is shown in simulation, not field data.
- **Identify and protect trendsetters** — low sensitivity to the bad norm, high credibility in the relevant reference network — to collapse an unpopular practice. [H] — case studies, not controlled tests.
- **Do not rely on values statements to create social norms.** [V] that norms should be built this way; [E] that stated values alone predict behavior poorly.
- **Make conventions wherever you can.** Anything that can be a coordination equilibrium (formats, defaults) is self-enforcing; save maintenance effort for genuinely mixed-motive behaviors. [E]

## Retrieval practice

1. **Recall.** State Bicchieri's two conditions for a rule to be a social norm and name the two kinds of expectation involved.
<details>Contingency (people know the rule applies to the situation) and conditional preference (they prefer to follow it given that they expect enough others to follow it — empirical expectation — and believe enough others expect them to follow it, possibly with sanctions — normative expectation).</details>

2. **Explain-why.** Why does a convention not need normative expectations while a social norm does?
<details>A convention is a coordination equilibrium: given that others conform, conforming is already in your interest, so empirical expectations suffice. A social norm prescribes behavior that runs against your interest in the underlying mixed-motive game (e.g., cooperating in a PD), so only the belief that others expect and may sanction compliance makes conforming preferable.</details>

3. **Apply.** A startup's engineers all write extensive PR descriptions. When a new lead joins and writes terse ones, within a month everyone writes terse ones. What kind of norm was this, and why did it collapse?
<details>A descriptive norm sustained by empirical expectations only (people matched what they saw). When the visible behavior changed, the expectation changed and the practice flipped. If it had been a social norm with normative expectations, people would have expected disapproval for terse PRs and the lead's deviation would more likely have been sanctioned or ignored.</details>

4. **Explain-why.** Why does Bicchieri reject "a taste for fairness" as the explanation of ultimatum-game offers?
<details>Because fair behavior varies dramatically with cues that should not affect a stable preference: anonymity, earned entitlement, moral wiggle room, information about other proposers, cultural context. A norm activated by expectations and situational cues predicts this variance; a fixed taste does not.</details>

5. **Recall/explain.** In norm-based utility, what determines whether a PD becomes a coordination game?
<details>Two things: the players' sensitivity k to the norm and whether they expect the norm to apply. If expectations are live and k is high enough that the deviation penalty exceeds the temptation gain, mutual cooperation becomes an equilibrium alongside mutual defection — a stag hunt. If expectations are absent, the penalty does not apply and the game reverts to the PD.</details>

6. **Spot the misconception.** "Communication increases cooperation because it lets people make binding commitments."
<details>The promises in these experiments are not binding — there is no enforcement. Bicchieri's explanation is that relevant talk creates the empirical and normative expectations a cooperation norm requires: people learn that others intend to cooperate and that others expect them to, which reclassifies the situation. Hence "covenants without swords" still work.</details>

7. **Apply.** An anonymous survey shows that 70% of a team privately think weekend Slack messages are inappropriate, but 80% believe most colleagues think they are fine, and 75% send them. Diagnose and prescribe.
<details>Pluralistic ignorance: private attitudes reject the norm, perceived normative expectations support it, behavior follows the perceived norm and reinforces it. Prescription: publish the survey results credibly to correct normative expectations, and have a visible trendsetter (someone credible with low sensitivity to the norm) stop sending, to shift empirical expectations; consider a simultaneous, coordinated switch to avoid first-mover costs.</details>

8. **Explain-why.** Why are cascades both a mechanism for bad norms and a reason for optimism?
<details>Because cascades aggregate almost no private information — later actors ignore their own signals — a bad norm can spread fast; but by the same token the cascade is fragile, and a credible public signal or a cluster of visible deviators can reverse it quickly.</details>

9. **Recall.** What does the evolutionary model in Ch 6 show, and what does it not show?
<details>It shows that boundedly rational learners in repeated ultimatum games can converge on fair-division norms without any taste for fairness, and that the specific division is path-dependent. It does not show that any actual historical fairness norm arose this way; it is a possibility proof.</details>

10. **Spot the misconception.** "Bicchieri says norms are just equilibria enforced by sanctions."
<details>No. She rejects the pure sanction-equilibrium account: sanctions are one source of normative expectations but not necessary; people conform to social norms without monitoring when they believe others expect it. Equally she rejects the pure internalization account. Norms are conditional preferences keyed to expectations, which is why they are both robust (self-fulfilling) and fragile (belief-dependent).</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- **"A social norm is what most people do."** That is a descriptive norm. For Bicchieri a social norm requires normative expectations and can exist while widely violated, and a widespread behavior can fail to be a norm at all.
- **"Norms are internalized values."** She explicitly separates moral norms (unconditional) from social norms (conditional). Treating a conditional norm as a value leads you to run values workshops when you should be changing expectations.
- **"If people privately disapprove, the norm will die."** Ch 5 is precisely about norms that persist despite private disapproval because of pluralistic ignorance. Disapproval has to become *common knowledge* to matter.
- **"Norm-based utility means people calculate."** Ch 2 argues the opposite: norm-following is automatic and script-driven; the utility function is the analyst's description of the resulting preference, not the agent's deliberation.
- **"Bicchieri denies fairness preferences exist."** Her claim is narrower: you do not *need* them to explain most experimental fairness behavior, and they cannot explain its context-sensitivity. Critics argue some substantive fairness concept still sneaks back in; she allows moral norms as a residual category.
- **"The book is a policy toolkit."** It is the theory. The toolkit — diagnosing norms vs other practices, eliciting empirical and normative expectations, mapping reference networks, finding trendsetters, media and legal levers — is *Norms in the Wild* (2017; chapters: Diagnosing Norms, Measuring Norms, Norm Change, Tools for Change, Trendsetters), which builds on Chapters 1 and 5 and was field-tested in sanitation and child-marriage programs.
