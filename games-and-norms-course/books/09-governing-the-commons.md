# Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action — Elinor Ostrom (Cambridge University Press, 1990; Canto Classics reissue 2015)
Level: Order  ·  Priority: Core
Est. reading time saved: ~10h  ·  Your time with this file: ~35 min

## The book in one sentence
Groups sharing a depletable resource are not doomed to the tragedy of the commons and need neither a Leviathan nor privatization to escape it: under identifiable conditions they craft, monitor and enforce their own rules, and Ostrom uses field cases to extract the eight structural features that separate self-governed commons that last from those that fail.

## The book in one paragraph
Ostrom starts from three models that dominated policy thinking about shared resources in 1990 — Hardin's tragedy of the commons, the prisoner's dilemma, and Olson's logic of collective action — and shows each is a special case misused as a general law. All three assume users cannot communicate, cannot change the rules of the game they are in, and cannot make binding commitments; where those assumptions are false the predictions fail. She then builds an institutional approach: common-pool resources (CPRs) as a distinct type of good, appropriators as boundedly rational actors under uncertainty, and rules at three nested levels (operational, collective-choice, constitutional). Three chapters of cases follow. Long-enduring systems — Swiss alpine pastures, Japanese village commons, Spanish irrigation huertas, Philippine zanjeras — share eight design principles. California's groundwater basins show institutions changing incrementally through litigation, bargaining and new special districts rather than by one constitutional act. Turkish, Sri Lankan and Nova Scotian fisheries and Sri Lankan irrigation schemes show what fragility and failure look like and which principles were missing. The last chapter turns the cases into a framework: the puzzles of institutional supply, credible commitment and mutual monitoring, plus the situational variables that make self-organization more or less likely.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
Earlier books explain why humans cooperate (Origins), what individuals and strategic actors do (Mechanisms), and how norms and conventions stabilize (Skyrms, Bicchieri). Ostrom is the bridge from norms to *institutions*: rules that people deliberately make, monitor, enforce and can change on purpose. Read it with this question: **when a group of mine faces a shared-resource or free-rider problem, which of the eight principles are present, which are absent, and who has the standing to change the rules?** Most design-brief sections (boundaries, monitoring, sanctions, conflict handling, nesting) have roots here.

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

### Ch 1 — Reflections on the commons
Core claims
- Three models are treated as laws of what happens when people share a scarce resource: Hardin's *tragedy of the commons* (1968), the *prisoner's dilemma* (two players, one shot, dominant strategy to defect), and Olson's *logic of collective action* (1965: in a large group no rational individual contributes to a public good, because the contribution is imperceptible and non-contributors cannot be excluded). All three forecast overuse and no cooperation, and they are structurally the same game.
- The models are valid; their assumptions do the work. Users are assumed unable to communicate, change the rules, make binding agreements, or monitor and exclude; there is no shared past or future. Hardin's pasture is *open access with no rules*, which is not what most real commons are. The tragedy is right for open access and wrong as a description of communal property.
- Because the models were read as universal, two prescriptions followed, each claiming to be the *only* way: a central authority (Leviathan) must impose and enforce rules, or the commons must be privatized. Both assume rules designed from outside; both ignore the designer's information and enforcement costs; the Leviathan version silently assumes an accurate, cheap, incorruptible enforcer.
- Empirical alternative: the inshore fishery at Alanya, Turkey (Berkes 1986). Fishers list eligible boats, name the fishing spots, assign them by lot in September and rotate boats through the spots daily, so everyone gets the best sites in season. Violators are displacing the boat entitled to the spot, so monitoring is a by-product of fishing. No state design, no private ownership of the sea.
- She does not claim self-organization always works; the claim is that a third class of solutions exists, succeeds and fails for identifiable reasons, and is ruled out by the dominant prescriptions by assumption rather than evidence.

Key model / example
- Hardin's pasture vs. Alanya: the same situation (many boats, few good spots) is a PD if you assume no communication and no rule-making, and a solvable assignment problem if users can meet, agree and observe each other. The error is treating a model of a situation as if the participants could not change the situation.

So-what for a designer
- Before concluding that a group "has a free-rider problem," ask whether members can talk, see each other's behavior and change their own rules. If any is blocked, that is the problem; the payoff matrix is the symptom.

### Ch 2 — An institutional approach to the study of self-organization and self-governance in CPR situations
Core claims
- A CPR is a resource *system* (fishery, groundwater basin, canal) producing a flow of resource *units* (fish, acre-feet, water turns), from which exclusion is costly. Unlike a pure public good, units are *subtractable*: what I take, you cannot. *Appropriators* withdraw units; *providers* and *producers* supply or maintain the system. This lets her separate *appropriation* problems (who gets how much, when) from *provision* problems (who maintains the stock and infrastructure).
- Appropriators are boundedly rational, fallible, norm-following people under uncertainty about the resource and each other. They learn, use rules of thumb and adopt contingent strategies: "I follow the rule if most others do and I expect to be caught if I do not."
- Rules operate at three nested levels (Kiser and Ostrom 1982). *Operational* rules govern daily appropriation, provision, monitoring and enforcement. *Collective-choice* rules govern how operational rules are made and changed. *Constitutional-choice* rules govern who may make collective-choice rules and how. Changing a rule at one level is an action taken under the rules of the level above; this is what lets her say "the players changed the game" without contradiction.
- Rules-in-use matter, not rules-in-form. A written rule nobody monitors is not part of the institution.
- Three puzzles any theory of self-organization must solve: (1) *institutional supply* — new rules are a public good, so who supplies them? (2) *credible commitment* — why keep a rule when defecting pays and no state enforces? (3) *mutual monitoring* — monitoring is costly and a public good, so why does anyone monitor? Standard theory predicts none happens; the cases show all three.

Key model / example
- Contingent strategy resolves commitment: it is conditional on believing others comply and that non-compliance is likely detected. Monitoring and commitment are therefore jointly determined.

So-what for a designer
- Diagnose at all three levels. Most proposed fixes are operational (a new rule); most failures are at the collective-choice level (nobody affected can legitimately change the rule) or constitutional level (the group has no recognized standing to govern itself).
- Design monitoring and commitment together. A rule that cannot be observed will not be kept, whatever the initial buy-in.

### Ch 3 — Analyzing long-enduring, self-organized, and self-governed CPRs
Ostrom picks cases that persisted for centuries, on the argument that survival across generations under changing conditions is the closest a field study gets to a robustness test.

Cases
- **Törbel, Switzerland** (Netting's ethnography). A village of a few hundred governing alpine summer pastures, forests and waste as communal property since at least the thirteenth century, with cultivated fields privately owned. A 1517 rule forbids sending more cows to the alp than one can winter, capping appropriation in proportion to private capacity; an elected official fines violators and keeps part of the fine; the users' association elects officials, votes on rules and organizes maintenance labor; the cattle count is public. Netting's argument, which Ostrom adopts: communal tenure is rational for low-value, variable-yield land where fencing and small parcels would be costly; private tenure for high-value, predictable fields.
- **Japanese villages: Hirano, Nagaike, Yamanoka** (McKean). Villages held mountain forest and grassland as commons for fuel, thatch, fodder and fertilizer. Rules fix opening dates, permitted tools, quantities per household and required labor; village assemblies revise them; appointed monitors patrol; sanctions escalate from small fines paid in sake to confiscation of tools and, at the extreme, ostracism. McKean found few open conflicts or collapses over centuries.
- **Huerta irrigation in Spain: Valencia, Murcia and Orihuela, Alicante** (Maass and Anderson; Glick). Farmer-controlled irrigation communities, some dating to the Moorish period. Valencia allocates water by turn along each canal in proportion to land, with the rule that one takes water when the turn comes but no more than needed; canal guards monitor; the Tribunal de las Aguas has met weekly in public for centuries with elected syndics as judges and modest fines. Alicante separates water rights from land and auctions them, an institution that works because the community itself sells the rights and reinvests. The huertas show variation *within* success: different allocation rules fit different scarcity patterns.
- **Zanjera irrigation communities, Ilocos Norte, Philippines** (Siy 1982). Tenant farmers build and maintain dam and canals in exchange for shares (*atar*) of irrigated land; each share carries proportional water, proportional labor obligation and a vote. Each member's parcels are spread from head to tail of the system so everyone shares tail-end shortage risk. Labor obligations run to dozens of days a year, attendance is recorded, fines for absence are graduated, officers are elected annually.

The eight design principles (Table 3.1; my wording, tracking hers closely)
1. **Clearly defined boundaries.** The individuals or households with rights to withdraw units from the CPR, and the boundaries of the CPR itself, are clearly defined.
2. **Congruence between appropriation and provision rules and local conditions.** Appropriation rules restricting time, place, technology and/or quantity are related to local conditions and to provision rules requiring labor, materials and/or money.
3. **Collective-choice arrangements.** Most individuals affected by operational rules can participate in modifying them.
4. **Monitoring.** Monitors who actively audit CPR conditions and appropriator behavior are accountable to the appropriators or are the appropriators.
5. **Graduated sanctions.** Rule violators are likely to receive graduated sanctions, depending on the seriousness and context of the offense, from other appropriators, from officials accountable to them, or both.
6. **Conflict-resolution mechanisms.** Appropriators and officials have rapid access to low-cost local arenas to resolve conflicts among appropriators or between appropriators and officials.
7. **Minimal recognition of rights to organize.** Appropriators' rights to devise their own institutions are not challenged by external governmental authorities.
8. **Nested enterprises** (for CPRs within larger systems). Appropriation, provision, monitoring, enforcement, conflict resolution and governance are organized in multiple layers of nested enterprises.

Her reasoning behind the principles matters more than the list:
- Boundaries (1) are necessary, not sufficient: without them outsiders capture the gains of any restraint. Congruence (2) is why rules are accepted; a rule copied from elsewhere that does not fit is seen as illegitimate. Collective choice (3) keeps rules congruent as conditions change; people who can change rules understand and follow them.
- Monitoring (4) and graduated sanctions (5) are her direct attack on the theoretical prediction. Monitoring is near-universal in the durable cases, done by users or agents they appoint, often as a by-product of appropriation (the boat waiting for its spot, the farmer next in turn). First-offense sanctions are surprisingly small. Her explanation: sanctions exist to sustain contingent quasi-voluntary compliance, not to make every defection unprofitable. A small first fine tells the violator they were seen and tells everyone else the rule is alive; severe escalation is reserved for repeat or flagrant violators. Monitoring solves commitment and vice versa.
- Conflict resolution (6) exists because rules are ambiguous at the margins and honest disagreement otherwise becomes a stream of unpunished violations. External recognition (7) separates a working institution from an informal arrangement an agency can override on the first complaint. Nesting (8) handles scale: zanjera federations and huerta canal hierarchies solve problems at the smallest level that can, with larger units handling inter-unit conflict and shared infrastructure.

So-what for a designer
- The principles are a diagnostic checklist, not a blueprint. The specific rules differ wildly; what is shared is the structure that keeps rules legitimate, observable and revisable.
- The graduated-sanctions insight is the one organizations most often violate: either no sanction (norm decays) or a nuclear first sanction (violations get hidden, monitors stop reporting).

### Ch 4 — Analyzing institutional change
Core claims
- Institutions are rarely created in one constitutional moment; they evolve through incremental, sequential, self-transforming change, each step lowering the cost of the next. The case is the groundwater basins under the Los Angeles area (Raymond, West and Central Basins), from Ostrom's own 1960s doctoral research.
- Starting point: a *pumping race*. California law left rights ambiguous between overlying landowners and appropriators; with rising demand and saltwater intrusion, every pumper's dominant strategy was to pump before others did. A real PD with a real tragedy under way.
- The path out, told as a sequence of games. *Litigation*: Raymond Basin pumpers sued, obtained a state-referee investigation (the state bore much of the information cost) and negotiated a stipulated judgment defining shares and cutting extraction to safe yield, with a court-appointed watermaster to monitor. West Basin followed with a longer, costlier suit; Central Basin used a lighter "mutual prescription" formula. *Entrepreneurship*: water associations formed to gather data, negotiate and lobby. *Polycentric public enterprise*: special districts were created under enabling legislation to import water, build seawater barriers and tax pumping to fund replenishment. The result is a layered system of private pumpers, courts, a watermaster, districts and the state agency.
- *Institutional supply* is solved here not by a heroic collective act but by low-cost external arenas (courts, enabling law) that cut transformation costs; leadership by large pumpers with the most to lose; incremental rule changes that reshaped payoffs and information for the next round; and a visibly collapsing resource that raised expected benefits.

Key model / example
- The litigation game turned "pump before others do" into "negotiate shares under a judge who can impose something worse." Participants used the state as a *facilitator* (information, credible threat, legal standing), not as a Leviathan writing the rules.

So-what for a designer
- Do not wait for the whole institution; find the first cheap change that produces information and standing (a shared dashboard, a recognized forum) and let it lower the cost of the next.
- External authority is most useful as backstop and information supplier, least useful as rule-writer: "court plus enabling legislation," not "central plan."

### Ch 5 — Analyzing institutional failures and fragilities
Each failure is paired with the missing principles.
- **Turkish inshore fisheries (Bodrum; Bay of Izmir).** Unlike Alanya, Bodrum was flooded by new entrants (subsidized trawlers, migrants) with no way to define a boundary; conflict between artisanal and industrial fishers went unresolved and state licensing was unenforced. Izmir added urban pollution. Missing: 1, 3, 6, partly 7.
- **A California basin that did not organize (Mojave).** Same legal environment as Ch 4, but a far larger, more heterogeneous group with poorer shared information and greater hydrological uncertainty; adjudication failed at the time. Supply of institutions is sensitive to size, heterogeneity and information, not just legal opportunity.
- **Sri Lankan fishery (Mawelle).** A local rotation for beach-seine launches worked while nets were few; as nets multiplied (outside political patrons backing entrants), the rotation broke and national attempts to limit nets were undermined by political intervention. Missing: enforceable boundary, external recognition.
- **Sri Lankan irrigation projects (Kirindi Oya and similar).** Large, externally designed and funded schemes: settlers had no role in rule-making, government officers monitored without accountability to farmers, head-end farmers took water and tail-enders went short, maintenance decayed. Contrast Gal Oya, where an outside intervention deliberately built farmer organizations (Uphoff) and reversed the pattern. Missing: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.
- **Nova Scotia inshore fisheries (Port Lameron Harbour).** Long-standing informal rules on who fishes where with what gear were unrecognized by federal licensing; they survived only as long as the state ignored them and were fragile against licensed newcomers. Missing: 7 (and by implication 8).

Core claim: failure is not a mystery needing a new theory; it is the absence of a specific subset of principles, most often boundaries, congruence and external recognition. Central governments here were often a cause of fragility: licensing without local knowledge, overriding local rules, or supplying monitors unaccountable to users.

So-what for a designer
- The pattern "outside sponsor sets up the group, appoints the monitor, and lets no member change the rules" recurs in companies (centrally imposed processes with no team ownership) and platforms (staff-run moderation unaccountable to the community). Kirindi Oya is that pattern.
- Growth is the classic killer: a turn-taking norm that works for 20 breaks at 60 unless boundaries or nesting are introduced.

### Ch 6 — A framework for analysis of self-organizing and self-governing CPRs
Core claims
- The puzzles revisited. *Supply*: rules get supplied incrementally when expected benefits of changing them exceed expected costs for enough affected people, with external arenas lowering transformation costs. *Commitment*: appropriators adopt contingent quasi-voluntary compliance when they believe most others comply, rules are legitimate and violations are likely caught. *Monitoring*: cheap when it is a by-product of use, monitors benefit directly from the information, and small sanctions plus reputation make enforcement worthwhile.
- Framework of institutional choice: an individual supports a rule change depending on expected benefits (state of the resource, information flow, current rules, perceived alternatives), expected costs (transformation plus ongoing monitoring and enforcement), and internal norms and discount rates. Discount rates matter enormously: users who expect to be there in 30 years weigh the future; migrants, absentee owners and politically insecure users discount it heavily.
- Situational variables that raise the odds of self-organization (offered tentatively): a small, stable group; shared understanding of the resource and of rule effects; low discount rates; reasonably homogeneous interests; cheap information on resource condition; prior organizing experience; external authorities that permit rather than obstruct.
- Challenge to social science: stop using the PD as a stand-in for reality; take seriously that participants choose rules, that levels of choice matter, and that field cases are data. The proposed comparative program became the Institutional Analysis and Development (IAD) framework and later the social-ecological systems framework.

So-what for a designer
- The cost-benefit framing tells you where to intervene: lower the cost of rule change (recognized forums, cheap information), raise the perceived benefit (make the state of the shared resource visible), lengthen horizons (tenure, stakes, stable membership).

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. **The tragedy of the commons is a tragedy of open access, not of shared property.** Hardin, the PD and Olson assume no communication, no rule-making, no monitoring; where those exist the predictions fail, and the models describe a situation participants can change.
2. **A CPR has subtractable units and costly exclusion**, separating appropriation problems (who takes how much) from provision problems (who maintains). Real free-rider problems mix both and need rules for both.
3. **Rules live at three levels** — operational, collective-choice, constitutional — and durable self-governance requires that those bound by operational rules have a legitimate route to change them, recognized from outside.
4. **The eight design principles** are the structural regularities of durable self-governed commons; they are diagnostic conditions, not rules to install.
5. **Monitoring and commitment are one problem.** Compliance is contingent on expected compliance and expected detection; cheap by-product monitoring plus small first sanctions keep the shared belief that the rule is alive.
6. **Graduated sanctions are a feature.** Small first fines preserve information flow and the norm; harsh escalation is held for repeat offenders.
7. **Institutions are supplied incrementally.** The California basins escaped a real tragedy through small changes, each lowering the cost of the next, with external authority as facilitator rather than designer.
8. **Failures have identifiable missing principles**, most often boundaries, congruence and external recognition; central governments are frequently the cause of fragility rather than its cure.
9. **Situational variables predict take-off**: small stable groups, shared information, low discount rates, prior organizing experience, homogeneous interests, non-obstructive authorities.
10. **Neither markets nor states are the default answer**; polycentric arrangements with overlapping authorities are the empirical norm in the durable cases.

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **Common-pool resource (CPR)** — subtractable units, costly exclusion — bites when a "public good" (a codebase, an on-call rota, a shared budget) is actually depletable.
- **Appropriation vs. provision problem** — allocating flow vs. maintaining stock — assignment and timing vs. contribution and free-riding on upkeep.
- **Operational / collective-choice / constitutional rules** — daily rules / rules for changing rules / rules for who may change those — most stalled reforms fail at the middle level.
- **Rules-in-use vs. rules-in-form** — what is followed vs. what is written — audit the former.
- **Contingent strategy / quasi-voluntary compliance** — comply if most others do and violators are likely caught — commitment is conditional and fed by visible compliance.
- **Graduated sanctions** — small first penalties escalating with repetition and seriousness — protect the information flow about violations.
- **Institutional supply** — new rules are a public good nobody has individual incentive to supply — solved by cheap arenas, high-stakes leaders, incremental steps.
- **Polycentricity / nested enterprises** — overlapping decision centers at several scales — the alternative to one center and to pure decentralization.
- **Discount rate** — how much users weigh the resource's future — the fastest way to break a commons is to make its users transient.
- **Design principle** — an essential condition that helps account for durability — something to check for, not to copy.

## Evidence strength & limits
- **Shown vs. argued.** The book shows, via well-documented secondary cases and Ostrom's own primary research on the California basins, that durable self-organized CPR institutions exist and share structural features. It *argues*, but cannot prove from these cases, that the eight principles cause durability: cases were selected on the dependent variable, the sample is small, and failures are compared informally.
- **Later systematic evidence (verified via search).** Cox, Arnold and Villamayor-Tomás (2010, *Ecology and Society* 15(4): 38) reviewed 91 empirical studies and found the principles well supported: where a study assessed a principle, its presence was mostly associated with success and its absence with failure. They reformulated three: 1A user boundaries / 1B resource boundaries; 2A congruence with local conditions / 2B proportionality of benefits to costs; 4A monitoring users / 4B monitoring the resource. That version is now standard. Their caveats: publication bias toward fitting cases; loose, qualitative coding; and the principles describe structure, not the processes (trust, legitimacy, transparency) that make structure work.
- **Experimental evidence (background knowledge).** Ostrom, Gardner and Walker's *Rules, Games, and Common-Pool Resources* (1994) and later lab work found that face-to-face communication sharply raises cooperation in CPR games and that subjects pay to sanction over-appropriators; the communication effect is among the most robust findings in experimental economics (see 06-behavioral-game-theory). Field experiments (Cardenas and colleagues, Colombia, 2000s) found weakly enforced external regulation can *crowd out* communication-driven cooperation, consistent with the book.
- **Critiques worth knowing.**
  - *Variable proliferation.* Agrawal (2001, *World Development*) noted that Ostrom, Wade and Baland and Platteau together list around 30 or more success conditions; without a theory of which matter when, the lists risk being unfalsifiable. Ostrom's diagnostic and social-ecological systems frameworks (2007, 2009) partly respond.
  - *Panacea and scale.* Ostrom herself (2007, PNAS) warned against treating the principles, or community management generally, as a universal fix. Critics note the principles come from small, place-based, low-tech commons and may transfer poorly to large-scale, global or digital commons; work on knowledge commons (Hess and Ostrom), open source and Wikipedia found partial fit, with principles 1 and 2 needing reinterpretation where units are non-subtractable.
  - *Power and heterogeneity.* Political ecologists (Agrawal, Cleaver, Mosse) argue the book underplays power: institutions can be durable because they entrench elites (the huerta and zanjera cases have internal inequalities), and "community" is more contested than the cases suggest. Cleaver's "institutional bricolage" holds that rules are assembled from existing social practice rather than designed.
  - *Static principles.* They describe steady state and say little about adaptation or resource regime shifts; resilience scholars (Anderies, Janssen and Ostrom 2004) tried to add this.
- **Robust vs. contested.** Robust: open-access assumptions drive the tragedy prediction; communication and self-monitoring raise cooperation; boundaries and external recognition matter; graduated sanctions are widespread in durable systems. Contested: necessity or sufficiency of any single principle, applicability to large or digital commons, and the treatment of power.

## Design implications for cooperation in real groups
- Before treating a problem as free-riding, check whether members can communicate, observe each other and change the rules; fix those channels first. [E]
- Define who is in the group that shares the resource and where the resource's edge is; ambiguous membership is the commonest structural cause of decay. [E]
- Match contribution obligations to benefits; people accept heavy obligations when the ratio is visibly fair and reject light ones that seem arbitrary. [E]
- Give those bound by an operational rule a legitimate, low-cost way to change it; imposed rules are followed only while enforced. [E]
- Make monitoring a by-product of participation (visible queues, public logs, turn-taking that exposes cheaters) rather than a separate policing function. [E]
- Use graduated sanctions: small, quick, public first response; escalation for repeat or flagrant violations; never a nuclear first strike. [E]
- Provide a cheap, fast, local forum for disputes about what a rule means. [E]
- Secure explicit recognition from the surrounding authority (management, platform owner, funder) that the group may govern this domain; otherwise every local sanction can be appealed over its head. [E]
- When the group outgrows by-product monitoring, nest: subgroups handle local problems, a higher layer handles inter-subgroup conflict and shared infrastructure. [E]
- Lengthen members' horizons (stable membership, tenure, stakes in future outcomes); turnover raises discount rates and undermines every other principle. [H]
- Prefer incremental change that produces information and standing over grand redesign; use external authority as backstop and data source, not rule author. [H]
- Treat the principles as a diagnostic checklist, not a template; rules must be locally crafted. [V]
- Prefer polycentric arrangements over a single decision center when problems occur at several scales. [V]

## Retrieval practice
1. (Recall) Name the three models Ostrom argues are misused, and the shared assumption that makes all three predict failure.
<details>Tragedy of the commons (Hardin), prisoner's dilemma, logic of collective action (Olson). Shared assumption: participants cannot communicate, cannot make binding agreements and cannot change the rules, so they are trapped in a one-shot dominant-strategy structure.</details>

2. (Explain-why) Why are first-offense sanctions in durable systems small rather than large enough to make cheating unprofitable?
<details>Compliance is contingent and quasi-voluntary. A first sanction signals to the violator that they were seen and to everyone else that the rule is still enforced, preserving the expectation that most others comply. Harsh first sanctions drive violations and reports underground and erode legitimacy; escalation is reserved for repeat offenders.</details>

3. (Recall) Distinguish operational, collective-choice and constitutional rules with one example each.
<details>Operational: the daily rotation of fishing spots. Collective-choice: the cooperative meeting that can change the rotation. Constitutional: the law and state tolerance that give the cooperative standing to bind its members.</details>

4. (Apply) An engineering org shares a CI cluster; queues are long, some teams submit huge jobs, and a platform team wants to impose per-team quotas. Diagnose with the principles.
<details>Check 1 (are cluster users defined?), 2 (are quotas proportional to need or contribution, or arbitrary?), 3 (can affected teams change the quota rule?), 4 (is usage visible to all teams as a by-product of the queue?), 5 (is the first violation a visible warning, not a lockout?), 6 (where do teams argue about whether a job was necessary?), 7 (has leadership recognized the teams' right to set rules?), 8 (is the org big enough to need per-department sub-allocation plus an inter-department layer?). Prediction: an imposed quota without 3 and 4 gets gamed; a visible queue plus a team forum likely outperforms it.</details>

5. (Spot the misconception) "Ostrom showed that communities always manage commons better than governments or markets."
<details>Wrong. She showed self-governance is a third option that works under identifiable conditions and fails for identifiable reasons, and that durable cases usually combine local rules with external backstops (courts, enabling law). She explicitly warned against panaceas.</details>

6. (Explain-why) How did the California pumpers escape a real pumping race without a central authority writing the rules?
<details>Sequential incremental change: a lawsuit using a state referee to produce shared hydrological data, a negotiated stipulated judgment defining shares, a court-appointed watermaster, water associations as entrepreneurs, and special districts under enabling legislation to import water and tax pumping. The state lowered information and enforcement costs; the pumpers designed the rules.</details>

7. (Apply) An online community's moderation is run entirely by platform staff who are not members; complaints about inconsistent bans are rising. Which principles are missing, and which failure case does it resemble?
<details>Missing 3 (members cannot change rules), 4 (monitors unaccountable to users), 5 (bans not graduated), 6 (no cheap dispute forum). It resembles the Sri Lankan irrigation projects, where officers monitored without accountability to farmers and rules were set from outside.</details>

8. (Recall) What did Cox, Arnold and Villamayor-Tomás (2010) conclude and change?
<details>Reviewing 91 studies, they found the principles well supported and split boundaries into user and resource boundaries, congruence into local fit and cost-benefit proportionality, and monitoring into monitoring users and monitoring the resource. They flagged that the principles capture structure, not processes like trust and legitimacy.</details>

9. (Explain-why) Why does Ostrom treat monitoring and commitment as one puzzle?
<details>A rational appropriator's commitment is contingent on believing others comply and that violations are likely detected; monitoring generates that belief. Conversely, people monitor because they benefit from knowing whether their own contingent compliance is warranted. Neither is explicable alone.</details>

10. (Spot the misconception) "The eight design principles tell you which rules to adopt."
<details>They specify no rules. Törbel, the Japanese villages, Valencia, Alicante and the zanjeras all have different rules. The principles specify structural conditions — who is in, whether rules fit, who can change them, who watches, how sanctions escalate, where disputes go, whether outsiders recognize the group, how scale is handled — under which locally crafted rules tend to endure.</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- **Reading it as anti-state or anti-market.** Her durable cases lean on courts, enabling legislation and recognized property rights; the claim is that neither central control nor privatization is the *only* solution, not that either is always wrong.
- **Treating the principles as a recipe.** They are conditions found in durable institutions, not installable features; she said rules must be locally crafted and the list is provisional.
- **Assuming the successes are egalitarian.** Several durable systems have hereditary shares, unequal holdings and exclusion of outsiders; durability and fairness are different criteria.
- **Extending the results to any shared thing.** The book is about subtractable resources with costly exclusion; knowledge commons, open source and attention economies differ on subtractability and need reinterpretation.
- **Thinking the book refutes the prisoner's dilemma.** It refutes the PD as a law of nature. The PD remains a valid model of open access; participants can, and do, move themselves into a different game.
