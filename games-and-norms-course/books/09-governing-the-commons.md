# Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action — Elinor Ostrom (Cambridge University Press, 1990; Canto Classics reissue 2015)
Level: Order  ·  Priority: Core
Est. reading time saved: ~10h  ·  Your time with this file: ~40 min

## The book in one sentence
Groups that share a depletable resource are not doomed to the tragedy of the commons and do not need either a Leviathan or privatization to escape it: given the right conditions they craft, monitor and enforce their own rules, and Ostrom uses field cases to extract the eight structural features that separate self-governed commons that last from those that fail.

## The book in one paragraph
Ostrom starts from three models that dominated policy thinking about shared resources in 1990 — Hardin's tragedy of the commons, the prisoner's dilemma, and Olson's logic of collective action — and shows that each is a special case being misused as a general law. All three assume users cannot communicate, cannot change the rules of the game they are in, and cannot make binding commitments; when those assumptions are false, the predictions fail. She then builds an institutional approach: common-pool resources (CPRs) as a distinct type of good, appropriators as boundedly rational actors under uncertainty, and rules operating at three nested levels (operational, collective-choice, constitutional). Three chapters of cases follow. Long-enduring systems — Swiss alpine pastures, Japanese village commons, Spanish irrigation huertas, Philippine zanjeras — share eight design principles. California's groundwater basins show how institutions change incrementally through litigation, bargaining and the creation of new special districts, rather than by a single constitutional act. Turkish fisheries, Sri Lankan fisheries and irrigation schemes, and Nova Scotia fisheries show what fragility and failure look like and which principles were missing. The final chapter turns the cases into a framework: the puzzles of institutional supply, credible commitment and mutual monitoring, plus the situational variables that make self-organization more or less likely. The book's target is not just resource policy; it is any analysis that reads the outcome of a game off its payoff matrix without asking who can rewrite the matrix.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
The earlier books explain why humans cooperate (Origins), what individuals and strategic actors do (Mechanisms), and how norms and conventions stabilize (Skyrms, Bicchieri). Ostrom is the curriculum's bridge from norms to *institutions*: rules that people deliberately make, monitor and enforce, and that can be changed on purpose. Read it with this question: **when a group of mine faces a shared-resource or free-rider problem, which of the eight principles are present, which are absent, and who has the standing to change the rules?** Almost every design section in the capstone brief (boundaries, monitoring, sanctions, conflict handling, nesting) has its roots here.

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

### Ch 1 — Reflections on the commons
Core claims
- Three models have been treated as general laws of what happens when people share a scarce resource: Hardin's *tragedy of the commons* (1968), the *prisoner's dilemma* (formalized as a two-person, one-shot game with a dominant strategy to defect), and Olson's *logic of collective action* (1965; in a large group, rational individuals will not contribute to a public good because their contribution is imperceptible and non-contribution cannot be excluded from the benefit). All three deliver the same forecast — the resource is overused and cooperation does not happen — and they are structurally the same game.
- The models are internally valid but their assumptions are what do the work: users cannot communicate, cannot change the rules, cannot enter binding agreements, have no shared past or future, and there is no way to exclude or monitor. Hardin's pasture is a *no-rules, open-access* resource, which is not what most real commons are. The tragedy is correct for open access and wrong as a description of communal property with rules.
- Because the models were read as universal, two policy prescriptions followed and each claimed to be the *only* way: (a) a central authority (Leviathan) must impose rules and enforce them, and (b) the commons must be privatized into individual property rights. Both prescriptions assume the state or the market designs rules from outside; both ignore the information and enforcement costs the outside designer faces; and the Leviathan version silently assumes an accurate, cheap, incorruptible enforcer.
- Empirical alternative: the inshore fishery at Alanya, Turkey (documented by Berkes, 1986), where fishers devised a rotation system that assigns named fishing spots by lot and rotates boats daily through the spots so that everyone gets access to the best sites in season; fishers monitor each other because a violator is displacing the boat entitled to the spot. No state, no private ownership of the sea.
- Ostrom is careful: she does not claim that self-organization always works. Her claim is that a third class of solutions exists, that it succeeds and fails for identifiable reasons, and that the two dominant prescriptions rule it out by assumption rather than by evidence.

Key model / example
- Hardin's pasture vs. Alanya. The comparison is the book's whole method in miniature: the same "game" (many boats, limited good spots) is a PD if you assume no communication and no rule-making, and a solvable assignment problem if the users can meet, agree and observe each other.
- Metaphorical misuse: the PD is a fine model of a situation; the error is treating a model of a situation as if the situation could not be changed by the people in it. She calls the levels of rules that the players can alter the difference between being *in* a game and being able to *change* the game.

So-what for a designer
- Before you conclude that a group "has a free-rider problem," ask whether the group can talk, can see each other's behavior, and can change its own rules. If any of those is blocked, that is the problem to fix; the payoff matrix is a symptom.
- The Leviathan and privatization prescriptions map onto "add a manager/owner" and "split the shared thing into individually owned pieces." Both are sometimes right; neither is automatically right, and both have hidden costs of information and enforcement that Ostrom's models make visible.

### Ch 2 — An institutional approach to the study of self-organization and self-governance in CPR situations
Core claims
- A common-pool resource is a resource system (a fishery, a groundwater basin, an irrigation canal) that produces a flow of resource units (fish, acre-feet, water turns) and that is costly to exclude people from. Unlike a pure public good, the units are *subtractable*: what I take, you cannot. She separates the *resource system* (jointly used, must be maintained) from the *resource units* (individually taken). *Appropriators* withdraw units; *providers* and *producers* supply or maintain the system. This distinction is what lets her separate appropriation problems (who gets how much) from provision problems (who maintains the stock and infrastructure).
- Appropriators are not the hyper-rational players of the models but boundedly rational, fallible, norm-following people acting under uncertainty about the resource and about each other. They learn, use rules of thumb and adopt contingent strategies ("I will follow the rule if most others do and I expect to be caught if I do not").
- Rules operate at three nested levels. *Operational* rules govern day-to-day appropriation, provision, monitoring and enforcement. *Collective-choice* rules govern how operational rules get made and changed (who participates, by what procedure). *Constitutional-choice* rules govern who is eligible to make collective-choice rules and how. Changing a rule at one level is an action taken under the rules of the level above. This (from Kiser and Ostrom 1982) is the analytical core that lets her say "the players changed the game" without contradiction.
- Rules-in-use are what matter, not rules-in-form. A rule on paper that no one monitors is not part of the institution.
- Three puzzles that any theory of self-organization must solve: (1) *institutional supply* — new rules are themselves a public good, so who supplies them and why do they not free-ride on the supplying? (2) *credible commitment* — why would a rational appropriator keep to a rule when defecting pays and the state is not enforcing? (3) *mutual monitoring* — monitoring is costly and is itself a public good, so why does anyone monitor? Standard theory predicts none of the three happens; the cases show all three happening.

Key model / example
- The nested-levels framework, illustrated by how a fishing rotation (operational) is set by a fishers' cooperative meeting (collective choice) whose legal standing to meet and bind members rests on cooperative law and non-interference by the state (constitutional).
- Contingent strategy as the resolution to the commitment puzzle: commitment is not unconditional; it is conditional on the belief that others comply and that non-compliance is likely to be detected. That is why monitoring and commitment are jointly determined, not two separate problems.

So-what for a designer
- Diagnose at all three levels. Most fixes proposed for teams and communities are operational (a new rule); most failures are at the collective-choice level (nobody affected has a legitimate way to change the rule) or the constitutional level (the group has no recognized standing to govern itself).
- Design monitoring and commitment together. A rule that cannot be observed will not be kept, no matter how strong the initial buy-in.

### Ch 3 — Analyzing long-enduring, self-organized, and self-governed CPRs
Ostrom chooses cases that have persisted for centuries, on the argument that survival over many generations under changing conditions is the closest a field study gets to a robustness test. Every case is a real CPR with subtractable units, a bounded group and locally crafted rules.

Cases
- **Törbel, Switzerland** (Netting's ethnography, 1970s–80s). A village of a few hundred people governing alpine summer pastures, forests and waste land as communal property since at least the thirteenth century, while cultivated fields are privately owned. A 1517 rule that no citizen may send more cows to the alp than he can winter (the "wintering rule") caps appropriation in proportion to private capacity; violators are fined by an elected official who keeps part of the fine; the association of users elects its officials, votes on rules and organizes required maintenance labor, with the cattle count recorded and published. Netting's argument, which Ostrom adopts: the choice between communal and private tenure is rational — communal for low-value, variable-yield land where boundaries and small parcels would be costly; private for high-value, predictable-yield fields.
- **Japanese villages: Hirano, Nagaike, Yamanoka** (McKean's work). Hundreds of villages held mountain forest and grassland as *iriai* commons for fuel, thatch, fodder and fertilizer. Rules specify opening dates, permitted tools, permitted quantities per household, and required labor; village assemblies write and revise the rules; monitors ("detectives") patrol the commons, and sanctions escalate from small fines paid in sake to confiscation of tools and, at the extreme, ostracism. McKean found remarkably few instances of open conflict or ecological collapse over centuries.
- **Huerta irrigation in Spain: Valencia, Murcia and Orihuela, Alicante** (Maass and Anderson, and Glick's historical work). Farmer-controlled irrigation communities dating in some cases to the Moorish period. Valencia's rules allocate water by turn along each canal in proportion to land, with a rule that a farmer may take water when his turn comes but no more than he needs, monitored by canal guards; the Tribunal de las Aguas has met weekly in public in front of the cathedral for centuries to hear violations, with elected syndics as judges and modest fines. Alicante is the contrasting variant: water rights were separated from land and are auctioned, an institution that works because the irrigation community itself sells the water rights and reinvests. Ostrom uses the huertas to show variation *within* success: different allocation rules fit different scarcity and rainfall patterns.
- **Zanjera irrigation communities in Ilocos Norte, Philippines** (Siy's 1982 study). Tenant farmers who build and maintain a dam and canal system in exchange for shares (*atar*) of the irrigated land; each share carries a proportional share of water, a proportional share of the maintenance labor, and a vote. The land parcels for each member are distributed across the head and tail of the system, so that everyone shares the risk of shortage at the tail. Labor obligations are high (dozens of days a year), attendance is recorded, fines for absence are graduated, and officers are elected annually.

The eight design principles (Table 3.1 in the book; stated here in my own words but tracking her phrasing closely)
1. **Clearly defined boundaries.** Both the set of individuals or households with rights to withdraw resource units from the CPR *and* the boundaries of the CPR itself are clearly defined.
2. **Congruence between appropriation and provision rules and local conditions.** Appropriation rules that restrict time, place, technology and/or quantity of units are matched to local conditions, and are related to provision rules requiring labor, materials and/or money. (Later read as two things: rules fit the local ecology, and benefits are proportional to contributions.)
3. **Collective-choice arrangements.** Most individuals affected by the operational rules can participate in modifying them.
4. **Monitoring.** Monitors who actively audit the condition of the resource and the behavior of appropriators are accountable to the appropriators or are the appropriators themselves.
5. **Graduated sanctions.** Appropriators who violate operational rules are likely to receive graduated sanctions — depending on the seriousness and context of the offense — from other appropriators, from officials accountable to them, or both.
6. **Conflict-resolution mechanisms.** Appropriators and their officials have rapid access to low-cost local arenas to resolve conflicts among appropriators or between appropriators and officials.
7. **Minimal recognition of rights to organize.** The rights of appropriators to devise their own institutions are not challenged by external governmental authorities.
8. **Nested enterprises** (for CPRs that are part of larger systems). Appropriation, provision, monitoring, enforcement, conflict resolution and governance activities are organized in multiple layers of nested enterprises.

Ostrom's own reasoning behind the principles is more important than the list:
- Boundaries (1) are necessary but obviously not sufficient: without them there is nothing to organize and outsiders capture the gains of any restraint.
- Congruence (2) is why rules are accepted: a rule copied from elsewhere that does not fit local conditions will be seen as illegitimate and will not be followed.
- Collective choice (3) is how rules stay congruent as conditions change; people who can change rules also understand them and are more willing to follow them.
- Monitoring (4) and graduated sanctions (5) are where she directly attacks the theoretical prediction. She finds *near-universal* monitoring in the durable cases, done by the users or by agents they appoint, often as a by-product of appropriation (the boat waiting for its spot, the farmer next in turn). And she finds that first-offense sanctions are surprisingly small. Her explanation: sanctions are not there to make defection unprofitable on every occasion; they are there to sustain the contingent strategy of quasi-voluntary compliance. A small first fine tells the violator that they were seen and tells everyone else that the rule is still in force. Severe, escalating sanctions are reserved for repeated or flagrant violators, protecting the reputation system from collapse. Monitoring is thus a solution to the commitment problem and vice versa.
- Conflict resolution (6) exists because rules are always ambiguous at the margins and because honest disagreement about what a rule means will otherwise become a stream of unpunished violations.
- External recognition (7) is what separates a working local institution from an informal arrangement that a government agency can override on the first complaint; where external authorities treat local rules as illegitimate, monitoring and sanctioning by locals lose their teeth.
- Nesting (8) handles scale: the zanjera federations and the huerta canal hierarchies handle problems at the smallest level that can handle them, and larger units deal with inter-unit conflicts and shared infrastructure.

So-what for a designer
- The principles are a *diagnostic checklist*, not a blueprint. Ostrom stresses that the specific rules differ wildly across cases; what is shared is the structure that keeps rules legitimate, observable and revisable.
- The graduated-sanctions insight is the one most often violated in organizations: either no sanction (norm decays) or a nuclear first sanction (people hide violations, monitors stop reporting).

### Ch 4 — Analyzing institutional change
Core claims
- Institutions are not usually created in one constitutional moment; they evolve through incremental, sequential, self-transforming change, where each step lowers the cost of the next. The case is the groundwater basins beneath Los Angeles (Raymond Basin, West Basin, Central Basin), which Ostrom studied in her own doctoral research in the 1960s.
- Starting position: a *pumping race*. Water rights under California law were ambiguous between overlying landowners and appropriators; with no defined rights and rising demand, every pumper's dominant strategy was to pump as fast as possible before saltwater intrusion and falling water tables ruined the basin. This is a real-world PD with a genuine tragedy under way.
- Path out, told as a sequence of games: (1) *litigation* — pumpers in Raymond Basin filed a suit, obtained a state-referee investigation (the reference procedure meant the state paid much of the information cost), and negotiated a stipulated judgment that defined each party's share and cut total extraction to safe yield; the court appointed a watermaster to monitor. West Basin followed with a longer and costlier suit; Central Basin used a lighter "mutual prescription" formula. (2) *entrepreneurship* — water associations formed to gather data, negotiate and press for legislation. (3) *polycentric public enterprise* — new special districts (replenishment district, water-quality agencies) were created under enabling state legislation to import water, build seawater barriers and tax pumping to pay for replenishment. (4) A layered system now exists in which private pumpers, courts, a watermaster, replenishment districts and the state water agency each have a role.
- Analysis of *institutional supply*: the puzzle from Ch 2 is solved here not by a heroic collective act but by (a) the availability of low-cost external arenas (courts and enabling legislation) that lowered the transformation costs; (b) leadership by a few large pumpers who bore disproportionate costs because they had the most to lose; (c) incremental rule changes that changed the payoffs and information base for the next round; and (d) the fact that participants could see the basins collapsing, which raised the expected benefits of change.

Key model / example
- The litigation game: the court and the referee changed the game from "pump before others do" to "negotiate shares under a judge who can impose a worse outcome." Ostrom makes the point that the participants used the state as a *facilitator* of self-organization (information, a credible threat, legal standing), not as a Leviathan designing the rules from above.

So-what for a designer
- Do not wait for the whole institution; find the first cheap change that produces information and standing (a shared dashboard, a recognized forum) and let it lower the cost of the next.
- External authority is most useful as a backstop and information supplier, less useful as the rule-writer. Think "court plus enabling legislation," not "central plan."
- Expect the largest stakeholders to bear the early costs; design so that they can, and so that the resulting rules do not simply entrench them.

### Ch 5 — Analyzing institutional failures and fragilities
Ostrom pairs each failure with the principles that were absent.
- **Turkish inshore fisheries (Bodrum and the Bay of Izmir).** In contrast to Alanya, the Bodrum fishery was flooded by new entrants (trawlers subsidized by government loans, migrants) and had no way to define a boundary; conflict between artisanal and industrial fishers was unresolved and the state licensing regime was not enforced. The Izmir bay case had similar open entry plus pollution from the city. Missing: principles 1, 3, 6 and, to an extent, 7.
- **A California basin that did not organize (the Mojave River basin).** Same legal environment as Ch 4, but a much larger group, higher heterogeneity among pumpers, less shared information and greater uncertainty about the hydrology, leading to a failed adjudication effort at the time. Ostrom's point: the supply of institutions is sensitive to group size, heterogeneity and information, not just to the legal opportunity.
- **Sri Lankan fishery (Mawelle).** Beach seine fishers had a local rotation rule for net launches that worked while the number of nets was limited; when the number of nets grew (partly because outside political patrons backed new entrants), the rotation became unworkable and the national government's attempts to limit nets were undermined by local political intervention. Missing: boundary rule that could be enforced, and external recognition — external actors repeatedly overrode local rules.
- **Sri Lankan irrigation projects (Kirindi Oya and related schemes).** Large, externally designed and funded schemes in which settlers had no role in rule-making, government officers were the monitors and were not accountable to farmers, head-end farmers took water and tail-end farmers were left short, and maintenance was neglected. Ostrom contrasts this with the Gal Oya project, where an outside intervention that deliberately built farmer organizations (Uphoff's work) reversed the pattern. Missing: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.
- **Nova Scotia inshore fisheries (Port Lameron Harbour).** Fishers had long-standing informal rules about who could fish where and with what gear, but the Canadian federal licensing system did not recognize them; the local rules survived only as long as the state ignored them and were fragile against new licensed entrants. Missing: 7 (and by implication 8).

Core claim across the failures: failure is not a mystery that needs a new theory; it is the absence of a specific subset of the principles, and the most common absentees are boundaries, congruence and external recognition. Central governments in these cases were often not the solution but a cause of fragility, because they issued licenses without local knowledge, overrode local rules, or provided monitors with no accountability to users.

So-what for a designer
- The failure pattern "outside sponsor sets up the group, appoints the monitor, and does not let the members change the rules" recurs in companies (centrally imposed OKR processes with no team ownership) and platforms (moderation run by staff with no accountability to the community). Kirindi Oya is that pattern.
- Growth is the classic killer: a rotation or turn-taking norm that works for 20 members breaks at 60 unless boundaries or nesting are introduced.

### Ch 6 — A framework for analysis of self-organizing and self-governing CPRs
Core claims
- Return to the three puzzles. *Supply*: institutions get supplied incrementally when the expected benefits of changing rules exceed the expected costs for enough of the affected people, with the calculation heavily shaped by situational variables and by external arenas that lower transformation costs. *Commitment*: rational appropriators adopt contingent strategies of quasi-voluntary compliance when they believe most others comply, the rules are legitimate and violations are likely to be caught. *Monitoring*: mutual monitoring is cheap when it is a by-product of using the resource, the monitors benefit directly from the information, and small sanctions plus reputation make enforcement worthwhile.
- Framework of institutional choice. An individual's choice to support a rule change depends on the expected benefits (which depend on the state of the resource, the flow of information, the rules in place and the perceived alternatives), the expected costs (transformation costs and ongoing monitoring/enforcement costs), and internal norms and discount rates. Discount rates matter enormously: people who expect to still be using the resource in 30 years, and whose children will, weigh the future more; migrants, absentee owners and politically insecure users discount it heavily.
- Situational variables that raise the odds of self-organization (Ostrom lists these tentatively): a small enough and stable group; shared understanding of the resource and of the effects of rules; low discount rates; reasonably homogeneous interests; low-cost information about the resource condition; prior experience of organizing; and external authorities that permit rather than obstruct.
- A challenge to social science: stop using the PD as a stand-in for reality; take seriously that rules are chosen by participants, that the *levels* of choice matter, and that field cases are data. She proposes an explicitly comparative research program on institutional variables, which became the Institutional Analysis and Development (IAD) framework and, later, the social-ecological systems framework.

So-what for a designer
- The cost-benefit framing tells you where to intervene: lower the cost of rule change (recognized forums, cheap information), raise the perceived benefit (make the state of the shared resource visible), lengthen horizons (tenure, stakes, membership stability).
- Group size, heterogeneity and turnover are not fixed facts; they are design levers (subgroup, nest, slow the churn).

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. **The tragedy of the commons is a tragedy of open access, not of shared property.** Hardin's model, the PD and Olson's logic all assume no communication, no rule-making and no monitoring. Where those are possible, the predictions fail, and the models should be read as descriptions of a situation that its participants can change.
2. **A CPR is defined by subtractable units and costly exclusion**, which separates appropriation problems (who takes how much) from provision problems (who maintains the system). Most real free-rider problems are a mix and need rules for both.
3. **Rules live at three levels** — operational, collective-choice, constitutional — and durable self-governance requires that the people affected by operational rules have a legitimate route to change them, and that their right to do so is recognized from outside.
4. **The eight design principles** (boundaries, congruence, collective choice, monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict resolution, recognized right to organize, nested enterprises) are the structural regularities of durable self-governed commons; they are diagnostic, not prescriptive rules.
5. **Monitoring and commitment are one problem, not two.** Compliance is contingent on expected compliance by others and expected detection; cheap, by-product monitoring plus small first sanctions maintain the shared belief that the rule is alive.
6. **Graduated sanctions are a feature, not weakness.** Small first fines preserve information flow and the norm; harsh escalation is held in reserve for repeat offenders.
7. **Institutions are supplied incrementally.** The California basins escaped a real tragedy through a sequence of small changes, each lowering the cost of the next, using external authority as a facilitator rather than a designer.
8. **Failures have identifiable missing principles**, most often boundaries, congruence and external recognition; central governments are often the cause of fragility rather than its cure.
9. **Situational variables predict whether self-organization gets off the ground**: small stable groups, shared information, low discount rates, prior organizing experience, homogeneous interests, and non-obstructive external authorities.
10. **Neither markets nor states are the default answer**; polycentric arrangements in which several overlapping authorities each handle what they are best placed to handle are the empirical norm in the durable cases.

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **Common-pool resource (CPR)** — a resource system whose units are subtractable and from which exclusion is costly — bites when a "public good" (a codebase, a support rota, a shared budget) is actually depletable and someone's use degrades others'.
- **Resource system vs. resource units** — the stock (fishery, canal, on-call rota) vs. the flow (fish, water turns, hours of attention) — separates who maintains from who consumes.
- **Appropriator / provider / producer** — those who take units / those who arrange supply / those who build or maintain — most group members are all three, and rules must cover all three roles.
- **Appropriation problem vs. provision problem** — allocation of flow vs. maintenance of stock — the first is about assignment and timing; the second about contribution and free-riding on upkeep.
- **Operational / collective-choice / constitutional rules** — daily rules / rules for changing rules / rules for who may change the rule-changing rules — most stalled reforms fail at the middle level.
- **Rules-in-use vs. rules-in-form** — what people actually follow vs. what is written — audit the former.
- **Contingent strategy / quasi-voluntary compliance** — "I comply if most others do and violators are likely to be caught" — commitment is conditional and must be fed by visible compliance.
- **Graduated sanctions** — small first penalties, escalating with repetition and seriousness — protects the flow of information about violations.
- **Institutional supply** — the problem that new rules are a public good nobody has an individual incentive to supply — solved by low-cost arenas, leaders with high stakes and incremental steps.
- **Transformation costs** — the cost of moving from one set of rules to another — the thing external authorities can most usefully lower.
- **Polycentricity / nested enterprises** — many overlapping decision centers at different scales, each handling what fits it — the alternative to both a single center and pure decentralization.
- **Discount rate (of appropriators)** — how much users weigh the future of the resource — the fastest way to break a commons is to make its users transient.
- **Design principle (Ostrom's sense)** — an essential element or condition that helps account for the success of durable institutions — not a rule to be copied, a condition to check for.

## Evidence strength & limits
- **What is shown vs. argued.** The book shows, through carefully documented secondary cases and Ostrom's own primary research on the California basins, that self-organized durable CPR institutions exist and that they share structural features. It *argues*, but cannot prove from these cases alone, that the eight principles are what causes durability: the cases were selected on the dependent variable, the sample is small, and the failures are compared informally.
- **Later systematic evidence.** Cox, Arnold and Villamayor-Tomás (2010, *Ecology and Society* 15(4): 38) reviewed 91 empirical studies that had examined one or more of the principles and found them well supported: in the large majority of studies that assessed a principle, its presence was associated with success or its absence with failure. They also proposed a reformulation splitting three principles: 1A (user boundaries) and 1B (resource boundaries); 2A (congruence with local conditions) and 2B (proportionality of benefits to costs); 4A (monitoring users) and 4B (monitoring the resource). The reformulation is now the standard version in the literature. Caveats the reviewers themselves raise: publication bias toward cases that fit; many studies are qualitative and code the principles loosely; the principles describe structure, not the processes (trust, legitimacy, transparency) that make structure work.
- **Experimental evidence.** Ostrom, Gardner and Walker's *Rules, Games, and Common-Pool Resources* (1994) and later lab work found that face-to-face communication ("cheap talk") dramatically raises cooperation in CPR games and that subjects will pay to sanction over-appropriators, consistent with the book's claims about communication, monitoring and sanctioning; the general finding that communication raises cooperation in social dilemmas is one of the most robust in experimental economics (see 06-behavioral-game-theory). Field experiments in the 2000s (Cardenas and colleagues in Colombia) found externally imposed regulation with weak enforcement can *crowd out* the cooperation that communication produces, again consistent with the book.
- **Critiques worth knowing.**
  - *Variable proliferation.* Agrawal (2001, *World Development*) noted that Ostrom, Wade and Baland & Platteau together list around 30+ conditions for success; without a theory of which matter when, the lists risk being unfalsifiable. Ostrom's later diagnostic and social-ecological systems frameworks (2007, 2009) were partly a response.
  - *Panacea and scale.* Ostrom herself (2007, PNAS, "A diagnostic approach for going beyond panaceas") warned against treating the principles, or community management generally, as a universal fix; critics note the principles are drawn from small, place-based, low-tech commons and may transfer poorly to large-scale, global or digital commons. Later work by Hess and Ostrom on knowledge commons and by others on open-source and Wikipedia found partial fit but also that principles 1 and 2 need reinterpretation when units are non-subtractable.
  - *Power and heterogeneity.* Political ecologists (e.g. Agrawal, Cleaver, Mosse) argue that the book underplays power: durable institutions can be durable because they entrench elites (the huerta and zanjera cases have internal inequalities), and "community" is often less bounded and more contested than the cases suggest. Cleaver's "institutional bricolage" argues that rules are assembled from existing social practices rather than designed.
  - *Static principles.* The principles describe steady state; they say little about adaptation, ecological dynamics or what to do when the resource itself changes regime. Resilience scholars (Anderies, Janssen, Ostrom 2004) attempted to add this.
  - *Selection and history.* Surviving centuries-old cases may have survived for reasons unrelated to their rules (isolation, low external demand); the failure cases are less thoroughly documented than the successes.
- **What is robust.** That open-access assumptions drive the tragedy prediction; that communication and self-monitoring raise cooperation; that external recognition and boundaries matter; that graduated sanctions are widespread in durable systems. What is contested: the necessity or sufficiency of any individual principle, the applicability to large or digital commons, and the neglect of power.

## Design implications for cooperation in real groups
- Before treating a problem as a free-rider problem, check whether members can communicate, observe each other's behavior and change the rules; fix those channels first. [E]
- Define who is in and who is out of the group that shares the resource, and define the resource's edge; ambiguous membership is the most common structural cause of decay. [E]
- Match contribution obligations to benefits (proportionality); people tolerate high obligations when the ratio is visibly fair, and reject light obligations that seem arbitrary. [E]
- Give the people bound by an operational rule a legitimate, low-cost way to change it; rules imposed from outside without such a route are followed only while enforced. [E]
- Design monitoring as a by-product of participation (visible queues, public logs, turn-taking that reveals cheaters) rather than as a separate policing function. [E]
- Use graduated sanctions: a small, quick, public first response; escalating penalties for repeat or flagrant violations; never a nuclear first strike. [E]
- Provide a cheap, fast, local forum for disputes about what a rule means before disputes become violations. [E]
- Secure explicit recognition from the surrounding authority (management, platform owner, funder) that the group may govern this domain; otherwise every local sanction can be appealed over the group's head. [E]
- When the group grows past the scale at which by-product monitoring works, nest: subgroups handle local problems, a higher layer handles conflicts between subgroups and shared infrastructure. [E]
- Lengthen members' horizons (stable membership, tenure, stakes in future outcomes); high turnover raises discount rates and undermines every other principle. [H]
- Prefer incremental institutional change that produces information and standing over grand redesign; use external authority as a backstop and data source rather than as the rule author. [H]
- Expect the large stakeholders to fund the early organizing; design collective-choice rules so this early investment does not become permanent capture. [H]
- Treat the eight principles as a checklist to diagnose which is missing, not as a template to install; the specific rules must be locally crafted. [V]
- Prefer polycentric arrangements over a single decision center where problems occur at multiple scales. [V]

## Retrieval practice
1. (Recall) Name the three models Ostrom argues are misused, and the single shared assumption that makes all three predict failure.
<details>Tragedy of the commons (Hardin), prisoner's dilemma, logic of collective action (Olson). Shared assumption: participants cannot communicate, cannot make binding agreements and cannot change the rules of the game, so they are trapped in a one-shot dominant-strategy structure.</details>

2. (Explain-why) Why does Ostrom find *small* first-offense sanctions in durable systems rather than penalties large enough to make cheating unprofitable?
<details>Because compliance is contingent and quasi-voluntary. The purpose of a first sanction is to signal to the violator that they were observed and to everyone else that the rule is still enforced, preserving the shared expectation that most others comply. Harsh first sanctions make violators and monitors hide information and can erode the sense of legitimacy; escalation is reserved for repeat offenders.</details>

3. (Recall) State the difference between operational, collective-choice and constitutional rules with one example each.
<details>Operational: the daily rotation of fishing spots. Collective-choice: the cooperative meeting that can change the rotation. Constitutional: the law and the government's tolerance that gives the cooperative standing to bind its members.</details>

4. (Apply) An engineering org shares a CI cluster; queues are long, some teams submit huge jobs, and a platform team wants to impose per-team quotas. Diagnose with the principles.
<details>Check 1 (are the users of the cluster well defined?), 2 (are quotas proportional to what teams contribute or need, or arbitrary?), 3 (can the teams affected change the quota rule, or is it imposed?), 4 (is usage visible to all teams as a by-product of the queue?), 5 (what happens on a first violation — a visible warning, not a lockout?), 6 (where do teams argue about whether a job was necessary?), 7 (has leadership recognized the teams' right to set rules?), 8 (is the org large enough that per-department sub-allocation with an inter-department layer is needed?). Prediction: an imposed quota without 3 and 4 will be gamed; a visible queue with a team forum will likely outperform it.</details>

5. (Spot the misconception) "Ostrom showed that communities always manage commons better than governments or markets."
<details>Wrong. She showed that self-governance is a third option that works under identifiable conditions, that it fails for identifiable reasons, and that durable cases usually combine local rules with external backstops (courts, enabling law). She explicitly warned against panaceas.</details>

6. (Explain-why) How did the California groundwater pumpers escape a real pumping race without a central authority writing the rules?
<details>Through sequential, incremental change: a lawsuit using a state referee to produce shared hydrological information, a negotiated stipulated judgment defining shares, a court-appointed watermaster to monitor, water associations as entrepreneurs, and new special districts under enabling legislation to import water and tax pumping. The state facilitated by lowering information and enforcement costs; the pumpers designed the rules.</details>

7. (Apply) An online community's moderation is run entirely by platform staff who are not community members; complaints about inconsistent bans are rising. Which principles are missing, and what failure case does it resemble?
<details>Missing 3 (members cannot change the rules), 4 (monitors not accountable to users), 5 (bans are not graduated), 6 (no cheap local dispute forum). It resembles the Sri Lankan irrigation projects where government officers monitored without accountability to farmers and rules were set from outside.</details>

8. (Recall) What did the Cox, Arnold and Villamayor-Tomás (2010) review conclude and what did it change?
<details>Reviewing 91 studies, they found the principles well supported empirically, and reformulated them by splitting boundaries into user and resource boundaries, congruence into local fit and cost-benefit proportionality, and monitoring into monitoring users and monitoring the resource. They flagged that the principles capture structure but not processes such as trust and legitimacy.</details>

9. (Explain-why) Why does Ostrom treat monitoring and commitment as a single puzzle?
<details>Because a rational appropriator's commitment is contingent on believing others comply and that violations are likely to be detected; monitoring generates that belief. Conversely, people monitor because they benefit from knowing whether their own contingent compliance is warranted. Neither can be explained without the other.</details>

10. (Spot the misconception) "The eight design principles tell you which rules to adopt."
<details>They do not specify rules. The rules in Törbel, the Japanese villages, Valencia, Alicante and the zanjeras are all different. The principles specify structural conditions — who is in, whether rules fit, who can change them, who watches, how sanctions escalate, where disputes go, whether outsiders recognize the group, and how scale is handled — under which locally crafted rules tend to endure.</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- **Reading it as anti-state or anti-market.** Ostrom's durable cases lean on courts, enabling legislation and recognized property rights; her point is that neither central control nor privatization is the *only* solution, not that either is always wrong.
- **Treating the principles as a recipe.** She calls them design principles because they are conditions found in durable institutions, not because they can be installed; she was explicit that rules must be locally crafted and that the list is provisional.
- **Assuming the successes are egalitarian idylls.** Several durable systems have hereditary shares, unequal holdings and exclusion of outsiders; durability and fairness are different criteria, and later critics rightly press on the power dimension.
- **Extending the results to any shared thing.** The book is about subtractable resources with costly exclusion. Knowledge commons, open-source projects and attention economies differ on subtractability, and the principles need reinterpretation there.
- **Believing group size is the key variable.** Ostrom treats size as one of several situational variables interacting with heterogeneity, information, discount rates and external context; the Mojave case failed partly on size, but the zanjera federations succeeded with hundreds of members through nesting.
- **Thinking the book refutes the prisoner's dilemma.** It refutes the use of the PD as a law of nature. The PD remains a valid model of the open-access situation; the argument is that participants can, and do, move themselves into a different game.
