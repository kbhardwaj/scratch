# The Stag Hunt and the Evolution of Social Structure — Brian Skyrms (Cambridge University Press, 2004)
Level: Order  ·  Priority: Core
Est. reading time saved: ~5h  ·  Your time with this file: ~35 min

## The book in one sentence
The social contract is not a prisoner's dilemma but a stag hunt — a game with a safe, poor equilibrium and a risky, rich one — and three features of real interaction (where you are, what you can signal, and whom you get to associate with) each tilt evolutionary dynamics from the safe equilibrium toward the cooperative one.

## The book in one paragraph
Skyrms takes Rousseau's parable — hunters can each take a hare alone or all cooperate to take a stag — and argues that most of what political philosophy calls "the problem of cooperation" has this shape once repetition and reputation are included, not the prisoner's dilemma's shape. The stag hunt has two pure equilibria: all-stag (better for all) and all-hare (safer). Evolutionary dynamics with random matching usually deliver the safe one because hare hunting is *risk dominant*. The book then shows, with small models drawn from Skyrms's papers with Alexander, Pemantle and Zollman, that three departures from random mixing change the answer. *Location*: interacting with neighbors and copying successful neighbors lets fair division and stag hunting spread contagiously. *Signals*: costless pre-play talk can evolve meaning and act as a handshake that lets stag hunters find each other. *Association*: when people choose partners and reinforce partnerships that pay, stag hunters cluster together, and when structure changes faster than strategies, cooperation wins. The postscript draws the moral: Hobbes, Hume and Rousseau were describing a stag hunt, and the road out of the state of nature is paved by correlation, not altruism.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
This is the Order level's formal spine. Dixit et al. and Camerer gave you the games; Schelling gave you micro-structure producing macro-pattern. Skyrms gives you the dynamics: given agents who imitate, reinforce and drift, which equilibrium do they actually reach, and what structural levers move them? Read it with the question: **is my group's problem really a prisoner's dilemma (someone always gains by defecting) or a stag hunt (everyone would cooperate if they trusted others to)? And if a stag hunt, which of location, signalling and partner choice is the cheapest lever into the good equilibrium?** Most "cooperation failures" in teams are trust failures in a stag hunt, and the fixes are structural, not motivational.

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

### Ch 1 — The Stag Hunt
- Core claims
  - Rousseau's stag hunt: each hunter can defect to a sure hare, or stay at his post and share a stag caught only if everyone stays. Two strict Nash equilibria: all-stag (*payoff dominant*) and all-hare (*risk dominant*, the safer bet under uncertainty about others). The game is a problem of *trust*, not *temptation*.
  - The prisoner's dilemma has one equilibrium (mutual defection) and cooperation is never a best reply. Skyrms argues the PD is the wrong model for the social contract: add repetition, reputation or a shadow of the future and it becomes a stag hunt. In a repeated PD, "cooperate-as-long-as-you-do" versus "always defect" has two equilibria, and which you land in depends on what you expect of others. Hume's farmers draining a shared meadow and Hobbes's state of nature are stag hunts in this reading.
  - Risk dominance (Harsanyi and Selten): in a symmetric 2x2 game, the equilibrium that is the best reply when you assign 50/50 to the other's choice. Equivalently, the strategy with the *larger basin of attraction* under replicator dynamics.
  - Replicator dynamics: a strategy's share grows in proportion to how much its average payoff exceeds the population average — the canonical model of success-biased imitation. Under random pairing the population goes to whichever pure equilibrium's basin it starts in; the mixed equilibrium is the unstable watershed.
- Key model / worked example
  - Payoffs (row player): Stag/Stag 4, Stag/Hare 0, Hare 3 against either. Let p be the fraction hunting stag. Expected payoff of Stag = 4p, of Hare = 3, so the watershed is p* = 3/4. Above it replicator dynamics push p to 1; below it to 0. The payoff-dominant equilibrium's basin is only the top quarter of the state space, so a random start almost always ends at hare. Hare is risk dominant: against a 50/50 opponent Stag gets 2, Hare 3. Raise Stag/Hare from 0 to 2 and p* drops to 1/2 — equal basins. The ratio of "loss from being duped" to "gain from cooperating" sets the watershed. (Illustrative numbers of my own; the book uses similar small games.)
- So-what
  - Diagnose the game before you design. If a member gains from defecting even when everyone else cooperates, it is a PD and needs enforcement or repetition. If members would cooperate given confidence that others will, it is a stag hunt and the problem is *expectation management*. Many "free rider" complaints are hare hunting by people who do not trust the team to show up.
  - Basin size, not the attractiveness of the good outcome, predicts where a group settles. Sweetening the joint payoff rarely helps; cutting the cost of being the lone cooperator helps a lot.

### Part I: Location

### Ch 2 — Bargaining with Neighbors
- Core claims
  - Divide-the-pie: two players simultaneously demand shares; if demands are compatible each gets its demand, otherwise nothing. Any compatible pair is a Nash equilibrium. Skyrms's *Evolution of the Social Contract* (1996) showed that under replicator dynamics with random pairing the equal split has the largest basin but the population can get stuck in *polymorphic traps*, e.g. a stable mix of 4/10-demanders and 6/10-demanders.
  - With local interaction on a ring or lattice and "imitate the most successful neighbor" (Alexander and Skyrms 1999, "Bargaining with neighbors: is justice contagious?"), the equal split takes over from almost every start, and fast — tens of generations rather than hundreds. Polymorphic traps essentially vanish.
  - Why: a patch of fair dividers meeting each other gets the full pie every time, while greedy demanders on its boundary meet fair dividers and get nothing. Neighbors copy the visibly successful fair dividers and the patch grows. Positive correlation between like strategies, a by-product of clustering, does the work.
- Key model / worked example
  - 10-unit pie; strategies demand 4, 5 or 6. Random mixing: 4-vs-6 pairs succeed, so a 4/6 polymorphism sustains itself. Ring with local imitation: a run of fair dividers earn 5 each. A greedy cell beside the run earns 0 from its fair neighbor and 4 from a modest neighbor on its other side; the adjacent fair cell earns 5 and 0. The greedy cell's best-scoring neighbor is the fair one (5 > 4), so it copies fairness and the run grows. (Illustrative numbers in the chapter's spirit, not the book's exact runs.)
- So-what
  - Fairness norms are *contagious* when interaction is local and success is visible. To spread an equal-effort or equal-credit norm, use small stable subgroups and make success visible; random-mixing organizations are where fairness gets stuck in polymorphisms.

### Ch 3 — Stag Hunt with Neighbors
- Core claims
  - Put the stag hunt on a lattice where each agent plays its neighbors. Under *best-response* revision, Ellison (1993) showed the risk-dominant equilibrium wins: a cluster of hare hunters spreads through stag hunters, and local interaction speeds convergence to the safe equilibrium. Under *imitate-the-best-neighbor* revision the result can flip: a small cluster of stag hunters can spread through hare hunters if the stag payoff is high enough relative to hare. The boundary between the outcomes is not the risk-dominance boundary.
  - The difference is what an agent looks at. Best responders ask "what does my neighborhood do?"; imitators ask "who near me is doing well?" A stag hunter inside a cluster earns the stag payoff from every neighbor and is the most successful agent boundary hare hunters can see. Structure helps cooperation only if the *learning rule* copies success. Clusters also make the relevant risk local: what fraction of *my* neighbors hunt stag, which a cluster keeps high even when the global fraction is small.
- Key model / worked example
  - Moore neighborhood (8 neighbors), Stag/Stag = s, Stag/Hare = 0, Hare = 3 against anyone. A hare hunter earns 24 regardless. In a 3x3 stag cluster the center earns 8s, an edge cell 5s, a corner 3s. With s = 4 the edge earns 20 < 24, edge cells copy hare, the cluster shrinks. With s = 5 the edge earns 25 > 24, so the hare hunter beside it copies stag, while corners (15) still copy hare. Growth or dissolution depends on this local arithmetic and on tie-breaking, which is why the book reports simulations. With s = 5 hare is still risk dominant (Stag gets 2.5, Hare 3 against a 50/50 opponent), yet stag can spread from a cluster.
- So-what
  - Local interaction is a lever only with a success-copying culture. If the group updates by "what is everyone doing?" (conformity), cooperator clusters get swamped; if by "who is thriving?" (success bias, see 01-secret-of-our-success), a visibly successful seed team converts its neighbors. Make cooperators' payoffs visible and protect a seed cluster until its interior is untouchable.

### Part II: Signals

### Ch 4 — Evolution of Inference
- Core claims
  - Lewis (1969) signaling game: nature picks a state; a sender who sees the state sends one of several costless signals; a receiver who sees only the signal chooses an act; both get paid if the act fits the state. A *signaling system* is an equilibrium where signals are used and interpreted so that the right act is always chosen. Lewis worried about how such a convention could arise without prior agreement (which would already require language). Skyrms's answer: evolution or learning does it, with no pre-existing meaning.
  - Result for the simplest game (2 equiprobable states, 2 signals, 2 acts): under replicator dynamics, the population converges to one of the two signaling systems from almost every starting point (Skyrms 1996; later proved rigorously by Huttegger 2007). Meaning is the output of dynamics, not an input. Which system you get — "signal A means state 1" or the reverse — is arbitrary, exactly Lewis's conventionality.
  - "Inference": build the game up so that a receiver gets signals from two senders who each see part of the world (say, one sees whether it rained and the other whether it is cold), and must choose an act that depends on both. Populations evolve receivers who act on the *combination* of signals, which is a primitive logical operation (conjunction, or a step of a syllogism) implemented by behavior alone. Skyrms's point is deflationary and constructive: proto-inference needs no mind, only correlated selection. He connects this to animal signaling (vervet monkey alarm calls, Cheney and Seyfarth) as real evolved signaling systems.
- Key model / worked example
  - Two states each with probability 1/2; sender strategies map state to signal; receiver strategies map signal to act; payoff 1 if the act matches the state, else 0. A signaling-system pair earns 1 every round. A "pooling" sender (same signal regardless) leaves the receiver guessing: expected 0.5. Starting from a population with 60% of senders using "A for state 1" and 60% of receivers using "act 1 on A", the matched pair earns above average and grows; replicator dynamics take it to 100%. Start from a 40/40 mix and the other signaling system wins. Only the knife-edge 50/50 start fails to converge.
  - Limits the book concedes and later work sharpened: with *unequal* state probabilities or more than two states, partial-pooling equilibria (some signals carry no information) can attract a positive share of starting points (Huttegger, Skyrms, Tarrès and Wagner 2010; Pawlowitsch 2008). Evolution of meaning is robust in the simplest case and merely likely in richer ones.
- So-what
  - Shared vocabulary in a group — what a status word, a ticket label or an alarm means — need not be designed; it can emerge from repeated payoff-driven use. But the arbitrary and path-dependent character of the result means two subgroups can evolve incompatible dialects (see Zollman below). Designers should either let a convention emerge and then codify it, or seed one early before rival conventions take root.

### Ch 5 — Cheap Talk
- Core claims
  - Cheap talk is costless, non-binding pre-play messaging. Classical objections: (a) in a stag hunt everyone wants to say "I'll hunt stag" regardless of intention, so the message carries no information (Aumann's argument); (b) in a PD, talk cannot help because defection dominates no matter what is said. Skyrms's reply is dynamic, not equilibrium-based: even if talk carries no information *at equilibrium*, it carries *transient* information along the evolutionary path, and that can change which equilibrium is reached.
  - Model: a strategy is a triple — which signal to send, what to do on receiving signal A, what to do on receiving signal B. Signals start meaningless. Under replicator dynamics with random pairing, the basin of attraction of stag hunting expands dramatically compared to the stag hunt without signals: in Skyrms's simulations the majority of random starting points now end at all-stag. Mechanism: a "secret handshake" (Robson 1990) — a type that sends A and hunts stag only with A-senders — can grow because it cooperates with its own kind and plays safe with others. Once stag hunting dominates, the signal loses its informational content, but by then the population is at the good equilibrium and the meaningless signal is harmless.
  - Contrast with the PD: a handshake type can invade, but it is then invaded by a mimic that sends A and defects. Cheap talk helps in the stag hunt because the good outcome is an equilibrium once reached; in the PD it is not.
- Key model / worked example
  - Same payoffs as ch 1 (Stag/Stag 4, Stag/Hare 0, Hare 3). Handshake strategy H: send A; stag with A-senders, hare otherwise. Against a plain hare hunter who sends B, H hunts hare and gets 3 — no worse than a hare hunter. Against another H, it gets 4. So H weakly dominates plain hare hunting and can grow from a small share. The only threat is a "send A, hunt hare" free rider, which gets 3 against H while H gets 0 against it. In the PD version, this free rider's payoff advantage is decisive; in the stag hunt, once H is common, the free rider earns 3 while H-H pairs earn 4, so the free rider is only a nuisance if it becomes common enough to push the handshake types below the watershed.
- So-what
  - Cheap, pre-commitment talk — public pledges, stand-up declarations of intent, visible sign-ups — is not empty in a trust problem, even when everyone knows the words are not binding. It works by creating a temporary correlation between those who use the convention and those who cooperate, long enough to move the group into the good equilibrium. It is nearly worthless where defection pays regardless (a true PD); there, talk must be backed by enforcement.

### Part III: Association

### Ch 6 — Choosing Partners
- Core claims
  - Drop the assumption that partners are assigned randomly or fixed by geography. Agents *choose* whom to interact with, and the choice is learned by simple reinforcement: the propensity to visit a partner increases by the payoff the visit produced (a Pólya-urn / Roth-Erev style rule). This is the Skyrms and Pemantle (2000) model of dynamic social network formation.
  - "Making friends": even when every interaction pays the same to everyone (no strategic content at all), reinforcement learning produces structure — the visiting probabilities do not stay uniform; the population's network converges to a random but definite pattern in which some pairs interact a lot and others almost never. Structure can be a by-product of learning with no payoff difference driving it. Adding discounting of old reinforcement (forgetting) makes the emerging ties stronger and the network freeze into cliques and pairs.
  - With stag hunt payoffs and fixed strategies: stag hunters reinforce visits to other stag hunters (they earn 4 there and 0 elsewhere) and rapidly learn to associate with each other; hare hunters earn 3 wherever they go and drift. The result is positive correlation: stag hunters interact mostly with stag hunters and out-earn hare hunters on average — the opposite of the random-mixing result. Partner choice manufactures the correlation that ch 3 needed geography to supply.
- Key model / worked example
  - Three agents, each with a weight of 1 on each of the other two. Each round every agent visits one other with probability proportional to weights and both get the game payoff; weights increase by the payoff received. Suppose agents 1 and 2 are stag hunters and 3 is a hare hunter. Round 1: agent 1 visits 2, both get 4, so agent 1's weight on 2 becomes 5 (weight on 3 stays 1) and agent 2's weight on 1 becomes 5. Now agent 1 visits 2 with probability 5/6. Agent 3 visits either and gets 3 either way, so its weights grow evenly. Within a handful of rounds 1 and 2 are almost always together; agent 3 earns 3 per round and they earn close to 4. With hare risk dominant under random matching, association still makes stag hunting the better-paid strategy.
- So-what
  - Freedom to choose partners, plus a memory that reinforces what worked, is the strongest single lever for cooperation in the book. A platform or organization that lets cooperators find and stick with each other (stable teams, opt-in collaboration, reputational search) generates the correlation that makes cooperation pay. Forced random assignment does the opposite.

### Ch 7 — Coevolution of Structure and Strategy
- Core claims
  - Now let both things evolve: the interaction network (by reinforcement as in ch 6) and strategies (by imitation of successful others). What matters is the *relative speed* of the two processes. If structure adapts fast relative to strategy revision, stag hunters find each other before they can be tempted to switch, they out-earn hare hunters, hare hunters imitate them, and the population goes to all-stag. If strategy revision is fast and structure is sluggish (close to random mixing), the dynamics resemble ch 1 and the risk-dominant hare equilibrium tends to win.
  - This reframes the "which equilibrium" question: it is not fixed by the payoffs but by the ratio of structural fluidity to strategic fluidity. Most evolutionary game theory holds structure fixed and evolves strategies; Skyrms shows that letting structure move is a qualitatively different regime, generally friendlier to cooperation.
  - In the prisoner's dilemma the same machinery helps less: defectors also learn to seek out cooperators (they are paid best there), so association alone cannot rescue cooperation; the cooperators' only protection is that their partners also choose, and reinforce visits to those who cooperated with them. The book is honest that the PD remains hard.
- Key model / worked example
  - Take the ch 6 setup with ten agents, half stag half hare, plus a rule that every k rounds each agent copies the strategy of the agent with the highest recent average payoff among those it has interacted with. With k large (structure learns fast), by the time the first imitation step happens, stag hunters are averaging near 4 and hare hunters 3, so hare hunters convert; the population tips to all-stag. With k = 1 (strategy revision every round), early on stag hunters are still visiting hare hunters half the time and averaging 2, hare hunters average 3, and stag hunters convert to hare before association can help. Same payoffs, opposite outcomes, set only by k. (Illustrative construction summarizing the chapter's qualitative result; the book reports simulation distributions rather than this exact setup.)
- So-what
  - When you introduce a cooperative practice, slow down strategy churn and speed up partnership formation: give cooperators time and channels to find each other before the practice is judged. Frequent re-evaluation of "is this worth it?" while teams are still randomly composed kills stag hunts.

### Postscript
- Core claims
  - Restates the philosophical thesis. Hobbes's escape from the state of nature via a sovereign, Hume's convention, and Rousseau's parable are attempts to explain how a population reaches the cooperative equilibrium of a stag hunt. The book's models show the reach is not mysterious: location, signals and association are each ordinary features of human life, each generates correlation between like-minded players, and correlation is what expands the basin of the cooperative equilibrium.
  - Correlation is the master concept. Random mixing is the assumption under which cooperation is hardest; almost any real structure departs from it in cooperation's favour. The evolution of social structure and the evolution of cooperation are the same process seen from two sides.
- So-what
  - The design mantra: *do not try to change preferences; change who meets whom, what they can signal, and how fast they can re-sort.*

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. Most cooperation problems worth solving are stag hunts (two equilibria, the good one requires trust), not prisoner's dilemmas (one bad equilibrium, cooperation never a best reply). Repetition and reputation turn PDs into stag hunts. Diagnose which one you have.
2. In a stag hunt, populations under random mixing tend to the *risk-dominant* equilibrium, which is often the poor one. Basin size, not payoff size, decides; the watershed is set by the cost of being the lone cooperator relative to the gain from joint cooperation.
3. Replicator dynamics — strategies grow in proportion to above-average payoff — is the model of success-biased imitation; it is what "the group learns" means in this book.
4. Correlation between like strategies is what rescues cooperation. Every mechanism in the book works by making stag hunters meet stag hunters more often than chance.
5. Location: local interaction plus imitate-the-successful lets fair division and stag hunting spread contagiously from small clusters; local interaction plus best-response favours the risk-dominant outcome. The learning rule decides whether structure helps.
6. Signals: meaning evolves from nothing in Lewis signaling games; costless pre-play talk expands the basin of stag hunting by letting cooperators recognize each other transiently, even though the signal is uninformative once everyone cooperates. Cheap talk does not rescue a true PD.
7. Association: letting agents choose and reinforce partners generates structure even with no payoff differences, and with stag hunt payoffs it sorts cooperators together so they out-earn hare hunters.
8. Coevolution: when interaction structure adapts faster than strategies, cooperation wins; when strategies adapt faster, the risk-dominant outcome wins. The ratio of speeds is a design variable.
9. Simple inference (acting on the conjunction of two signals) can evolve in populations of mindless agents — a warning against assuming that coordination requires shared understanding or explicit agreement.

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **Stag hunt (assurance game)** — 2x2 game with two strict equilibria, one payoff dominant and one risk dominant — where the problem is trust in others' choices. Bites when a team's "free rider problem" is really a "nobody believes the others will show up" problem.
- **Payoff dominance vs risk dominance** — payoff dominant: best for everyone if reached; risk dominant: best reply against a 50/50 opponent, equivalently the larger basin of attraction. Bites when a clearly better process fails to be adopted because trying it alone is costly.
- **Basin of attraction** — the set of population states from which dynamics lead to a given equilibrium. Bites when estimating whether a change initiative starting from current practice will tip or fizzle.
- **Replicator dynamics** — share of a strategy grows at a rate proportional to its payoff advantage over the population average. Bites as the baseline model of success-biased imitation.
- **Polymorphic trap** — a stable mix of incompatible strategies (e.g. 4/10 and 6/10 demanders) that blocks the fair norm. Bites when a group has settled into unequal but stable roles.
- **Local interaction / imitate-the-best** — play neighbors, copy the most successful neighbor. Bites as the mechanism of contagious norm spread in small stable subgroups.
- **Lewis signaling game / signaling system** — sender-receiver game whose equilibria are conventions of meaning. Bites when a team's vocabulary or status labels drift or fork.
- **Cheap talk / secret handshake** — costless message that correlates cooperators during transition. Bites when deciding whether public pledges are worth anything.
- **Reinforcement learning of partners (Pólya urn)** — visit propensity grows with the payoff a partner produced. Bites in any opt-in collaboration design.
- **Correlation of interactions** — the deviation from random matching that makes like meet like. The master variable.
- **Structure/strategy speed ratio** — how fast partnerships re-sort relative to how fast people change behavior. Bites in rollout pacing.

## Evidence strength & limits
- **What is shown**: everything in the book is a model result — analytic (replicator dynamics on 2x2 games, the Pólya-urn limits in Skyrms–Pemantle) or simulation (lattice bargaining, lattice stag hunt, cheap talk, coevolution). Within their assumptions these results are robust and several were later proved: convergence to signaling systems in the 2x2x2 equiprobable Lewis game (Huttegger 2007); network formation limits (Pemantle and Skyrms 2004).
- **What is argued**: that the social contract "is" a stag hunt rather than a PD is an interpretive thesis about Hobbes, Hume and Rousseau and about what real social dilemmas look like. It is plausible but not tested; some real commons problems are genuinely PDs at the margin (see 09-governing-the-commons for cases needing monitoring and sanctions).
- **Sensitivity to the learning rule**: the lattice results flip between best-response (Ellison 1993, risk dominance wins) and imitation (cooperation can win). Skyrms is explicit about this, but it means the design advice depends on an empirical claim about how people update, which the book does not test. Kandori, Mailath and Rob (1993) and Young (1993) show that with persistent noise the risk-dominant equilibrium is the long-run stochastically stable one under best response; Skyrms's optimistic results rely on imitation dynamics and on transient rather than long-run analysis.
- **Signaling results scale imperfectly**: with unequal state probabilities or more states and signals, partial-pooling outcomes attract non-trivial measure (Pawlowitsch 2008; Huttegger, Skyrms, Tarrès and Wagner 2010). The "meaning evolves almost surely" headline is for the simplest game.
- **Space plus signals interact non-trivially**: Zollman (2005, "Talking to neighbors: the evolution of regional meaning") added local interaction to both the Lewis game and the stag hunt with cheap talk and found that regional "dialects" emerge and that the stag hunt results depart strikingly from the random-mixing case — signals on a lattice can stabilize different local outcomes rather than uniformly delivering cooperation.
- **Robustness of the bargaining results**: D'Arms, Batterman and Górny (1998) criticized the earlier Skyrms bargaining models for sensitivity to modelling choices; the local-interaction results are partly a reply, but the general worry — that small changes in dynamics change which norm wins — stands.
- **Behaviourism critique**: Alexander's review (Economics and Philosophy, 2006) notes that the book identifies basins of attraction for strategies that are *behaviourally* cooperative or just, and says nothing about motivation, moral judgement or the phenomenology of norms. Alexander's own *The Structural Evolution of Morality* (2007) extends the framework to many games and structures (lattices, small-world networks, dynamic networks) with several learning rules, confirming that structure generally helps cooperation while showing that results vary by game and rule. Bicchieri (08-grammar-of-society) supplies the missing account of what a norm is *to the agent*.
- **Experimental evidence outside the book**: coordination failure in stag-hunt-like games is well documented in the lab (Van Huyck, Battalio and Beil 1990 minimum-effort games; see 06-behavioral-game-theory ch 7), and lab work shows that communication does help stag hunt coordination while helping PD cooperation less — broadly in line with ch 5, though the lab mechanism (belief change, promise-keeping) is not the evolutionary one. Later experiments on free neighbourhood choice in stag hunts report improved coordination on the payoff-dominant outcome, consistent with Part III, but this is a growing literature rather than a settled one.

## Design implications for cooperation in real groups
- Diagnose PD vs stag hunt before choosing a fix: ask whether a member gains by defecting when everyone else cooperates. If not, stop building enforcement and start building assurance. [E — the game-theoretic distinction is exact; H for any particular group's classification]
- Reduce the cost of being the lone cooperator (the Stag/Hare payoff) rather than sweetening the joint outcome; it moves the watershed more. [E for the model; H for the group]
- Organize interaction in small stable neighbourhoods and make cooperators' success visible so that success-biased imitation can operate; avoid random rotation while a cooperative norm is young. [H, grounded in ch 2-3 simulations]
- Check the group's learning rule: if people copy the majority (conformity) rather than the successful, local structure will not spread cooperation and may entrench the safe equilibrium; add success-visibility mechanisms. [H]
- Use cheap talk deliberately in stag hunts — public intent statements, opt-in pledges, visible sign-ups — as a handshake, and do not expect it to work where defection dominates. [E for the mechanism in models and lab; H for the specific group]
- Let people choose and keep partners; give partnership formation memory (reputation, repeat matching) so that reinforcement can sort cooperators together. [E in model; H in practice]
- Pace rollouts so structure re-sorts faster than behaviour is re-evaluated: form teams first, judge the practice later. [H]
- Seed a shared vocabulary early or accept that emergent conventions will be arbitrary and may fork between subgroups. [H]
- Do not mistake behavioural cooperation for internalized norms; structure can hold a stag hunt at the good equilibrium while beliefs remain fragile — pair with norm-building (Bicchieri) and monitoring (Ostrom). [V/H]

## Retrieval practice
1. (Recall) State the two pure equilibria of the stag hunt and name which is payoff dominant and which is risk dominant in the payoffs Stag/Stag 4, Stag/Hare 0, Hare 3.
<details>All-stag (payoff dominant: 4 > 3) and all-hare (risk dominant: against a 50/50 opponent hare earns 3, stag earns 2).</details>

2. (Explain-why) Why does Skyrms say a repeated prisoner's dilemma is "really" a stag hunt?
<details>With repetition, conditional cooperation (cooperate while the other does) against itself is an equilibrium, and always-defect against itself is another. Neither strategy is dominant; which one pays depends on what the other does. That two-equilibria trust structure is the stag hunt.</details>

3. (Apply) A population starts with 60% stag hunters under random matching with the ch 1 payoffs. Where does replicator dynamics take it, and what one payoff change would reverse the outcome?
<details>The watershed is p* = 3/4, so 60% is below it and the population goes to all-hare. Raising the Stag/Hare payoff from 0 to 2 lowers p* to 1/2, and 60% then tips to all-stag.</details>

4. (Explain-why) Why does local interaction favour cooperation under imitate-the-best but not under best-response dynamics?
<details>Best responders react to the mix of behaviour around them, so a risk-dominant strategy spreads because it is a best reply to any sufficiently mixed neighbourhood (Ellison). Imitators copy the most successful visible agent; a stag hunter inside a cluster earns the high joint payoff on every neighbour and becomes the model that boundary hare hunters copy.</details>

5. (Recall) What is a polymorphic trap in the bargaining game and how does local interaction dissolve it?
<details>A stable mix such as 4/10-demanders and 6/10-demanders whose demands are compatible with each other. Under local interaction, fair 5/5 dividers meeting each other always succeed, while greedy demanders next to them get nothing; neighbours copy the fair dividers and the fair patch grows until the polymorphism disappears.</details>

6. (Spot-the-misconception) "Cheap talk cannot matter because at equilibrium everyone sends the same message." What does this miss?
<details>It assesses only the equilibrium. Skyrms's point is dynamic: during the transition, a handshake signal correlates cooperators with each other, expanding the basin of the stag equilibrium. Once there, the signal can become uninformative without harm.</details>

7. (Apply) You run an opt-in mentoring platform. Explain, using the reinforcement model of partner choice, why letting pairs re-match freely and remembering good matches should raise the share of productive pairs.
<details>Each participant's propensity to return to a partner rises with the payoff of past interactions. Productive (stag-hunting) participants reinforce each other quickly and lock into repeated pairing; unproductive ones drift. Positive correlation forms, so cooperators mostly meet cooperators and out-earn the rest, and imitation then spreads the productive behaviour.</details>

8. (Explain-why) Why is the ratio of structural to strategic adaptation speed decisive in ch 7?
<details>If partnerships re-sort quickly, cooperators associate before anyone re-evaluates behaviour, so their higher earnings are visible when imitation happens and hare hunters convert. If behaviour re-evaluates quickly while matching is near random, stag hunters see poor average payoffs and switch to hare before association can rescue them.</details>

9. (Recall) In the 2-state, 2-signal, 2-act Lewis game with equiprobable states, what does replicator dynamics deliver, and what qualification did later work add?
<details>Convergence to one of the two signaling systems from almost every initial state (proved by Huttegger 2007). With unequal state probabilities or larger games, partial-pooling equilibria can attract a positive share of starting points.</details>

10. (Spot-the-misconception) "Skyrms shows that cooperation evolves naturally, so groups do not need institutions." What is wrong?
<details>The results are for stag hunts under specific learning rules and structures; they do not rescue genuine prisoner's dilemmas, they depend on success-biased rather than conformist learning, and long-run stochastic-stability analyses under best response still favour the risk-dominant outcome. Institutions that create local interaction, signalling and partner choice are exactly what the models recommend, and enforcement remains needed where defection dominates.</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- Reading it as a book about the stag hunt game only. It is a book about *correlation*: the stag hunt is the test case because it is the simplest game where correlation flips the outcome.
- Taking the local-interaction result as unconditional. It depends on imitation dynamics; with best response, local interaction accelerates the risk-dominant outcome.
- Believing cheap talk solves the prisoner's dilemma. In the book it helps the stag hunt substantially and the PD only transiently.
- Assuming "signals evolve meaning almost surely" for all signaling games. Proved for the simplest symmetric case; partial pooling appears in richer cases.
- Treating the models as descriptions of human psychology. They are dynamics over behaviour with no beliefs or motives; the learner needs Bicchieri and Camerer for the psychology, and Ostrom for the institutions.
- Assuming the payoff-dominant equilibrium is "the rational one" and that its failure to appear is irrationality. Under uncertainty the risk-dominant choice is defensible; the failure is structural, and so is the fix.
