# Games of Strategy — Avinash Dixit, Susan Skeath & David McAdams (5th ed., Norton 2020; David Reiley co-authored eds. 3–4)
Level: Mechanisms  ·  Priority: Core
Est. reading time saved: ~25h  ·  Your time with this file: ~45 min

## The book in one sentence
The standard undergraduate toolkit for reasoning about situations where your best move depends on what others will do: it teaches you to draw the game, find its equilibria, and then use commitment, information, repetition, and rule-design to change which equilibrium you land in.

## The book in one paragraph
Dixit, Skeath and McAdams build game theory from the ground up without heavy math. Part One sets the vocabulary (players, strategies, payoffs, information, sequential vs. simultaneous moves). Part Two gives the core solution concepts: rollback (backward induction) for sequential games, Nash equilibrium and dominance for simultaneous games, and mixed strategies when no pure equilibrium exists. Part Three covers the broad classes of games that matter most for social life: strategic moves (commitments, threats, promises), uncertainty and information (signaling and screening), the prisoners' dilemma and the repeated-game logic that can rescue cooperation, collective-action problems with many players, and evolutionary games where "strategies" are inherited rather than chosen. Part Four applies the toolkit to brinkmanship, incentive design (principal-agent contracts), auctions, voting, and bargaining. Throughout, the authors flag where laboratory evidence diverges from the theory's predictions and treat that gap as information about real people, not a reason to abandon the models.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
Read it with this question: **for the group I care about, what game are the members actually playing, and which lever — payoffs, information, sequencing, repetition, or rules — would move them to a better equilibrium?** Every other book in the curriculum either explains where the payoffs and beliefs come from (Henrich, Laland, Gilovich), shows that real people deviate from the equilibrium predictions (Camerer, Schelling), or describes institutions that change the game (Ostrom, Bicchieri, Skyrms, Easley & Kleinberg). This book gives you the formal language those arguments are conducted in. Without it, "incentives," "commitment," "credible threat," "focal point," "signaling," and "Nash equilibrium" stay as buzzwords rather than diagnostic tools.

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

### Part One / Ch 1–2 — Basic Ideas and Examples; How to Think about Strategic Games
- Core claims: A "game" is any situation where outcomes depend on the choices of two or more decision-makers who are aware of each other. Strategic thinking means putting yourself in others' shoes and reasoning about what they will do, given what they think you will do.
- The classification questions to ask about any interaction: (1) Are moves sequential or simultaneous? (2) Is the game zero-sum (pure conflict) or is there common interest? (3) Is it played once or repeatedly, with the same or changing partners? (4) Do players have full information about each other's payoffs and past moves? (5) Are the rules fixed, or can players manipulate them? (6) Is cooperation enforceable by contract, or must it be self-enforcing?
- Vocabulary fixed here: players, strategies (complete contingent plans, not single actions), payoffs (numbers that rank outcomes; ordinal is usually enough), rationality (consistent pursuit of your own payoffs, whatever they are — altruism is allowed), common knowledge of rules, and equilibrium (a situation where each player's strategy is a best response to the others').
- Key examples: choosing a route home when others do too, a penalty kick, a study-group free-rider problem, the "guess two-thirds of the average" game used to show levels of reasoning.
- So-what: the six classification questions are the first diagnostic pass on any group. Most cooperation failures in real teams are misdiagnosed because people treat a repeated, incomplete-information, mixed-motive game as if it were a one-shot pure-conflict game (or vice versa). "Non-cooperative" in the technical sense does not mean hostile; it means agreements must be self-enforcing — which is exactly the situation most communities and platforms are in.

### Part Two / Ch 3 — Games with Sequential Moves
- Setup: players move in turn and later movers see earlier moves. Draw a game tree: nodes (decision points), branches (actions), terminal nodes with payoffs. A strategy is a full plan of what to do at every node you might reach.
- Result (rollback / backward induction): start at the last decisions, assume each player picks their best branch, prune the rest, and work backward. The path that survives is the rollback equilibrium. Equilibrium strategies include what a player *would* do at nodes never actually reached — those off-path plans are what make earlier choices rational.
- Worked example: an incumbent firm faces a potential entrant. Entrant moves first (Enter / Stay out). If Enter, incumbent chooses Fight (price war) or Accommodate. Payoffs (entrant, incumbent): Stay out → (0, 10); Enter then Accommodate → (3, 5); Enter then Fight → (-2, 2). Rollback: at the incumbent's node, Accommodate (5) beats Fight (2), so the entrant anticipates accommodation and enters. The incumbent's "we'll fight" talk is not credible — which is the seed of Chapter 8.
- Additional results: first-mover vs. second-mover advantage depends on the game (commitment helps in some, flexibility in others); in games with perfect information and finite length, rollback always finds an equilibrium (Zermelo's theorem in spirit — chess has one, we just can't compute it); the *centipede game* shows rollback predicting immediate defection where humans in the lab cooperate for several rounds, a first flag that theory and behavior diverge.
- So-what: in group design, sequencing is a lever. Who moves first, and who can see what, changes the equilibrium. Rollback also disciplines wishful thinking: "if we do X, will the other side really respond the way we hope, given *their* payoffs?"

### Part Two / Ch 4 — Simultaneous-Move Games: Discrete Strategies
- Setup: players choose at the same time (or without seeing each other's choice). Represent as a payoff matrix. A **dominant strategy** is best no matter what others do; a **dominated strategy** is never best. Iterated elimination of dominated strategies shrinks the game.
- Result (Nash equilibrium): a profile of strategies where no player can gain by unilaterally switching. Find it by the best-response method: for each row, mark the column player's best cell; for each column, mark the row player's best cell; cells marked twice are Nash equilibria.
- Worked example, prisoners' dilemma (payoffs are years in prison, lower is better; row player first):

| | Confess | Deny |
|---|---|---|
| **Confess** | 10, 10 | 1, 25 |
| **Deny** | 25, 1 | 3, 3 |

Confess dominates Deny for both (10<25 and 1<3). Unique Nash equilibrium: (Confess, Confess) at 10,10 — worse for both than (Deny, Deny) at 3,3. This is the template for every "individually rational, collectively dumb" outcome.
- Other canonical matrices introduced here: **coordination** (two pure equilibria, players want to match — e.g. which standard to adopt); **assurance / stag hunt** (two equilibria, one better for both but riskier); **battle of the sexes** (two equilibria, players disagree about which); **chicken** (two equilibria, each prefers the other yields); and games with no pure-strategy equilibrium (matching pennies). Multiple equilibria are the norm, not the exception, and the theory alone often cannot say which one will be played. The book introduces **focal points** (Schelling) as the answer: an equilibrium that stands out by convention, culture, or salience.
- So-what: most cooperation problems are not prisoners' dilemmas. Diagnose which matrix you're in. A stag hunt is fixed by assurance (reducing fear that others will defect); a battle-of-the-sexes is fixed by a fair tie-breaking rule; a chicken game is fixed by commitment or by turn-taking; only a genuine PD needs changed payoffs or repetition. Misclassifying a stag hunt as a PD leads to heavy-handed enforcement where cheap reassurance would do.

### Part Two / Ch 5 — Simultaneous-Move Games: Continuous Strategies, Discussion, and Evidence
- Setup: when strategies are quantities (price, effort, contribution), draw each player's **best-response function** — the best choice as a function of what the other does — and the Nash equilibrium is where the curves cross.
- Key models: price competition between two firms (Bertrand), quantity competition (Cournot), and the political-science "median voter" logic of two candidates converging on the center.
- The "Discussion and Evidence" sections are important for this curriculum: the authors survey what happens when the Nash assumptions fail. Players may not have common knowledge of rationality; they may make errors; equilibria may be many. They review level-k reasoning (the "guess two-thirds" game: a Nash player guesses 0, real players guess around 20–35), learning toward equilibrium over repeated play, and ultimatum-game rejections of unfair offers. The stance: Nash is a benchmark; deviations are systematic and can themselves be modeled. Camerer's file carries the detail.
- Also introduced: **rationalizability** (strategies that survive iterated elimination of never-best-responses) as a weaker but more defensible concept than Nash.
- So-what: for continuous decisions like effort or contribution, ask what each member's best response is to others' levels. If best-response curves slope up (strategic complements — my effort is worth more when yours is high), the group can tip to a high- or low-effort equilibrium; if they slope down (substitutes — I slack when you work), you get free-riding.

### Part Two / Ch 6 — Combining Sequential and Simultaneous Moves
- Setup: real games mix both. Represent with trees that contain simultaneous-move subgames (information sets), or with matrices whose entries are full contingent strategies.
- Result: **subgame-perfect equilibrium** — a Nash equilibrium that also gives an equilibrium in every subgame, which rules out incredible threats. Changing the order of moves changes the outcome; making a simultaneous game sequential can create first- or second-mover advantage; making a sequential game simultaneous (hiding information) can remove one.
- Worked example: in chicken, if one driver can visibly commit first (rip out the steering wheel), the sequential version has a unique equilibrium where the other swerves. The same payoffs played simultaneously have two pure equilibria plus a mixed one.
- So-what: the *rules about who observes what and when* are a design variable. Sealed-bid vs. open-outcry, blind vs. visible pledges, synchronous vs. asynchronous decisions — each changes the game's equilibrium set.

### Part Two / Ch 7 — Simultaneous-Move Games with Mixed Strategies
- Setup: when no pure-strategy equilibrium exists (or players want to be unpredictable), players randomize. A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over pure strategies.
- Result: in a mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium, each player's mix makes the *opponent* indifferent among the pure strategies in the opponent's mix. That is the "opponent's indifference" property, and it produces a counterintuitive fact: your equilibrium mix depends on *the other player's* payoffs, not your own. Nash (1950) showed every finite game has at least one equilibrium once mixed strategies are allowed.
- Worked example, a simplified penalty kick (kicker's success probability; kicker maximizes, goalie minimizes):

| | Goalie dives Left | Goalie dives Right |
|---|---|---|
| **Kick Left** | 0.5 | 0.9 |
| **Kick Right** | 0.8 | 0.6 |

Kicker chooses p = Pr(Left) so goalie is indifferent: 0.5p + 0.8(1-p) = 0.9p + 0.6(1-p) → 0.8 − 0.3p = 0.6 + 0.3p → p = 1/3. Goalie chooses q = Pr(Left) so kicker is indifferent: 0.5q + 0.9(1-q) = 0.8q + 0.6(1-q) → q = 3/4. Kicker scores 70% in equilibrium.
- Evidence: field studies of professional penalty takers and tennis servers (Palacios-Huerta 2003; Walker & Wooders 2001 — from background knowledge; the book cites this literature) find pros mix close to equilibrium proportions, though they tend to switch too often (negative serial correlation) rather than randomizing truly. Lab novices do worse.
- Mixed strategies in non-zero-sum games are shakier: they exist but often make no one better off and are unstable to small payoff changes.
- So-what: unpredictability is a strategy — random audits, random inspection, rotating who checks the commons. For enforcement design, the mixed-equilibrium logic says: to make cheating unattractive, you do not need to catch everyone; you need the probability-weighted penalty to equal the gain. And the indifference property warns that raising *your* payoff to enforcing does not change *their* cheating rate in equilibrium — it changes yours.

### Part Three / Ch 8 — Strategic Moves
- Setup: a strategic move is an action taken *before* the main game to change its outcome by changing the other players' expectations. Three kinds, following Schelling: **commitment** (unconditional: "I will do X"), **threat** (conditional response to their bad action), **promise** (conditional reward for their good action). Threats and promises are response rules.
- Central result: a strategic move works only if it is **credible** — the other side must believe you would actually carry it out when the time comes. Since carrying out a threat is usually costly to the threatener too, credibility must be manufactured: by contracts, reputation, cutting off your own options (burning bridges), delegation to an agent with different incentives, breaking a big move into small steps, brinkmanship (Ch 13), or automatic mechanisms (doomsday devices). The paradox: reducing your own freedom of action can increase your power.
- Worked example: return to the entry game in Ch 3. The incumbent's threat to fight was incredible because, once entry has happened, accommodating (5) beats fighting (2). Suppose the incumbent signs a contract that pays a penalty of 4 to a third party if it ever accommodates an entrant. Now after entry, Fight (2) beats Accommodate (5 − 4 = 1); the entrant rolls this back and stays out; the incumbent gets 10 and never pays the penalty. Its threat became credible by making its own alternative worse.
- Other results: threats should be no larger than needed (excessive threats are less credible and invite escalation); promises must be sized so keeping them is cheaper than the reputation loss of breaking them; a combined threat-and-promise is often needed when the other side has multiple bad options; deterrence (stop them doing something) vs. compellence (make them do something) call for different move types; countering another's strategic move includes irrationality, cutting communication, and undermining their credibility.
- Behavioral note: in the lab, people carry out costly threats and honor costly promises more than pure self-interest predicts, which is why cheap-talk promises sometimes work — an anchor for Bicchieri's and Camerer's material.
- So-what: for group design, this chapter is the theory of *credible rules*. A norm with a penalty nobody would actually impose is not a norm. Design so that the enforcer's own payoff at the moment of enforcement favors enforcing (delegate to a rotating monitor, make sanctions graduated and cheap to apply — compare Ostrom's principles).

### Part Three / Ch 9 — Uncertainty and Information
- Setup: players may face external risk (nature's moves) and, more importantly, **asymmetric information** — one side knows something (its type, its quality, its intentions) the other does not. The chapter covers attitudes to risk and the value of information, then the two workhorse mechanisms: **signaling** (the informed party takes a costly action to reveal their type) and **screening** (the uninformed party designs choices that induce the informed party to reveal themselves).
- Core result: a signal is credible only if it is *too costly to fake* — the cost of sending it must differ across types so that only the "good" type finds it worthwhile. Education as a signal of ability (Spence 1973), warranties as a signal of quality, and expensive-to-forge commitments are the classic cases. Cheap talk conveys information only when interests are sufficiently aligned. Equilibria come in flavors: **separating** (types send different signals and are identified), **pooling** (all types act alike, no information revealed), and semi-separating (mixed).
- Worked example: an employer will pay 100 to a high-ability worker and 50 to a low-ability one but cannot tell them apart. A hard certification costs the high type 20 in effort and the low type 60. If the employer pays 100 only to certified workers: the high type certifies (100 − 20 = 80 > 50) and the low type does not (100 − 60 = 40 < 50). Separating equilibrium; the certification works even if it teaches nothing. If the certification cost 40 for both types, both would certify and it would reveal nothing (pooling).
- Also: adverse selection (the market for lemons — Akerlof 1970: when quality is hidden, bad types drive out good), moral hazard (hidden action), and the Bayesian updating players use to revise beliefs after observing signals. The chapter extends Nash equilibrium to **Bayesian Nash** and **perfect Bayesian equilibrium** (beliefs must be consistent with strategies along the path and reasonable off it).
- So-what: every group with heterogeneous members has an information problem: who is a cooperator, who is competent, who is committed? Costly signals (initiation rituals, time-consuming onboarding, visible sunk contributions) and screening menus (tiered membership, probation) are the formal versions of what Henrich calls credibility-enhancing displays and what Ostrom sees in boundary rules. Design the signal so that it is cheap for the types you want and expensive for the types you don't.

### Part Three / Ch 10 — The Prisoners' Dilemma and Repeated Games
- Setup: the PD's unique equilibrium is mutual defection. Three families of solutions: **repetition**, **penalties/rewards** (changing payoffs), and **leadership** (a large player who finds it worthwhile to cooperate unilaterally).
- Core result (repetition): with an infinite or indefinite horizon and enough weight on the future, cooperation can be sustained as an equilibrium by *contingent strategies* — tit-for-tat, grim trigger — because the one-period gain from cheating is outweighed by the discounted loss of future cooperation. With a *known finite* horizon, rollback unravels cooperation from the last round backward: no cooperation in any round. The key variables: the discount factor / continuation probability, the size of the temptation relative to the cooperative payoff, and the ability to detect and respond to defection.
- Worked example: per-round payoffs (row, column): both cooperate → (3,3); both defect → (1,1); defect against cooperate → (5,0). Under grim trigger (cooperate until the other defects, then defect forever), cheating once yields 5 now and 1 forever after; cooperating yields 3 forever. With discount factor δ per round, cooperation holds if 3/(1−δ) ≥ 5 + δ·1/(1−δ), i.e. 3 ≥ 5(1−δ) + δ → δ ≥ 1/2. So cooperation is an equilibrium if the players value next period at least half as much as this one (equivalently, if the chance the game continues is at least 50%). Raising the temptation to 8 needs δ ≥ 5/7; more temptation demands more shadow of the future.
- Axelrod's tournaments (1980s, background knowledge; described in the book): tit-for-tat won by being nice, retaliatory, forgiving, and clear — but TFT is not uniquely optimal, is vulnerable to noise (two TFT players who misread one move fall into endless mutual punishment), and "contrite" or generous variants do better under noise.
- Evidence: in lab PDs, cooperation runs around 30–60% even in one-shot play, and rises with communication, with sequential moves, and with smaller groups; cooperation decays over rounds in finite repeated public-goods games but is restored by the option to punish (Fehr & Gächter 2000). Detail in Camerer's file.
- So-what: this is the chapter that formalizes the "shadow of the future." Group design levers: make interaction repeated and indefinite (no announced endpoints), make defection observable quickly, keep groups small enough that reputations travel, and ensure punishments are proportionate and forgiving so noise does not trigger feuds. When repetition is impossible (one-shot, anonymous), change the payoffs directly or find a leader who benefits enough to carry the cost.

### Part Three / Ch 11 — Collective-Action Games
- Setup: many players, each choosing whether to contribute (participate, restrain, vote, show up). Model with two curves: the payoff to a participant and the payoff to a shirker, each as a function of how many others participate. Collective-action problems come in three shapes: **PD** (shirking is dominant for each; everyone shirks), **chicken** (someone must act; each hopes another will), and **assurance** (I contribute if enough others do; two equilibria).
- Core result: the equilibrium number of contributors is where the two payoff curves cross (individual best response), while the *social optimum* maximizes total payoff and is generally higher. The gap is the externality: each contributor confers benefits on others they do not internalize. The book gives a clean spillover formula — the marginal social benefit of one more contributor equals the private benefit plus the change in everyone else's payoffs.
- Worked example (schematic): a commons where n herders each choose whether to add a cow. Each cow yields 10 to its owner but reduces every other cow's yield by 1. With 10 herders, the private calculation says add (10 > 0); the social calculation says the 10th cow adds 10 and costs 9 others one each, net +1 — barely worthwhile; the 12th cow would add 10 and cost 11 — socially negative — yet each herder still adds. Result: overgrazing (Hardin's "tragedy of the commons," 1968).
- Solutions catalogued: Olson's *logic of collective action* (small groups and selective incentives); large-player leadership; repeated interaction and reciprocity; norms enforced by social sanction (shame, ostracism), which the authors treat as changing the payoffs; and Ostrom's field evidence that communities often solve commons problems without external enforcers — the book explicitly points to her work. The chapter also introduces **tipping** via Schelling's diagrams of participation payoff curves: when the payoff to joining rises with the number joining, small pushes can flip the group from a low to a high equilibrium.
- So-what: this chapter is the bridge to the Order books. It tells you to (a) identify which of the three shapes your collective-action problem is, (b) estimate where the payoff curves cross, and (c) choose a lever accordingly: selective incentives for a PD, a rotation or pre-assigned responsibility for chicken, and assurance mechanisms (visible pledges, thresholds, matching) for stag-hunt shapes. It also formalizes why norms are powerful: an internalized norm shifts the participant payoff curve up, moving the crossing point.

### Part Three / Ch 12 — Evolutionary Games
- Setup: drop the rationality assumption. Players are "programmed" with strategies (phenotypes, habits, cultural rules); those with higher payoffs reproduce or are imitated more; the population mix changes over time. A strategy is an **evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS)** (Maynard Smith & Price 1973) if a population playing it cannot be invaded by a small group of mutants playing something else.
- Core results: every ESS is a Nash equilibrium of the underlying game, but not every Nash equilibrium is an ESS (ESS is a refinement). In a PD, Always-Defect is the ESS; TFT can be stable in a repeated PD against invasion by Always-Defect if the game lasts long enough, but neutral drift lets nicer strategies creep in. In chicken / hawk-dove, the ESS is a *mix* of hawks and doves (or, equivalently, a polymorphic population), with the proportion of hawks rising with the prize and falling with the cost of fighting. In assurance / stag hunt games, both pure equilibria are ESS and which one the population reaches depends on the starting mix — history and basins of attraction matter.
- Worked example, hawk-dove with prize V = 4 and fighting cost C = 6 (payoff to row vs. column):

| | Hawk | Dove |
|---|---|---|
| **Hawk** | (4−6)/2 = −1 | 4 |
| **Dove** | 0 | 2 |

Hawk against Hawk loses on average; Dove against Hawk gets nothing. All-Hawk is invadable by Dove (0 > −1); all-Dove is invadable by Hawk (4 > 2). The stable mix has Hawk fraction h such that both types earn the same: −h + 4(1−h) = 0·h + 2(1−h) → 4 − 5h = 2 − 2h → h = 2/3. Two-thirds hawks. Raise C to 10 and h falls to 4/7.
- The chapter also treats the *replicator dynamics* graphically, cooperation via assortment (cooperators who preferentially interact with cooperators can invade), and the "bourgeois" strategy (fight if you are the owner, yield if not) as an ESS that generates property-like conventions out of an arbitrary asymmetry — a bridge to Skyrms.
- So-what: this is the formal engine behind Henrich, Laland and Skyrms. Norms and habits do not need to be chosen rationally to be stable; they need to be uninvadable. For design, ask what would happen if a few members adopted a different behavior — would it spread or die? If your cooperative equilibrium is a stag hunt, it is stable only inside its basin; a shock that pushes enough people to defect can flip the whole group, and getting back requires coordinated rather than individual moves.

### Part Four / Ch 13 — Brinkmanship: The Cuban Missile Crisis
- Setup: brinkmanship is a strategic move using a *probabilistic* threat: rather than a certain punishment that is too costly to be credible, the player deliberately creates a risk that things will get out of hand — "the threat that leaves something to chance" (Schelling). The player controls the *level* of risk, not the outcome.
- Result: brinkmanship works when there is a range of risk levels that is (a) high enough that the other side prefers to back down, and (b) low enough that the threatener prefers running the risk to conceding. Both thresholds depend on unknown resolve, so the tool is to raise the risk gradually and let the other side reveal its type. Failure (the risk actually materializing) is a genuine possibility, not a mistake, and both sides may be worse off ex post.
- The Cuban missile crisis (1962) is worked in detail: Kennedy's naval quarantine as a risk-raising step short of attack, the multiple decision-makers on each side creating genuine chance elements, and the eventual mutual concession. The chapter is careful that the "rational actor" model is one of several readings of the crisis (Allison's organizational and bureaucratic models are the alternatives).
- So-what: in organizational and community life, brinkmanship shows up as escalating disputes where each side raises the temperature hoping the other backs down. The lesson for design is the reverse: build off-ramps and graduated steps so parties can escalate a little without triggering catastrophe, and recognize that the point of ambiguity is to make a threat *credible* — which is why unclear-but-serious sanctions sometimes work better than precise ones.

### Part Four / Ch 14 — Incentive Design
- Setup (principal-agent model): a principal wants an agent to work hard but cannot observe effort, only noisy output. Design a contract that pays on observables. The **participation constraint** (the agent must accept the job) and the **incentive-compatibility constraint** (given the contract, the agent must choose the desired effort) jointly determine the contract.
- Results: if effort is observable, pay a flat wage conditional on effort. If not, pay must depend on output, which shifts risk onto a risk-averse agent, so the principal must pay a risk premium; the optimal contract trades incentive strength against risk-sharing. Under **hidden information** (the agent knows their own type), the principal offers a menu of contracts and the types self-select — the screening logic of Chapter 9 turned into mechanism design. Multiple tasks and multiple agents raise further problems: incentives on one measurable task crowd out unmeasured ones; tournaments (relative pay) filter out common noise but invite sabotage. The 5th edition expanded this material with McAdams's market-design perspective.
- Worked example: an agent's effort is high or low; output is "good" with probability 0.8 under high effort and 0.4 under low. High effort costs the agent 10. Pay bonus b only on good output. Incentive compatibility: 0.8b − 10 ≥ 0.4b → b ≥ 25. A risk-neutral principal happily pays 25 conditional on success; a risk-averse agent needs more expected pay than 0.8 × 25 = 20 to accept the gamble, which is the cost of unobservability.
- So-what: for a team or platform, this chapter says: pay on what you can measure only if what you can measure tracks what you want; otherwise you buy gaming. Where output is noisy and members are risk-averse, strong incentives are expensive, and norm-based motivation (intrinsic, reputational) is cheaper — but the chapter also warns that adding explicit incentives can *crowd out* those motivations (cited as evidence, detail in Camerer and Gilovich).

### Part Four / Ch 15 — Auctions, Bidding Strategy, and Auction Design
- Setup: auctions are games of incomplete information. Formats: English (ascending, open), Dutch (descending), first-price sealed bid, second-price sealed bid (Vickrey), plus all-pay auctions. Value environments: **private values** (each bidder knows their own value) vs. **common value** (the object is worth the same to all but each has a noisy estimate).
- Results: in a second-price auction with private values, bidding your true value is a dominant strategy (Vickrey 1961) — you never affect what you pay, only whether you win. In a first-price auction you shade your bid below value, more so with fewer rivals. The **revenue equivalence theorem**: under standard assumptions (risk-neutral bidders, independent private values), all four formats yield the seller the same expected revenue. The **winner's curse** in common-value auctions: the winner is the one with the most optimistic estimate, so winning is bad news; rational bidders shade to compensate, but lab and field bidders often do not (oil-lease and free-agent evidence).
- Worked example: two bidders with private values 10 and 6 in a second-price auction. Each bids their value. The high bidder wins at 6. If the high bidder had bid 8 instead, they still win and still pay 6; if they had bid 5, they would lose a profitable object. Nothing is gained by deviating — truthfulness is dominant.
- Design side (McAdams's contribution): reserve prices, entry fees, collusion-proofing (bidding rings are easier in open ascending formats), and the FCC spectrum auction and school-milk collusion cases. All-pay auctions model lobbying, patent races, and contests where losers still pay.
- So-what: allocation problems inside groups (who gets scarce slots, resources, tasks) are auctions in disguise. Second-price / Vickrey-style rules make honesty the easy choice; open formats reveal information but invite collusion; all-pay structures (compete for status by visible effort) generate waste. When members are estimating a common value (how much a project will really cost), beware the winner's curse — the most enthusiastic volunteer is the most likely to have overestimated.

### Part Four / Ch 16 — Strategy and Voting
- Setup: voting rules are games. Rules covered: plurality, runoff, pairwise majority (Condorcet), Borda count, approval voting, and single transferable vote.
- Core results: the **Condorcet paradox** (with three voters and three options, majority preferences can cycle A > B > C > A, so "the group's preference" may not exist); **Arrow's impossibility theorem** (1951: no rule for aggregating rankings satisfies a short list of reasonable conditions simultaneously); the **Gibbard–Satterthwaite** result (any non-dictatorial rule with three or more options can be manipulated by strategic — insincere — voting); the **median voter theorem** for single-peaked preferences on one dimension (the median voter's ideal point wins pairwise majority and cannot be manipulated). Agenda-setting power: whoever controls the order of pairwise votes can often determine the outcome when preferences cycle.
- Worked example, cycle: three members rank three options. Alice: A > B > C. Bob: B > C > A. Carol: C > A > B. Pairwise: A beats B (Alice, Carol), B beats C (Alice, Bob), C beats A (Bob, Carol). Whichever pair is voted on last wins; an agenda-setter who wants A schedules B vs. C first (B wins), then A vs. B (A wins).
- So-what: any group that "decides by vote" has a rule that can be gamed, and the choice of rule is itself a decision that shapes outcomes. Design implications: make preferences single-peaked where possible (decide one dimension at a time), use rules whose manipulation is hard or harmless (approval voting is relatively robust), publish the agenda process, and treat "the group wants X" with suspicion when preferences are multidimensional.

### Part Four / Ch 17 — Bargaining
- Setup: two parties split a surplus that exists only if they agree. Two approaches: the **cooperative / axiomatic** Nash bargaining solution (1950) — the split that maximizes the product of each side's gains over their outside option (BATNA), with weights reflecting bargaining power — and the **non-cooperative** alternating-offers model (Rubinstein 1982; Ståhl) where impatience determines the split.
- Core results: in Nash bargaining, each party gets their outside option plus a share of the remaining surplus; improving your BATNA is the most reliable way to improve your deal. In alternating offers, the more patient party gets more; with equal patience and rapid offers the split converges to 50/50; the first mover has a small advantage that vanishes as the delay between offers shrinks. Delay and breakdown are inefficient, so in complete-information models agreement is immediate — real delays (strikes, stalled negotiations) are explained by incomplete information (each side testing the other's resolve) or by strategic commitment (Ch 8).
- Worked example: a buyer values a used bike at 300; the seller's outside option is 100. Surplus = 200. With equal bargaining power the Nash solution splits the surplus: price 200. If the buyer can credibly show another bike available at 220, the buyer's outside option rises (surplus from this deal now 300 − 220 = 80 vs. seller's 200 − 100 = 100 — the model then splits the gains relative to those thresholds and the price falls toward 160).
- Also covered: multi-issue bargaining (trade across issues where valuations differ — the source of integrative "win-win" deals), multi-party bargaining, and the manipulation of BATNAs and information as strategic moves. Behavioral evidence: ultimatum-game responders reject low offers and proposers offer near 40–50%, far from the subgame-perfect prediction — again pointed to Camerer.
- So-what: internal group conflicts (dividing credit, workloads, budget) are bargaining games. Improving outside options changes deals more than clever tactics; fairness norms set the reference point that real people bargain around; and delay is usually a sign of information problems, which transparency fixes more cheaply than pressure.

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. **Draw the game before arguing about the outcome.** Players, strategies, payoffs, order of moves, information. Most disagreements about "why people won't cooperate" dissolve once the matrix is written down.
2. **Nash equilibrium is a consistency condition, not a prediction of goodness.** Mutual defection in a PD is an equilibrium precisely because it is stable, not because anyone wants it. "Rational" outcomes can be terrible.
3. **Most cooperation problems are not prisoners' dilemmas.** Stag hunts, chicken, and battle-of-the-sexes each fail differently and need different fixes (assurance, turn-taking, tie-break rules) rather than enforcement.
4. **Credibility is manufactured, usually by limiting yourself.** Threats and promises work only if carrying them out is in your interest at the time. Commitment devices, delegation, reputation and small steps create that interest.
5. **The shadow of the future rescues cooperation only when it is long, uncertain, and observable.** δ ≥ temptation/(temptation − punishment) in the simple case; announced endpoints unravel cooperation; noise requires forgiving strategies.
6. **Information asymmetry is solved by costly signals and self-selecting menus.** A signal works when it is cheaper for the good type than the bad type; a screen works when it makes each type reveal itself by choice.
7. **Collective action has three shapes and the fix depends on the shape.** Find where the participant and shirker payoff curves cross; social optimum is higher; norms shift the curves.
8. **Stability without rationality: evolutionarily stable strategies.** A norm persists if it cannot be invaded, not because anyone chose it; which of several stable norms you get depends on where you started.
9. **Rules are games too.** Voting rules, auction formats, contract structures and agenda control shape outcomes and can all be gamed; the honest-is-dominant designs (Vickrey, median-voter on single-peaked issues) are rare and valuable.
10. **The theory is a benchmark, and the deviations are systematic.** Real players cooperate more in one-shot PDs, reject unfair splits, over-bid in common-value auctions, and reason at limited depth. These are facts to design with, not noise.

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **Strategy** — a complete contingent plan covering every decision point, not a single move — bites when people evaluate a rule only by what happens on the expected path, ignoring what it commits them to off-path.
- **Rollback / backward induction** — solve sequential games from the end — bites when you ask "and then what will they do?" one step further than intuition.
- **Nash equilibrium** — no one can gain by unilaterally deviating — bites in diagnosing why a bad state is sticky.
- **Dominant / dominated strategy** — best (or never best) regardless of others — bites in spotting when a problem is really a PD.
- **Subgame-perfect equilibrium** — Nash in every subgame; rules out incredible threats — bites when evaluating whether a rule's sanction would actually be applied.
- **Mixed strategy / opponent's indifference** — randomize so the other is indifferent; your mix depends on their payoffs — bites in designing random audits and in understanding why raising your own stake does not change their behavior.
- **Focal point** — equilibrium selected by salience or convention — bites whenever a group has multiple ways to coordinate and needs a default.
- **Strategic move (commitment, threat, promise)** — pre-game action that changes others' expectations; must be credible — bites in every rule-enforcement design.
- **Signaling / screening; separating vs. pooling** — costly actions that reveal type; menus that induce self-revelation — bites in membership, hiring, and trust-building.
- **Discount factor / shadow of the future** — weight on future payoffs; the price of defection — bites in deciding whether repetition alone can hold a group together.
- **Trigger strategies (grim, tit-for-tat)** — contingent punishment rules; trade harshness for robustness to noise — bites in designing graduated sanctions.
- **Collective-action shapes (PD / chicken / assurance)** — what many-player participation games look like — bites in choosing between incentives, assignments and assurance.
- **Externality / spillover** — effect of my action on others' payoffs not reflected in mine — bites as the root cause of every commons problem.
- **ESS and basin of attraction** — uninvadable strategy; the set of starting mixes that lead to it — bites in judging whether a norm will survive a shock.
- **Winner's curse** — in common-value settings, winning implies overestimation — bites in volunteer allocation and project estimates.
- **BATNA / outside option** — what you get without agreement; sets bargaining floor — bites in every internal negotiation.
- **Incentive compatibility / participation constraint** — the contract must make the desired action the agent's own best choice, and be worth accepting — bites in any pay-for-performance or points scheme.

## Evidence strength & limits
- **What is robust:** the mathematics. Existence of Nash equilibria (Nash 1950), rollback in finite perfect-information games, the ESS conditions, Vickrey dominance, Arrow and Gibbard–Satterthwaite, revenue equivalence under its assumptions — these are theorems, and the book's exposition of them is standard and reliable. The folk-theorem logic of repeated games (cooperation sustainable when δ is high enough) is also a theorem, but note it is an *existence* result: it says cooperation *can* be an equilibrium, alongside many other equilibria including permanent defection. The theory does not say which will occur.
- **What is well-supported empirically:** mixed-strategy play by professionals in zero-sum sports settings (Walker & Wooders 2001; Palacios-Huerta 2003) is close to equilibrium; winner's-curse over-bidding is repeatedly observed in labs and in field data on oil leases; cooperation in repeated PDs rises with continuation probability (Dal Bó 2005; Dal Bó & Fréchette 2011 — background knowledge, not verified for this file); median-voter and agenda-control effects are widely documented in legislatures. Lab results on ultimatum, dictator, and public-goods games with punishment (Fehr & Gächter 2000) have replicated broadly, though effect sizes vary across cultures (Henrich et al. 2001, the 15-small-scale-societies study).
- **What the theory gets wrong or leaves open:** (1) *Equilibrium selection* — with multiple equilibria, the theory is silent; focal points, learning, and evolutionary dynamics are appended to fill the gap, and the book is honest that this is patchwork. (2) *Depth of reasoning* — humans reason a few steps (level-k, cognitive hierarchy) rather than to a fixed point; the centipede game and the two-thirds game show this clearly. (3) *Social preferences* — the book's stance that "rationality" accommodates any payoffs is formally correct but practically thin: it tells you to put fairness into the payoffs without telling you how much or when; Camerer's file and Bicchieri's file supply the content. (4) *Backward induction* itself is philosophically contested (what should a player believe when they reach a node that rollback says should never be reached?); the book mentions but does not resolve this. (5) The repeated-game rescue of cooperation requires observability and patience that many real groups lack, and with more than two players the theory of who punishes whom gets complicated — Ostrom's monitoring principles are the empirical answer.
- **Author stance:** this is a textbook, so it argues less than it shows, but the authors do take positions: that rationality is a useful benchmark even when violated; that deviations should be modeled, not used to discard the framework; and (in Part Four) that market and mechanism design is game theory's most practical payoff. Critics from behavioral and evolutionary camps would say the benchmark framing privileges the model over the data; critics from within economics would say the book underplays the fragility of many-equilibrium results. The Cuban missile crisis chapter is explicitly one interpretation among several.
- **Replication status:** the game-theory lab literature the book draws on (ultimatum, PD, public goods) is among the better-replicated parts of experimental social science; the classic social-psychology results are not the subject of this book, so the replication-crisis issues live in Gilovich's file.

## Design implications for cooperation in real groups
- **Write the matrix.** Before choosing any intervention, name the players, their real strategy options, and their payoffs as *they* see them; identify whether the shape is PD, stag hunt, chicken, battle of the sexes, or coordination. [E] The shapes are formally distinct and have different remedies.
- **Prefer assurance over enforcement when the game is a stag hunt.** Visible pledges, thresholds ("we start when 10 sign up"), and matching commitments move a group from the risk-dominant to the payoff-dominant equilibrium at low cost. [E] (formal) / [H] for any specific group's actual shape.
- **Make sanctions credible by making them cheap and subgame-perfect.** A sanction that costs the enforcer more than ignoring the violation will not be applied and will not deter. Graduated, low-cost, quickly-applied responses and rotating or delegated enforcers keep the threat on the equilibrium path. [E]
- **Lengthen and blur the shadow of the future.** Avoid announced end dates for collaborations; keep membership stable; ensure defection is observed within a round or two. [E] (theory + Dal Bó-style lab evidence) / [H] for the magnitude in a particular group.
- **Design forgiveness into reciprocity.** Grim-trigger norms are fragile to noise; use contrite or generous tit-for-tat equivalents (one warning, then escalation). [E] (Axelrod-era simulation, noise results).
- **Use costly signals to sort types at the boundary.** Onboarding effort, probationary contribution, and time-consuming rituals separate committed members from opportunists when the cost is asymmetric across types. [E] (signaling theory) / [H] that any given ritual has the asymmetry you need.
- **Screen with menus rather than interrogations.** Tiered roles or contribution options let members self-select into commitment levels honestly. [E] (mechanism design) / [H] in practice.
- **Randomize monitoring.** A known probability of inspection with a proportionate penalty deters as well as full monitoring at a fraction of the cost; the required probability is gain/penalty. [E]
- **Change the order of moves when it helps.** Sequential, visible commitment (one party goes first) solves chicken and some coordination problems; simultaneous, sealed choices prevent bandwagon and intimidation. [E] for the formal effect / [H] for which is better in your case.
- **Choose decision rules deliberately.** For contested multi-option choices, use approval voting or decide one dimension at a time (to approximate single-peakedness); publish agenda rules. [E] (Condorcet/Gibbard–Satterthwaite) / [V] that manipulation-resistance should be prioritized over other properties.
- **Allocate scarce resources with truth-telling-dominant rules.** Vickrey-style or deferred-acceptance mechanisms make honesty the easy choice and reduce politicking. [E]
- **Beware measuring what is measurable.** Pay-for-performance on partial metrics displaces unmeasured effort; where output is noisy, rely more on norms and reputation and less on explicit bonuses. [E] (multi-task principal-agent theory; crowding-out evidence is more mixed) / [H] for your setting.
- **Treat the equilibrium the theory predicts as a floor, not a forecast.** Real groups often start more cooperative than the PD predicts; the design task is frequently to *protect* initial cooperation from decay rather than to create it from zero. [E] (lab decay curves) / [V] that protecting the cooperative default is the right target.

## Retrieval practice

1. **(Recall)** What is the difference between a strategy and a move, and why does the distinction matter for evaluating a rule?
<details>A strategy is a complete contingent plan specifying what to do at every decision point that could arise; a move is a single action. It matters because a rule's credibility depends on what it commits you to at nodes that are not on the expected path — the off-path responses are what deter the other side.</details>

2. **(Explain why)** Why is mutual defection the equilibrium in a one-shot prisoners' dilemma even though both players prefer mutual cooperation?
<details>Because defection is a dominant strategy: whatever the other does, each player is better off defecting. Cooperation is not a best response to anything, so no profile including it is stable against unilateral deviation. Preferring an outcome does not make it an equilibrium.</details>

3. **(Apply)** A volunteer group needs eight people to run an event; each person prefers the event to happen but would rather not be one of the eight. Which collective-action shape is this, and what is the cheapest fix?
<details>If the event happens whenever at least eight show up and each person's payoff is higher when they attend *given* enough others attend, it is an assurance (stag hunt) game with a threshold; the fix is an assurance mechanism — public pledges or a conditional commitment ("I'm in if seven others are") — not incentives or sanctions. If instead each person strictly prefers others do it regardless, it is chicken and needs assignment or rotation.</details>

4. **(Spot the misconception)** "To make my threat credible I should make it as severe as possible."
<details>Wrong. Severity reduces credibility when carrying out the threat is costly to the threatener; the other side reasons that you would not follow through. Threats should be just large enough to deter and structured so that executing them is in your interest at the time (commitment, delegation, small steps), or made probabilistic (brinkmanship).</details>

5. **(Compute)** Per-round PD payoffs: mutual cooperation 4, mutual defection 1, defect-against-cooperator 6, cooperate-against-defector 0. Under grim trigger, what continuation probability makes cooperation an equilibrium?
<details>Cooperate: 4/(1−δ). Defect once: 6 + δ/(1−δ). Cooperation holds when 4 ≥ 6(1−δ) + δ = 6 − 5δ, so δ ≥ 2/5 = 0.4.</details>

6. **(Explain why)** In a mixed-strategy equilibrium, why does raising the goalie's payoff from a save not change how often the goalie dives left?
<details>Because each player's equilibrium mix is set to make the *other* player indifferent. The goalie's mix is pinned by the kicker's payoffs; changing the goalie's own payoffs changes the kicker's mix instead.</details>

7. **(Apply)** A community wants to distinguish members who will stick around from tourists. Propose a signal and explain what makes it work.
<details>Require a contribution that is cheap for committed members and expensive for tourists — e.g. a multi-week task before privileges unlock, or sustained participation over time. It works only if the cost differs by type so that the committed type finds it worthwhile and the tourist does not (separating equilibrium). A cost that is equal for both types yields pooling and reveals nothing.</details>

8. **(Recall)** State the winner's curse and the environment in which it arises.
<details>In common-value auctions (the object is worth the same to everyone but estimates are noisy), the winner is the bidder with the highest estimate, which is likely an overestimate. Winning is bad news about value; rational bidders shade their bids to compensate, real bidders frequently do not.</details>

9. **(Spot the misconception)** "Game theory shows that rational people cannot cooperate."
<details>No. It shows cooperation is not an equilibrium of a one-shot PD, but it also shows cooperation *is* an equilibrium in indefinitely repeated PDs with enough patience and observability, in assurance games, and under credible sanctions or norms. The theory's main message is that whether cooperation is stable depends on the structure of the game, which is designable.</details>

10. **(Apply)** Three co-founders must choose among three strategies A, B, C. Their rankings are A>B>C, B>C>A, C>A>B. One of them proposes voting pairwise, "starting with the two most controversial." What should the others notice?
<details>Preferences cycle (Condorcet paradox), so whichever option is placed in the final pairwise vote wins. The proposer's control of the agenda decides the outcome. They should either use a rule less sensitive to agenda (e.g. approval voting or Borda) or restructure the question so preferences become single-peaked on one dimension.</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- **"It's about competition."** The subtitle-level framing is strategy, but the bulk of the content is about coordination, cooperation and mechanism design. Zero-sum games are a small special case.
- **"Nash equilibrium predicts what people will do."** It identifies stable profiles; with multiple equilibria it makes no prediction, and lab play often departs from it. The book itself says this in the "Discussion and Evidence" sections; readers skip them.
- **"Rationality means selfishness."** The book defines rationality as consistent pursuit of one's own payoffs, whatever they contain. Fairness, spite and altruism are allowed inside the payoffs; the framework does not tell you to assume them away, though it also does not tell you how to fill them in.
- **"Tit-for-tat is the optimal strategy."** It won Axelrod's tournaments but is not uniquely optimal, is fragile under noise, and the folk theorem shows many strategies sustain cooperation. What matters is the properties (nice, retaliatory, forgiving, clear), not the specific rule.
- **"Commitment means stubbornness."** A strategic move only works if it is credible *and* observed *and* understood by the other side. Private resolve does nothing; a commitment nobody can verify is not a commitment.
- **"Signals must be informative to work."** A costly signal (education, initiation) can sort types even if it transmits no substantive content. What matters is the cost differential across types.
- **"Repetition solves the PD."** Repetition *permits* cooperation as one equilibrium among many; it does not select it. Groups also need a way to coordinate on the cooperative equilibrium and to detect defection fast enough. That selection problem is the subject of the Order-level books.
