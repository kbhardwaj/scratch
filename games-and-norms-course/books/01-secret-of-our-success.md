# The Secret of Our Success: How Culture Is Driving Human Evolution, Domesticating Our Species, and Making Us Smarter — Joseph Henrich (Princeton University Press, 2016)
Level: Origins  ·  Priority: Core
Est. reading time saved: ~12h  ·  Your time with this file: ~35 min

## The book in one sentence
Humans dominate the planet not because individuals are smart but because we are a cultural species: we copy selectively, accumulate know-how across generations in a "collective brain," and that accumulating culture has been the main force shaping our genes, our psychology and our capacity for large-scale cooperation.

## The book in one paragraph
Henrich argues that the standard picture (big brains → clever individuals → tools, language, cooperation) has the causality backwards. Our ancestors crossed a threshold where learning from others paid better than figuring things out alone. Cultural evolution then took over: it produced adaptive practices no individual understands (food detoxification, kayak design, hunting taboos) and created new selection pressures on genes, favouring brains built for absorbing culture, longer childhoods, longer lives and a norm-following psychology. Cooperation is downstream: norms, kinship rules, rituals and prestige hierarchies are cultural products sifted by competition between groups, and they "self-domesticated" us into a species that feels shame, tracks reputations and punishes cheats. The rate of accumulation depends on the size and interconnectedness of the learning population; isolated groups lose skills, connected groups innovate. The payoff is a reframing of intelligence, innovation and institutions as properties of networks rather than individuals.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
Read it with one question: **where do the norms, preferences and learning habits that every later book takes as given come from, and what does that origin imply about how they can be changed?** Game theory (Dixit, Camerer, Gintis) models players who already have preferences; Bicchieri and Ostrom study norms and institutions that already exist; Easley and Kleinberg model networks whose nodes already know what they want. Henrich explains why humans arrive pre-loaded with conformity, prestige-deference, over-imitation, shame and a taste for punishing violators, and why the social network is the engine of a group's competence, not just a channel. The message for a designer: you are designing for cultural learners inside a collective brain, and the levers are models, prestige, networks and norm-enforcement, not arguments.

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

### Ch 1 — A Puzzling Primate
- Core claims: Humans are ecologically dominant yet physically feeble, and "we're smart" does not survive inspection. Thesis: the secret is cumulative cultural evolution plus the gene-culture coevolution it triggered. Culture is not decoration on a clever ape; it is the process that built the ape. Two inheritance systems interact, and culture became the driver once it accumulated faster than genes could track.
- So-what: Start any group diagnosis with "what does this group know collectively that no member knows individually, and how does that knowledge move?" That is Henrich's unit of analysis.

### Ch 2 — It's Not Our Intelligence
- Core claims: Compared like for like, individual humans are not dramatically smarter than other apes on general problem solving; the gap that matters is in social learning. Adult humans in "natural" tests (spatial memory, quantity, causal reasoning) often perform similarly to chimpanzees.
- Key study: Herrmann, Call, Tomasello and colleagues (Science, 2007) tested 2.5-year-old children, chimpanzees and orangutans on physical and social cognition batteries. On space, quantity and causality the children roughly matched the chimps; on social learning (imitation, joint attention, reading intent) they were far ahead. The chimpanzee Ayumu's working-memory feats (Inoue and Matsuzawa, 2007) puncture the "smarter ape" story.
- So-what: Do not rely on each member reasoning their way to the right behaviour. Build for people who are excellent at copying well-chosen models and weak at solo derivation. Behaviour that is visible and attributed to respected people spreads; behaviour that is only argued for usually does not.

### Ch 3 — Lost European Explorers
- Core claims: When well-equipped, intelligent, motivated Europeans were stranded in environments where indigenous groups thrived, they usually died. The reason was not intelligence but the absence of a locally adapted cultural package that took generations to accumulate.
- Key cases: the Franklin expedition (1845, Arctic; well provisioned, died while Netsilik Inuit lived nearby with tools and skills the British could not reproduce); Burke and Wills (1860–61, Australia; ate nardoo fern as Aboriginal people did but without the leaching and roasting that neutralise its thiaminase, and starved); the Narváez expedition and Cabeza de Vaca (1528, Gulf Coast); and the contrast case, Roald Amundsen, who apprenticed himself to the Netsilik and survived the Northwest Passage.
- So-what: Competence in a new environment is a property of an inherited cultural package, not of the people in it. A talented new team in an unfamiliar domain will fail unless connected to a living lineage of practice (mentors, incumbents, communities of practice). "Hire smart people and let them figure it out" is the Franklin strategy.

### Ch 4 — How to Make a Cultural Species
- Core claims: Humans are equipped with evolved cultural-learning biases that decide *whom* to copy and *what* to copy. Because these biases are statistical rather than deliberative, learners acquire adaptive practices without understanding them.
- The biases (Henrich's taxonomy, building on Boyd and Richerson): 
  - Model-based (who): copy the skilled and successful; copy the prestigious (those whom others defer to); copy the self-similar (same sex, ethnicity, age); copy elders.
  - Content-based (what): attend preferentially to food, danger, sex, norm violations, fire.
  - Frequency-based: conformist transmission (copy the majority disproportionately when uncertain).
  - Credibility-enhancing displays (CREDs): beliefs are copied more when the model acts as if they are true at personal cost (Henrich, 2009).
- Key studies: children's selective trust experiments (e.g. copying the model who was previously accurate; attending to those others look at); over-imitation, in which children faithfully copy causally irrelevant actions on a puzzle box (Horner and Whiten, 2005, chimps vs children; Lyons, Young and Keil, 2007, who coined the term); Morgan et al. (2012) showing adults conform more when uncertain and when the majority is larger, consistent with a conformist bias.
- So-what: Your group's behaviour is being set by who is visibly successful and prestigious, what the majority appears to do, and whether leaders' costly actions match their words. Those three channels are your primary design levers. Slide decks about values are a weak fourth.

### Ch 5 — What Are Big Brains For? Or, How Culture Stole Our Guts
- Core claims: Once cultural know-how (cooking, tools, food processing) started doing digestive and ecological work, selection favoured bigger brains for cultural learning, smaller guts, longer childhoods and longer lifespans. Brain expansion is an effect of culture as much as a cause.
- Key ideas: the expensive-tissue hypothesis (Aiello and Wheeler, 1995): gut shrank as brain grew, enabled by externally processed food; Wrangham's cooking hypothesis; extended childhood as a learning investment; the "cultural brain hypothesis" in which the value of learning from others drives brain size and sociality together.
- So-what: Investment in learning capacity pays only if there is a rich stock of know-how to learn and time to absorb it. Onboarding length and the density of things worth learning should co-vary; a long apprenticeship in a thin knowledge base is waste, a short one in a deep base is the Franklin problem.

### Ch 6 — Why Some People Have Blue Eyes
- Core claims: Culture changes the environment that selects genes. Cultural practices are the leading edge; genes follow. Culturally acquired adaptations also exist that are purely cultural yet more effective than anything an individual could invent.
- Key examples: lactase persistence spreading after dairying; light skin and eyes in northern farmers whose cereal diets lacked vitamin D (the title's blue eyes); multistep Tukanoan processing of bitter manioc that removes cyanogenic compounds whose harm takes years to appear (unlearnable by individual trial and error); nixtamalization of maize with alkali, which releases niacin, and the pellagra epidemics when maize reached Europe and the American South without it.
- So-what: The most valuable practices are often those whose benefits are delayed, diffuse and invisible to the practitioner. People abandon them when they "don't see the point" unless norms, ritual or authority protect them. Design defensively for practices whose payoff is too slow for individual learning to sustain.

### Ch 7 — On the Origin of Faith
- Core claims: Because cultural packages are smarter than individuals, natural selection favoured a psychology that trusts inherited practice over one's own causal reasoning. Over-imitation and "faith" in tradition are features, not bugs. Individuals who tinkered with detox procedures on the basis of their own understanding died.
- Key examples and studies: manioc again; Fijian food taboos in pregnancy and lactation that reduce exposure to fish-borne toxins, held by women who give non-causal justifications (Henrich and Henrich, 2010); divination as a randomizing device, e.g. Naskapi scapulimancy for choosing hunting directions (Moore, 1957), which stops hunters' predictability being exploited.
- So-what: People follow practices they cannot justify and confabulate reasons on demand. So: do not assume an unexplained practice is useless; check its selection history before removing it (Chesterton's fence, evolutionary edition). And do not expect explanation to create adherence; adherence comes from models and norms.

### Ch 8 — Prestige, Dominance, and Menopause
- Core claims: Humans have two distinct routes to status. **Dominance** is ape-inherited: fear-based, enforced by threat, marked by expansive posture and approach-avoidance. **Prestige** is a human cultural adaptation: freely conferred deference to those seen as skilled or knowledgeable, paid so as to get proximity and copying access. Prestigious people are watched, imitated, given influence and are expected to be generous and humble in return. Long post-reproductive life (menopause, grandparenting) is explained partly by the value of old people as repositories of accumulated knowledge.
- Key studies: Henrich and Gil-White (2001) on prestige as deference paid for information goods; Cheng, Tracy, Henrich and colleagues (2013) showing that in small task groups both dominant and prestigious members gained influence via different behaviours and with different attentional signatures; skill-acquisition curves (e.g. hunting returns peaking decades after physical peak) and the grandmother hypothesis (Hawkes and colleagues).
- So-what: Influence in your group runs on two systems. Dominance is fast, produces compliance under observation, and suppresses information flow (nobody copies or informs the bully). Prestige is slower, produces voluntary copying and honest signalling, and scales. Route norms through prestigious models and protect prestige from capture by dominance. Your longest-tenured members are storage, not overhead, if there are channels for copying them.

### Ch 9 — In-Laws, Incest Taboos, and Rituals
- Core claims: Social norms arise as cultural products that recruit and extend evolved kin and pair-bond psychology. Kinship terminology, marriage rules, incest taboos and post-marital residence norms shape who cooperates with whom and expand cooperation beyond genetic relatives. Rituals (synchrony, costly initiation) create bonds and mark norm commitment. Norms are psychologically real: people track them, feel violations, and third parties are motivated to sanction.
- Key material: cross-cultural variation in kinship systems; incest taboos as the Westermarck aversion recruited and widened by norms; synchrony studies (Wiltermuth and Heath, 2009; suggestive, small samples); three-year-olds spontaneously enforcing rules on third parties (Rakoczy, Warneken and Tomasello, 2008).
- So-what: A norm is a *shared expectation with teeth*: a visible pattern, a belief that others expect compliance, and some sanction, even if only disapproval. Rituals and initiations are the technology that makes commitment common knowledge. Bicchieri (08) gives the anatomy; Henrich gives the origin.

### Ch 10 — Intergroup Competition Shapes Cultural Evolution
- Core claims: Because norms and institutions vary across groups and affect group success, competition between groups selects for cooperation-enhancing norms. This "cultural group selection" operates through several channels, not just war: differential group survival, differential migration into successful groups, differential imitation of successful groups' practices, differential reproduction, and prestige-biased transmission between groups. Cultural group selection does not require genetic group selection; norms and punishment stabilise within-group variation so that between-group differences persist.
- Key cases: the 19th-century Nuer expansion at Dinka expense (Kelly, 1985), attributed to Nuer kinship and bridewealth norms that allowed larger coalitions; the Ilahita Arapesh in New Guinea (Tuzin), who grew from a small village to an unusually large one by adopting and modifying a neighbouring group's Tambaran men's cult, using ritual to bind sub-groups into a cooperating whole.
- So-what: Institutions that cooperate at scale were usually copied from a more successful neighbour and modified, not designed. Benchmark the best peer group and copy its *norm package*, because the package was itself selected. And note that within-group cooperation was forged by between-group rivalry; a group with no meaningful external comparison has weak pressure toward internal cooperation.

### Ch 11 — Self-Domestication
- Core claims: Once norms and reputation-tracking existed, they created a new selection environment: individuals who impulsively violated norms were ostracised, denied mates and allies, or killed. Genes for norm-sensitivity, shame, guilt, self-control, and reduced reactive aggression were favoured. Humans "domesticated themselves" into a docile, norm-internalising species with an evolved **norm psychology**: we learn rules easily, expect others to follow them, feel bad when we break them, and are willing to pay to punish those who do.
- Key evidence: children's early rule learning and third-party enforcement; cross-cultural economic games showing costly punishment (Henrich and colleagues' multi-society studies; Herrmann, Thöni and Gächter, 2008, on antisocial punishment); ethnographic evidence of execution and ostracism in small-scale societies (Boehm); physical markers of domestication, argued more fully in Wrangham's later work.
- So-what: You are working with a species that wants to follow norms and wants to punish, but whose norm psychology is content-neutral: it internalises productive and destructive norms with equal ease, and in some environments punishes cooperators. Fill the norm slot deliberately and early, because it will be filled by something.

### Ch 12 — Our Collective Brains
- Core claims: The rate of cumulative cultural evolution depends on the number of minds in the learning network and how well connected they are. Larger, better connected populations preserve more complex skills (because more learners can find the rare expert and copy them) and innovate faster (because innovation is recombination of existing ideas across people). Small or isolated populations can *lose* technology.
- Key cases and studies: Tasmania after separation from Australia ~10,000 years ago, where the archaeological record is read as showing loss of bone tools, cold-weather clothing and fishing (Henrich, 2004, formal model); the Polar Inuit of northwest Greenland, who after an 1820s epidemic lost kayaks, bows and leisters until contact with Baffin Island Inuit in the 1860s; Powell, Shennan and Thomas (2009) on demography and Upper Palaeolithic behavioural modernity; Kline and Boyd (2010), tool-kit complexity across Oceanic islands rising with population size and contact; Muthukrishna and Henrich (2016); lab transmission-chain experiments (Derex et al., 2013; Muthukrishna et al., 2014) in which larger learner groups maintained more complex skills.
- So-what: The book's most actionable chapter. Group capability is a network property with three levers: size of the pool that actually shares knowledge; connectivity between subteams; transmission fidelity (can a learner watch a skilled model closely enough to copy?). Silos, isolation and turnover do not just slow a group; they cause skill loss. Diverse sources plus dense connection is the recipe for innovation; isolation is the recipe for Tasmania.

### Ch 13 — Communicative Tools with Rules
- Core claims: Language is not the cause of culture but a product of cumulative cultural evolution: languages are culturally evolved tools whose features (vocabulary size, phonemic inventory, grammatical complexity, number words, colour terms) are shaped by the size and connectedness of speaker communities. Communication norms (turn-taking, honesty within the group) coevolved with cooperation.
- Key material: cross-linguistic evidence that larger speech communities have larger phoneme inventories and simpler morphology; societies without exact number words above two or three (e.g. Pirahã, per Everett and Gordon); gesture and shared attention as scaffolds for speech.
- So-what: Shared vocabulary is a cooperation technology. Groups that coin precise terms for their recurring situations coordinate better; the terms spread through the same prestige and conformity channels as everything else. Name what you want people to notice and enforce.

### Ch 14 — Enculturated Brains and Honorable Hormones
- Core claims: Culture reaches down into biology within a lifetime. Learning to read rewires the brain (visual word-form area, shifts in face recognition, corpus callosum changes). Cultural norms about honour change endocrine responses to insult. Culture shapes perception, pain, placebo response and even tastes.
- Key studies: Dehaene and colleagues on literate vs illiterate brains; the culture-of-honour experiments of Cohen, Nisbett and colleagues (1996), in which insulted Southern US students showed larger cortisol and testosterone rises than Northerners (small samples; the regional homicide pattern is better established than the lab effect); placebo and expectancy effects.
- So-what: What a group treats as an insult, a fair share or an acceptable risk is trained into physiology, not layered over fixed biology. Installed culture is more powerful than incentives and slow to reverse. Do not model members as having stable, culture-free utility functions (a theme that resurfaces in Camerer and Gintis).

### Ch 15 — When We Crossed the Rubicon
- Core claims: There is a threshold (the "Rubicon") beyond which cultural evolution becomes self-sustaining: once a population's stock of cultural know-how is rich enough that learning from others beats individual learning, selection favours better cultural learners, who accumulate more know-how, which favours better learners still. The book sketches how our lineage crossed it, likely well before modern humans and before full language.
- Key material: autocatalytic feedback between sociality (group size, tolerance, pair-bonding), cultural learning and brain size; ecological pushes (drier climates, terrestrial life, meat and tools); the threshold as the reason cumulative culture is rare among animals although social learning is common.
- So-what: The same feedback loop exists in organisations. Below the threshold, learning from colleagues is not worth an individual's time and knowledge stays private; above it, learning from others dominates and the group compounds. Getting a group across (enough shared, visible, copyable practice) is a distinct design goal from optimising one already across.

### Ch 16 — Why Us?
- Core claims: Why this lineage? A conjunction of contingent starting conditions rather than a single trick: large, tolerant social groups; terrestriality; bipedalism freeing hands; pair-bonding and paternal investment creating multiple kin-based teachers; meat-sharing and tool use raising the payoff to learning. Other social learners (cetaceans, corvids) lack the combination.
- So-what: Cumulative know-how needs several preconditions at once: proximity, tolerance (safe to approach and watch), permission to copy, and time. Any one missing breaks the loop; check each rather than assuming knowledge "wants to be shared."

### Ch 17 — A New Kind of Animal
- Core claims: Innovation is mostly recombination in a collective brain; intelligence is partly downloaded from culture (the Flynn effect read as cultural evolution acting on cognition); institutions and psychologies coevolve. Individual-centred theories of innovation, rationality and institutions misdescribe humans. Foreshadows Henrich's later program (The WEIRDest People in the World, 2020).
- So-what: Designing cooperation means designing an evolutionary process: variation (people try things), selection (what gets copied and what gets sanctioned), transmission (who sees whom). The group's future norms will be whatever wins that process, with or without you.

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. **Cumulative cultural evolution, not individual intelligence, explains human competence.** Know-how accumulates across generations through selective copying; no individual could derive it, and lost explorers without it died.
2. **The collective brain.** Innovation rate and skill retention scale with the size and interconnectedness of the learning population; isolation causes skill loss (Tasmania, Polar Inuit).
3. **Cultural learning biases are the levers.** Success, prestige, conformity, self-similarity and CREDs determine what spreads. Persuasion is a weak channel by comparison.
4. **Prestige is different from dominance.** Freely conferred deference buys copying access and scales cooperation; coerced status suppresses information flow. Route through prestige.
5. **Over-imitation and faith in tradition are adaptive.** Practices with delayed or invisible payoffs survive only because people copy without understanding. Scrutinise unexplained practices before removal; do not expect explanation to produce adherence.
6. **Gene-culture coevolution.** Culture changes the selection environment for genes (lactase, pigmentation, brain size, lifespan); human minds are built for culture, which is why cultural learning is fast and automatic.
7. **Norm psychology and self-domestication.** Norms plus reputation plus sanction selected for a species that internalises rules, feels shame, and pays to punish. The psychology is content-neutral.
8. **Cultural group selection.** Cooperative norms spread because groups with them out-competed, out-grew and were imitated by groups without. Institutions are mostly copied and modified, not designed.
9. **Culture reaches into physiology.** Literacy, honour and expectation are trained into brains and hormones; installed culture beats incentives and reverses slowly.
10. **The Rubicon.** Cultural accumulation is a threshold process; getting a group past the point where learning from others beats going alone is a distinct design problem.

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **Cumulative cultural evolution** — know-how ratchets up across generations because each learner starts from the best available model rather than from scratch. Bites when: a team assumes its competence is in its people rather than in its inherited practices and then loses it through turnover.
- **Collective brain** — the network of individuals who learn from each other, whose size and connectivity set the group's innovative and retentive capacity. Bites when: silos, remote isolation or attrition shrink the effective learning network below what the skill base needs.
- **Cultural learning biases** — evolved heuristics for whom and what to copy: success/skill, prestige, self-similarity, conformity, content (danger, food, norm violation). Bites when: the visibly successful person is doing the wrong thing.
- **Prestige** vs **dominance** — voluntary deference paid for copying access (attention, approach, imitation) vs status held by threat (avoidance, compliance). Bites when: managers mistake compliance under observation for adoption.
- **Over-imitation** — faithful copying of causally irrelevant steps; humans do it, chimpanzees do not. Bites when: process cargo-cults form, and when you strip "pointless" steps that were load-bearing.
- **Credibility-enhancing display (CRED)** — costly action that only makes sense if the model holds the belief, making the belief more copyable. Bites when: leaders say "quality first" and ship anyway.
- **Conformist transmission** — disproportionate copying of the majority under uncertainty. Bites when: a minority best practice cannot reach tipping point; a bad majority practice self-reinforces.
- **Gene-culture coevolution / dual inheritance** — two interacting inheritance systems; culture leads, genes follow. Bites when: a behaviour is called "just human nature" (fixed) or "just culture" (easy to change).
- **Norm psychology** — evolved readiness to learn, expect, internalise and enforce rules, including third-party sanction. Bites when: you assume rules need material incentives, or that people will enforce only the rules you intended.
- **Self-domestication** — selection against reactive aggression and norm violation once reputation and coalitional punishment existed. Bites when: designing sanctions; ostracism and reputational threat often move people more than fines.
- **Cultural group selection** — differential survival, growth, migration and imitation between groups with different norm packages. Bites when: benchmarking a peer organisation (copy the norm package, not only the tools).
- **Tasmanian effect / the Rubicon** — the two ends of the same feedback loop: skill loss when the learning network shrinks; self-sustaining accumulation once learning from others beats going alone.

## Evidence strength & limits
**Robust or well supported**
- Specialised human social learning that chimpanzees lack, with the gap opening in early childhood (Herrmann et al., 2007, and a large follow-on literature).
- Over-imitation: replicated in many labs, but most studies use the same Horner–Whiten puzzle box, and Aka forager children were found to emulate rather than over-imitate while Aka adults over-imitated (Berl and Hewlett, 2015), so developmental universality is less clear than the book implies.
- Prestige and dominance as distinct routes to influence (Cheng et al., 2013, and later work).
- Lactase persistence and similar gene-culture cases: strong genetic and archaeological evidence. Food-processing cases (manioc, maize, nardoo): well documented.
- Population size and tool-kit complexity in Oceania (Kline and Boyd, 2010): clean but correlational and single-region.

**Contested or argued more than shown**
- **The collective brain population-size claim.** The book's most disputed empirical pillar. Vaesen, Collard, Cosgrove and Roebroeks (PNAS, 2016) argued that the demographic models (Henrich, 2004; Powell et al., 2009) yield the predicted link only under implausible assumptions, that most empirical tests do not support it, and that the Tasmanian record has been misread. Henrich, Boyd, Derex, Kline, Mesoudi, Muthukrishna, Powell, Shennan and Thomas replied in PNAS that the critics misunderstood models that concern effective learning-network size and connectivity, not census size. Collard and colleagues (2005, 2013) found hunter-gatherer tool-kit complexity tracks environmental risk rather than population. Lab transmission-chain studies support the mechanism at small scale. Fair summary: plausible mechanism, lab-demonstrated; archaeological support mixed; Tasmania a live dispute, not a settled example.
- **Cultural group selection.** Defended by Richerson, Boyd, Henrich and colleagues in a 2016 Behavioral and Brain Sciences target article that drew many critical commentaries: is between-group variation large and stable enough, is imitation of successful groups really "selection," do within-group mechanisms (reciprocity, reputation, punishment) suffice? The Nuer and Ilahita cases are illustrative, not statistical.
- **Self-domestication.** The general idea (Wrangham, Hare) is respectable, but the specific claim that norm-enforcement drove selection for a norm psychology is a well-motivated hypothesis with indirect evidence (developmental, cross-cultural games, ethnographic accounts of ostracism), not a measured selection history.
- **Culture of honour hormones.** Cohen et al. (1996) had small samples and, as far as I know, has not been subjected to a large pre-registered direct replication; treat the lab result as suggestive. Regional homicide-pattern data are stronger.
- **Menopause / grandmother hypothesis and "elders as knowledge repositories."** Plausible and consistent with skill-acquisition curves, but the demographic evidence for the grandmother hypothesis is debated.
- **Synchrony and ritual effects; cross-cultural economic games.** The former are small-sample results with mixed replications. The latter (Henrich et al., 2010: offers vary with market integration and world religion) are robust in direction but contested in interpretation (see the Camerer and Gintis files).

**Argued vs. shown.** Henrich is a synthesiser: puzzle, study, mechanism that fits. He rarely gives the rival explanation equal force. The developmental and cross-species work is strongest; the deep-time narrative (chapters 5, 15, 16) is a coherent reconstruction. Specific population figures, dates and brain-size numbers in the book are not verified here.

## Design implications for cooperation in real groups
- **Map the collective brain before changing anything.** Who learns from whom, in practice? Where are single points of failure for critical skills? Which subteams are Tasmania? [E for the mechanism at lab scale; H for your group]
- **Increase connectivity before increasing headcount.** Cross-team pairing, rotations, demos and observable work raise the effective size of the learning network more cheaply than hiring. [H]
- **Identify and protect prestige.** Find whom people actually watch and copy (not the org chart). Give those people visibility, ask them to model the target behaviours, and shield them from being displaced by dominant personalities. [E for the prestige/dominance distinction; H for the intervention]
- **Route norms through models, not memos.** Ensure a prestigious, self-similar model is visibly doing the behaviour and visibly succeeding; and make leaders' CREDs point the right way, since one visible costly exception (shipping despite the "quality" rule) transmits the opposite norm. [E for the biases; H for effect size in your setting]
- **Use conformity deliberately.** Make target behaviours look like majority behaviour where they are (public counts) and avoid advertising low adoption, because "most people don't do this" is itself transmitted. [E for conformist bias; H for the tactic]
- **Fill the norm slot early.** Norm psychology attaches to whatever pattern is first and visible; the first weeks of observed behaviour set norms that later rules will fight. [H]
- **Design sanctions around reputation and inclusion, not just money.** Mild public peer disapproval is a strong lever and a dangerous one; pair it with a legitimate process to avoid antisocial punishment. [E for the sensitivity; V for the constraint]
- **Audit before removing "pointless" practices.** Ask whether the payoff is delayed or diffuse; remove only when you can trace why it existed. [H]
- **Do not expect explanation to produce adherence.** Budget for modelling, repetition and enforcement; treat "we explained it" as zero adoption. [E for the psychology; H as a practice rule]
- **Copy norm packages from successful peer groups, then adapt;** and build copying channels (shadowing, narrated work) from long-tenured members, who are storage. [H]
- **Value commitment: prefer prestige-based to dominance-based influence** even where dominance would be faster, because dominance suppresses the information flow the collective brain runs on. [V]

## Retrieval practice

1. (Recall) Name four of Henrich's cultural learning biases and give one situation where each could spread a *bad* practice.
<details>
Success/skill bias (the visibly successful person's shortcut gets copied), prestige bias (a respected person's idiosyncratic habit spreads), conformity (a bad majority practice becomes self-reinforcing), self-similarity bias (people copy same-role peers rather than the best available model), CREDs (a leader's costly exception transmits the opposite norm). Any four with plausible examples.
</details>

2. (Explain why) Why did Burke and Wills starve while eating the same nardoo fern that local Aboriginal groups thrived on?
<details>
They lacked the culturally accumulated processing steps (leaching and roasting) that neutralise the plant's thiaminase. The knowledge was not derivable by intelligent individuals in the time available; it had been accumulated over generations. The case shows competence is a property of the inherited cultural package.
</details>

3. (Apply) Your team of eight has one engineer who knows the deployment system. She is respected, quiet and leaving in three months. Using chapter 12, what do you do and why?
<details>
A Tasmania risk: a complex skill held by one node with no copying channel. Widen the learning network: two people shadow real deployments (observing a skilled model, not reading docs), she narrates decisions, and there are repeated low-stakes occasions to copy. Documentation aids fidelity but does not replace modelling.
</details>

4. (Spot the misconception) "Henrich shows that bigger populations always produce more complex technology, so growing our organisation will make it more innovative."
<details>
Two errors. First, the claim is about the size and connectivity of the *learning network*, not census size; growth without connection can shrink the effective collective brain. Second, the population-size claim is contested (Vaesen et al., 2016; Collard et al.), with mixed archaeological support; the lab evidence supports the mechanism at small scale only.
</details>

5. (Recall) What distinguishes prestige from dominance in behaviour and in consequences for information flow?
<details>
Dominance: fear-based, enforced by threat, marked by expansive posture and others' avoidance; produces compliance under observation. Prestige: freely conferred deference to skilled/knowledgeable people, marked by attention, approach and imitation, reciprocated by generosity. Prestige enables copying and honest signalling; dominance suppresses them because people avoid rather than approach the dominant.
</details>

6. (Explain why) Why does Henrich say "faith" in tradition is adaptive rather than a cognitive flaw?
<details>
Because cultural packages contain adaptations whose causal logic is opaque and whose payoffs are delayed (manioc detoxification, food taboos). Individuals who edited practices according to their own understanding did worse than those who copied faithfully, so selection favoured over-imitation and deference to tradition.
</details>

7. (Apply) A community moderation team wants members to flag misinformation. They have written clear guidelines and a tutorial. Adoption is 5 percent. Diagnose with Henrich.
<details>
Explanation is a weak channel. Ask: is a prestigious, self-similar member visibly flagging and benefiting? Does the interface make flagging look like majority behaviour or advertise low adoption? Are leaders producing CREDs? Is there any norm enforcement (expectation plus mild sanction) or only information? Fix by modelling, visible adoption and social expectation, not better tutorials.
</details>

8. (Recall) List three channels of cultural group selection besides warfare.
<details>
Differential migration into successful groups; differential imitation of successful groups' norms and institutions; differential reproduction or group growth; prestige-biased transmission between groups; differential group survival through non-violent means (e.g. famine resilience).
</details>

9. (Spot the misconception) "Because norm psychology evolved to sustain cooperation, people will naturally enforce the cooperative norms we set."
<details>
Norm psychology is content-neutral. People internalise and enforce whatever pattern is visible and expected, including harmful norms, and in some norm environments they punish cooperators (antisocial punishment, Herrmann et al., 2008). The designer must fill the norm slot deliberately and channel enforcement through a legitimate process.
</details>

10. (Explain why) In what sense did culture "steal our guts," and what does it imply for how much a group should invest in onboarding?
<details>
Cultural food processing (cooking, tools) did digestive work externally, letting selection shrink metabolically expensive gut tissue and expand brains for cultural learning, together with longer childhoods. The general principle: investment in learning capacity pays only when there is a rich stock of transmissible know-how. Onboarding length should scale with the depth of what the group actually has to transmit.
</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- **"Humans are dumb, culture is smart."** Henrich's point is comparative: individual cognition is not what separates us, and our cognition is itself built by and for culture. He is not claiming individual reasoning is worthless; he is claiming it is a minor input to cumulative know-how.
- **"The collective brain means the wisdom of crowds."** The wisdom of crowds is about aggregating independent judgments. The collective brain is about non-independent, selective copying over generations; it depends on connection and on people copying the *right* models, not on independence.
- **"Cultural group selection means groups fight and the losers die."** War is one channel; migration, imitation and differential growth are the others and probably dominate.
- **"Gene-culture coevolution means behaviour is genetically determined."** The direction of influence in the book runs mostly from culture to genes; and the cases (lactase, pigmentation) show genes responding to culturally created environments, not genes fixing behaviour.
- **"Over-imitation shows people are irrational."** It is an adaptive default that preserves opaque-but-useful practice; the same default also produces cargo cults. Hold both.
- **"The population-size story is settled."** It is the book's most contested empirical claim; lab support for the mechanism, disputed archaeology, and a reply to critics that reframes the variable as network connectivity rather than raw numbers.
- **"Institutions are designed by smart people."** Henrich argues most were copied and modified under group competition, with designers rationalising afterwards. Ostrom is the partial counterweight later in the course.
