# The Secret of Our Success: How Culture Is Driving Human Evolution, Domesticating Our Species, and Making Us Smarter — Joseph Henrich (Princeton University Press, 2016)
Level: Origins  ·  Priority: Core
Est. reading time saved: ~12h  ·  Your time with this file: ~35 min

## The book in one sentence
Humans dominate the planet not because individuals are smart but because we are a cultural species: we copy selectively, accumulate know-how across generations in a "collective brain," and that accumulating culture has been the main force shaping our genes, our psychology and our capacity for large-scale cooperation.

## The book in one paragraph
Henrich argues that the standard picture (big brains → clever individuals → tools, language, cooperation) has the causality backwards. Our ancestors crossed a threshold where learning from others paid better than figuring things out alone. Once that happened, cultural evolution took over: it produced adaptive practices no individual understands (food detoxification, kayak design, hunting taboos), and it created new selection pressures on genes, favoring bodies and brains built for absorbing culture, longer childhoods, longer lives, and a norm-following psychology. Cooperation is downstream of this: social norms, kinship rules, rituals and prestige hierarchies are cultural products that were sifted by competition between groups, and they in turn "self-domesticated" us into a species that feels shame, monitors reputations and punishes cheats. The rate of cultural accumulation depends on the size and interconnectedness of the population doing the learning; isolated groups lose skills, connected groups innovate. The book's payoff is a reframing of intelligence, innovation and institutions as properties of groups and networks rather than of individuals.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
This is the first Origins book. Read it with one question: **where do the norms, preferences and learning habits that every later book takes as given actually come from, and what does that origin story imply about how they can be changed?** Game theory (Dixit, Camerer, Gintis) models players who already have preferences and beliefs; Bicchieri and Ostrom study norms and institutions that already exist; Easley and Kleinberg model networks whose nodes already know what they want. Henrich explains why humans arrive at those tables pre-loaded with conformity, prestige-deference, over-imitation, shame and a taste for punishing norm violators, and why the structure of the social network is not just a channel for behaviour but the engine of the group's competence. For a cooperation designer the book's core message is: you are not designing for rational agents, you are designing for cultural learners inside a collective brain, and the levers that move them are models, prestige, networks and norm-enforcement, not arguments.

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

### Ch 1 — A Puzzling Primate
- Core claims: Humans are ecologically dominant yet physically feeble, and the usual explanation ("we're smart") does not survive inspection. The book's thesis: our species' secret is cumulative cultural evolution plus the gene-culture coevolution it triggered. Culture is not decoration on top of a clever ape; it is the process that built the ape.
- Key framing: two inheritance systems (genes and culture) that interact; culture became the driving force once it began accumulating faster than genes could track.
- So-what: Start any diagnosis of a group with the question "what does this group know collectively that no member knows individually, and how does that knowledge move?" That is the unit of analysis Henrich is asking you to adopt.

### Ch 2 — It's Not Our Intelligence
- Core claims: Compared like for like, individual humans are not dramatically smarter than other apes on general problem solving; the gap that matters is in social learning. Adult humans in "natural" tests (spatial memory, quantity, causal reasoning) often perform similarly to chimpanzees.
- Key study: Herrmann, Call, Tomasello and colleagues (Science, 2007) tested 2.5-year-old children, chimpanzees and orangutans on a battery of physical and social cognition tasks. On physical-world tasks (space, quantity, causality) the children roughly matched the chimps; on social learning tasks (imitation, joint attention, understanding intent) the children were far ahead. Henrich also uses the chimpanzee Ayumu's working-memory feats (Inoue and Matsuzawa, 2007) to puncture the "smarter ape" story.
- So-what: Don't build cooperation designs that rely on each member reasoning their way to the right behaviour. Build for people who are excellent at copying well-chosen models and weak at solo derivation. If the right behaviour is visible and attributed to respected people, it spreads; if it is only argued for, it usually does not.

### Ch 3 — Lost European Explorers
- Core claims: When well-equipped, intelligent, motivated Europeans were stranded in environments where indigenous groups thrived, they usually died. The reason was not intelligence but the absence of a locally adapted cultural package that took generations to accumulate.
- Key cases (from the book): the Franklin expedition (1845, Arctic; well-provisioned, died while Netsilik Inuit lived nearby using tools and skills the British could not reproduce); Burke and Wills (1860–61, Australia; the survivors ate nardoo fern as the Aboriginal people did but without the leaching and roasting steps that neutralize its thiaminase, and starved); the Narváez expedition and Cabeza de Vaca (1528, Gulf Coast); and, as the contrast case, Roald Amundsen, who deliberately apprenticed himself to the Netsilik and survived the Northwest Passage.
- So-what: Competence in a new environment is a property of an inherited cultural package, not of the people in it. When you drop a talented new team into an unfamiliar domain, expect them to fail unless you connect them to a living lineage of practice (mentors, incumbents, communities of practice). "Hire smart people and let them figure it out" is the Franklin strategy.

### Ch 4 — How to Make a Cultural Species
- Core claims: Humans are equipped with evolved cultural-learning biases that decide *whom* to copy and *what* to copy. Because these biases are statistical rather than deliberative, learners acquire adaptive practices without understanding them.
- The biases (Henrich's taxonomy, building on Boyd and Richerson): 
  - Model-based (who): copy the skilled and successful; copy the prestigious (those whom others defer to); copy the self-similar (same sex, ethnicity, age); copy elders.
  - Content-based (what): attend preferentially to food, danger, sex, norm violations, fire.
  - Frequency-based: conformist transmission (copy the majority disproportionately when uncertain).
  - Credibility-enhancing displays (CREDs): beliefs are copied more when the model acts as if they are true at personal cost (Henrich, 2009).
- Key studies: children's selective trust experiments (e.g. copying the model who was previously accurate; attending to those others look at); over-imitation, in which children faithfully copy causally irrelevant actions on a puzzle box (Horner and Whiten, 2005, chimps vs children; Lyons, Young and Keil, 2007, who coined the term); Morgan et al. (2012) showing adults conform more when uncertain and when the majority is larger, consistent with a conformist bias.
- So-what: Your group's behaviour is being set by who is visibly successful and prestigious, what the majority appears to do, and whether leaders' costly actions match their words. Those three channels are your primary design levers. Slide decks about values are a weak fourth.

### Ch 5 — What Are Big Brains For? Or, How Culture Stole Our Guts
- Core claims: Once cultural know-how (cooking, tools, food processing) started doing digestive and ecological work, selection favoured bigger brains for cultural learning, smaller guts, longer childhoods and longer lifespans. Brain expansion is an effect of culture as much as a cause.
- Key ideas: the expensive-tissue hypothesis (Aiello and Wheeler, 1995): gut tissue shrank as brains grew, made possible by externally processed food; Wrangham's cooking hypothesis; extended juvenile period as a learning investment; the "cultural brain hypothesis" in which the value of learning from others drives brain size and sociality together.
- So-what: Investment in learning capacity only pays if there is a rich stock of know-how to learn from and enough time to absorb it. In organisations: onboarding length and the density of things worth learning should co-vary; a long apprenticeship in a thin knowledge base is waste, a short one in a deep base is the Franklin problem.

### Ch 6 — Why Some People Have Blue Eyes
- Core claims: Culture changes the environment that selects genes. Cultural practices are the leading edge; genes follow. Culturally acquired adaptations also exist that are purely cultural yet more effective than anything an individual could invent.
- Key examples: lactase persistence spreading after dairying; light skin and eye pigmentation in northern farmers whose cereal diets lacked vitamin D (the blue eyes of the title); cultural detoxification of foods, such as the multistep Tukanoan processing of bitter manioc that removes cyanogenic compounds whose effects take years to appear (nobody could learn this by individual trial and error); nixtamalization of maize with alkali, which releases niacin, and the pellagra epidemics that followed when maize spread to Europe and the American South without the process.
- So-what: The most valuable practices in a group are often ones whose benefits are delayed, diffuse and invisible to the practitioner. Expect people to abandon them when they "don't see the point" unless the practice is protected by norms, ritual or authority. Design defensively for the practices whose payoff is too slow for individual learning to sustain.

### Ch 7 — On the Origin of Faith
- Core claims: Because cultural packages are smarter than individuals, natural selection favoured a psychology that trusts inherited practice over one's own causal reasoning. Over-imitation and "faith" in tradition are features, not bugs. Individuals who tinkered with detox procedures on the basis of their own understanding died.
- Key examples and studies: manioc again (Henrich's "you can't reason your way to that"); Fijian food taboos during pregnancy and lactation that reduce exposure to fish-borne toxins, held by women who give non-causal justifications (Henrich and Henrich, 2010); divination as a randomizing device, e.g. the Naskapi scapulimancy for choosing hunting directions (Moore, 1957) that prevents the hunters' predictability from being exploited by prey; the medieval European use of rituals whose effects worked without anyone understanding why.
- So-what: People in your group will follow practices they cannot justify and will confabulate reasons on demand. Two consequences. First, do not assume that a practice nobody can explain is useless; check its selection history before removing it (Chesterton's fence, in evolutionary dress). Second, do not expect explanation to create adherence; adherence comes from model choice and norms.

### Ch 8 — Prestige, Dominance, and Menopause
- Core claims: Humans have two distinct routes to status. **Dominance** is ape-inherited: fear-based, enforced by threat, marked by expansive posture and approach-avoidance. **Prestige** is a human cultural adaptation: freely conferred deference to those seen as skilled or knowledgeable, paid so as to get proximity and copying access. Prestigious people are watched, imitated, given influence and are expected to be generous and humble in return. Long post-reproductive life (menopause, grandparenting) is explained partly by the value of old people as repositories of accumulated knowledge.
- Key studies: Henrich and Gil-White (2001) on prestige as an information-goods deference system; Cheng, Tracy, Henrich and colleagues (2013) showing that in small task groups both dominant and prestigious members gained influence, but via different behaviours and with different attentional signatures; ethnographic evidence on skill acquisition curves (e.g. Tsimane hunting returns peaking decades after physical peak) and the grandmother hypothesis (Hawkes and colleagues).
- So-what: Influence in your group is running on two systems. Dominance works fast, produces compliance under observation, and suppresses information flow (nobody copies or informs the bully; they avoid). Prestige works slower, produces voluntary copying and honest signalling, and scales. If you want norms that people internalise and transmit, you must route them through prestigious models and protect prestige from being captured by dominance. Also: your longest-tenured members are storage, not overhead, if you build channels for their knowledge to be copied.

### Ch 9 — In-Laws, Incest Taboos, and Rituals
- Core claims: Social norms arise as cultural products that recruit and extend evolved kin and pair-bond psychology. Kinship terminology, marriage rules, incest taboos and post-marital residence norms shape who cooperates with whom and expand cooperation beyond genetic relatives. Rituals (synchrony, costly initiation) create bonds and mark norm commitment. Norms are psychologically real: people track them, feel violations, and third parties are motivated to sanction.
- Key material: cross-cultural variation in kinship systems and their consequences for cooperation; incest taboos as norm-extended aversion (Westermarck effect recruited and widened); ritual and synchrony studies (e.g. Wiltermuth and Heath, 2009, on synchrony and cooperation, a result that is suggestive but from small samples); developmental work showing that three-year-olds spontaneously enforce rules on puppets and third parties (Rakoczy, Warneken and Tomasello, 2008).
- So-what: A norm is a *shared expectation with teeth*; to install one you need (a) a visible pattern, (b) the belief that others expect compliance, and (c) some sanction, even if only disapproval. Rituals and initiations are not fluff; they are the technology that makes commitment common knowledge. Bicchieri (08) will give you the precise anatomy; Henrich gives the origin.

### Ch 10 — Intergroup Competition Shapes Cultural Evolution
- Core claims: Because norms and institutions vary across groups and affect group success, competition between groups selects for cooperation-enhancing norms. This "cultural group selection" operates through several channels, not just war: differential group survival, differential migration into successful groups, differential imitation of successful groups' practices, differential reproduction, and prestige-biased transmission between groups. Cultural group selection does not require genetic group selection; norms and punishment stabilise within-group variation so that between-group differences persist.
- Key cases: the Nuer expansion at Dinka expense in the 19th century (Kelly, 1985), explained by Nuer kinship and bridewealth norms that allowed larger fighting coalitions; the Ilahita Arapesh in New Guinea (Tuzin), who scaled from a small village to a large one by adopting and modifying the Tambaran men's cult from a neighbouring group, using ritual to bind moieties into a cooperating whole.
- So-what: Institutions that cooperate at scale usually were not designed; they were copied from a more successful neighbour and modified. Practical version: benchmark against the best-performing peer group and copy its *norm package* (not just its tooling), because the package was itself selected. And understand that within-group cooperation was historically forged by between-group rivalry; a group with no meaningful external comparison or competitor has weak pressure toward internal cooperation.

### Ch 11 — Self-Domestication
- Core claims: Once norms and reputation-tracking existed, they created a new selection environment: individuals who impulsively violated norms were ostracised, denied mates and allies, or killed. Genes for norm-sensitivity, shame, guilt, self-control, and reduced reactive aggression were favoured. Humans "domesticated themselves" into a docile, norm-internalising species with an evolved **norm psychology**: we learn rules easily, expect others to follow them, feel bad when we break them, and are willing to pay to punish those who do.
- Key evidence: developmental studies of children's early rule learning and third-party enforcement; cross-cultural economic games showing willingness to punish (Henrich and colleagues' 15-society studies; Herrmann, Thöni and Gächter, 2008, on antisocial punishment); ethnographic evidence of capital punishment and ostracism in small-scale societies (Boehm); physical markers of domestication (reduced aggression, white sclera, softer facial features) argued more fully in Wrangham's later work.
- So-what: You are working with a species that wants to follow norms and wants to punish, but whose norm psychology is content-neutral: it will internalise a productive norm or a destructive one with equal ease. Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is real in some norm environments. The design task is to fill the norm slot deliberately and early, because it will be filled by something.

### Ch 12 — Our Collective Brains
- Core claims: The rate of cumulative cultural evolution depends on the number of minds in the learning network and how well connected they are. Larger, better connected populations preserve more complex skills (because more learners can find the rare expert and copy them) and innovate faster (because innovation is recombination of existing ideas across people). Small or isolated populations can *lose* technology.
- Key cases and studies: Tasmania after separation from Australia by rising seas ~10,000 years ago, where the archaeological record shows loss of bone tools, cold-weather clothing, fishing and other technologies (Henrich, 2004, formal model); the Polar Inuit of northwest Greenland, who after an epidemic in the 1820s lost kayaks, bows and leisters until contact with Baffin Island Inuit in the 1860s; Powell, Shennan and Thomas (2009) modelling demography and Upper Palaeolithic behavioural modernity; Kline and Boyd (2010) finding that tool-kit complexity across Oceanic island populations increases with population size and contact; Muthukrishna and Henrich (2016) "Innovation in the collective brain"; laboratory transmission-chain experiments (Derex et al., 2013; Muthukrishna et al., 2014) in which larger groups of learners maintained more complex skills.
- So-what: For a designer this is the book's most actionable chapter. Group capability is a network property. Three levers: (1) size of the pool that actually shares knowledge, (2) connectivity (are the subteams talking, is there cross-pollination?), (3) transmission fidelity (can a learner watch a skilled model closely enough to copy them?). Silos, isolation and high turnover do not just slow a group; they cause skill loss. Diversity of sources plus dense connection is the recipe for innovation; isolation is the recipe for Tasmania.

### Ch 13 — Communicative Tools with Rules
- Core claims: Language is not the cause of culture but a product of cumulative cultural evolution: languages are culturally evolved tools whose features (vocabulary size, phonemic inventory, grammatical complexity, number words, colour terms) are shaped by the size and connectedness of speaker communities. Communication norms (turn-taking, honesty within the group) coevolved with cooperation.
- Key material: cross-linguistic evidence that larger speech communities have larger phoneme inventories and simpler morphology; societies without exact number words above two or three (e.g. Pirahã, per Everett and Gordon); gesture and shared attention as scaffolds for speech.
- So-what: Shared vocabulary is a cooperation technology. Groups that coin precise terms for their recurring situations coordinate better; the terms spread through the same prestige and conformity channels as everything else. Name what you want people to notice and enforce.

### Ch 14 — Enculturated Brains and Honorable Hormones
- Core claims: Culture reaches down into biology within a lifetime. Learning to read rewires the brain (visual word-form area, shifts in face recognition, corpus callosum changes). Cultural norms about honour change endocrine responses to insult. Culture shapes perception, pain, placebo response and even tastes.
- Key studies: literacy studies by Dehaene and colleagues comparing literate and illiterate adults; the culture-of-honour experiments of Cohen, Nisbett and colleagues (1996), where Southern US students insulted in a corridor showed larger cortisol and testosterone rises and more aggressive follow-on behaviour than Northerners (small samples; the underlying regional homicide pattern is better established than the lab effect); placebo and expectancy effects.
- So-what: What a group treats as an insult, a fair share, or an acceptable risk is not a preference layered over fixed biology; it is trained into physiology. Two implications: culture is more powerful than incentives at shifting behaviour once installed, and installed cultural responses are slow to reverse. Do not model your members as having stable, culture-free utility functions (a theme that resurfaces in Camerer and Gintis).

### Ch 15 — When We Crossed the Rubicon
- Core claims: There is a threshold (the "Rubicon") beyond which cultural evolution becomes self-sustaining: once a population's stock of cultural know-how is rich enough that learning from others beats individual learning, selection favours better cultural learners, who accumulate more know-how, which favours better learners still. The book sketches how our lineage crossed it, likely well before modern humans and before full language.
- Key material: the autocatalytic feedback between sociality (group size, tolerance, pair-bonding), cultural learning, and brain size; the role of ecological pushes (drier climates, terrestrial life, meat and tool use) that made the ape lineage's starting conditions unusual; the argument that the threshold explains why cumulative culture is rare among animals despite social learning being common.
- So-what: Positive feedback loops between the value of learning and the stock of things worth learning exist in organisations too. Below the threshold, it is not worth an individual's time to learn from colleagues, and knowledge stays private; above it, learning from others becomes the dominant strategy and the group compounds. Getting a group across that threshold (enough shared, visible, copyable practice) is a distinct design goal from optimising a group that is already across.

### Ch 16 — Why Us?
- Core claims: Why did this lineage and not others cross the threshold? Henrich's answer is a conjunction of contingent starting conditions rather than a single trick: large, tolerant social groups; terrestriality; bipedalism freeing hands; pair-bonding and paternal investment creating multiple kin-based teachers; meat-sharing and tool use raising the payoff to learning. Cetaceans and others have social learning but lack the combination.
- So-what: The lesson is about preconditions. Cumulative know-how needs several things at once: proximity, tolerance (people are safe to approach and watch), motivation to teach or at least to permit copying, and time. Any one missing breaks the loop. In a group, check each precondition rather than assuming knowledge "wants to be shared."

### Ch 17 — A New Kind of Animal
- Core claims: We are a cultural species in which innovation is mostly recombination in a collective brain, intelligence is partly downloaded from culture (the Flynn effect as cultural evolution acting on cognition), and institutions and psychologies coevolve. Individual-centred theories of innovation, rationality and institutions systematically misdescribe humans. The chapter foreshadows Henrich's later program (The WEIRDest People in the World, 2020) on how institutions such as kinship rules shape psychology.
- So-what: When you design cooperation, you are designing an evolutionary process, not a machine: variation (people try things), selection (which get copied and which get sanctioned), and transmission (who sees whom). Ask about each stage explicitly. The group's future norms will be whatever wins that process, with or without you.

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. **Cumulative cultural evolution, not individual intelligence, explains human competence.** Adaptive know-how accumulates across generations through selective copying; no individual could derive it, and lost explorers prove that individuals without it die.
2. **The collective brain.** The rate of innovation and the maintenance of complex skills scale with the size and interconnectedness of the population that learns from each other; isolation causes skill loss (Tasmania, Polar Inuit).
3. **Cultural learning biases are the levers.** Success/skill bias, prestige bias, conformity, self-similarity and credibility-enhancing displays determine what spreads. Persuasion is a weak channel by comparison.
4. **Prestige is different from dominance.** Prestige is freely conferred deference that buys copying access and scales cooperation; dominance is coerced and suppresses information flow. Protect and route through prestige.
5. **Over-imitation and faith in tradition are adaptive.** Practices whose payoffs are delayed or invisible (manioc, nixtamalization, taboos) survive only because people copy without understanding. Unexplained practices deserve scrutiny before removal, and explanations rarely produce adherence.
6. **Gene-culture coevolution.** Culture changes the selection environment for genes (lactase, skin pigmentation, brain size, lifespan). Human bodies and minds are built for culture, which is why cultural learning is so fast and so automatic.
7. **Norm psychology and self-domestication.** Norms plus reputation plus sanction selected for a species that internalises rules, feels shame and guilt, and pays to punish violators. That psychology is content-neutral: it will carry good norms or bad ones.
8. **Cultural group selection.** Cooperative norms and institutions spread because groups that had them out-competed, out-grew and were imitated by groups that did not. Institutions are mostly copied and modified, not designed from scratch.
9. **Culture reaches into physiology.** Literacy, honour, expectation and taste are trained into brains and hormones, so cultural change is more powerful than incentives once installed, and slow to reverse.
10. **The Rubicon.** Cultural accumulation is a positive-feedback process with a threshold. Getting a group past the point where learning from others beats going alone is a distinct design problem.

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **Cumulative cultural evolution** — know-how ratchets up across generations because each learner starts from the best available model rather than from scratch. Bites when: a team assumes its competence is in its people rather than in its inherited practices and then loses it through turnover.
- **Collective brain** — the network of individuals who learn from each other, whose size and connectivity set the group's innovative and retentive capacity. Bites when: silos, remote isolation or attrition shrink the effective learning network below what the skill base needs.
- **Cultural learning biases** — evolved heuristics for choosing whom and what to copy: success/skill, prestige, self-similarity, conformity, content biases (danger, food, norm violation). Bites when: the visibly successful person in your group is doing the wrong thing.
- **Prestige** — voluntary deference given to acquire proximity to and copying rights from the skilled; marked by attention, approach and imitation, reciprocated by generosity. **Dominance** — status held by threat and enforced by fear; marked by avoidance and compliance. Bites when: managers confuse compliance under observation for adoption.
- **Over-imitation** — faithful copying of causally irrelevant steps; children and adults do it, chimpanzees do not. Bites when: process cargo-cults form, and also when you strip "pointless" steps that were load-bearing.
- **Credibility-enhancing display (CRED)** — costly action by a model that only makes sense if the model holds the belief, which makes the belief more copyable. Bites when: leaders say "quality first" and ship anyway; the CRED goes the wrong way.
- **Conformist transmission** — disproportionate copying of the majority, especially under uncertainty. Bites when: a minority best practice cannot reach the tipping point; also when a bad majority practice is self-reinforcing.
- **Gene-culture coevolution / dual inheritance** — two interacting inheritance systems; culture leads, genes follow. Bites when: someone claims a behaviour is "just human nature" and therefore fixed, or "just culture" and therefore easily changed.
- **Norm psychology** — evolved readiness to learn, expect, internalise and enforce rules, including third-party sanction. Bites when: you assume people will only follow rules with material incentives, or assume they will always enforce the rules you intended.
- **Self-domestication** — selection against reactive aggression and norm violation once reputation and coalitional punishment existed. Bites when: designing sanctions; humans respond strongly to ostracism and reputational threats, often more than to fines.
- **Cultural group selection** — differential survival, growth, migration and imitation between groups with different norm packages. Bites when: choosing what to copy from a successful peer organisation (copy the norm package, not only the tools).
- **Tasmanian effect / the Rubicon** — the two ends of the same feedback loop: skill loss when the learning network shrinks; self-sustaining accumulation once learning from others beats going alone.

## Evidence strength & limits
**Robust or well supported**
- That humans have specialised social-learning abilities that chimpanzees lack, and that early childhood is where the gap opens (Herrmann et al., 2007, and a large follow-on literature).
- Over-imitation as a phenomenon in children and adults: replicated in many labs, though most studies use the same Horner–Whiten puzzle box, and a study of Aka forager children (Berl and Hewlett, 2015) found children emulating rather than over-imitating while adults over-imitated, so its developmental universality is less clear than the book implies.
- Prestige and dominance as distinct routes to influence: supported by Cheng et al. (2013) and later work; the underlying theory (Henrich and Gil-White, 2001) is widely used.
- Lactase persistence and other cases of gene-culture coevolution: strong genetic and archaeological evidence.
- Food-processing cases (manioc, maize, nardoo): well documented historically and chemically.
- Population size and tool-kit complexity in Oceania (Kline and Boyd, 2010): a clean result, though correlational and limited to one region.

**Contested or argued more than shown**
- **The collective brain population-size claim.** This is the book's most disputed empirical pillar. Vaesen, Collard, Cosgrove and Roebroeks (PNAS, 2016) argued that the demographic models (Henrich, 2004; Powell et al., 2009) only generate the predicted link between population size and cultural complexity under implausible assumptions, that most empirical tests do not support the link, and that the Tasmanian archaeological and ethnographic record has been misread. Henrich, Boyd, Derex, Kline, Mesoudi, Muthukrishna, Powell, Shennan and Thomas replied in PNAS the same year that the critics misunderstood the models (which are about effective learning-network size and connectivity, not census size). Earlier, Collard and colleagues (2005, 2013) found that hunter-gatherer tool-kit complexity correlates with environmental risk rather than population size. Laboratory transmission-chain studies (Derex et al., 2013; Muthukrishna et al., 2014) support the mechanism at small scale. Fair summary: the mechanism is plausible and demonstrated in the lab; the archaeological support is mixed and the Tasmanian case is a genuine dispute, not a settled example.
- **Cultural group selection.** Richerson, Boyd, Henrich and colleagues defended it in a 2016 Behavioral and Brain Sciences target article that drew many critical commentaries: on whether between-group variation is large and stable enough, whether imitation of successful groups is really "selection," and whether within-group mechanisms (reciprocity, reputation, punishment) suffice. Henrich's ethnographic cases (Nuer, Ilahita) are illustrative rather than statistically demonstrative.
- **Self-domestication.** The general idea (Wrangham, Hare) is respectable, but the specific claim that norm-enforcement drove selection for a norm psychology is a well-motivated hypothesis with indirect evidence (developmental, cross-cultural games, ethnographic accounts of ostracism), not a measured selection history.
- **Culture of honour hormones.** Cohen et al. (1996) had small samples and, as far as I know, has not been subjected to a large pre-registered direct replication; treat the lab result as suggestive. Regional homicide-pattern data are stronger.
- **Menopause / grandmother hypothesis and "elders as knowledge repositories."** Plausible and consistent with skill-acquisition curves, but the demographic evidence for the grandmother hypothesis is debated.
- **Synchrony and ritual effects on cooperation.** Small-sample social psychology results; mixed replications; do not lean on specific effect sizes.
- **Cross-cultural economic games.** The finding that games results vary with market integration and world religion (Henrich et al., 2010) is robust in direction; the interpretation as evidence of norm internalisation is contested (see Camerer and Gintis files).

**Argued vs. shown, generally.** Henrich is a synthesiser; the book's rhetorical form is "here is a puzzle, here is a study, here is the mechanism that fits." He rarely presents the null model or the rival explanation with equal force. The developmental and cross-species work is the strongest; the deep-time evolutionary narrative (chapters 5, 15, 16) is coherent but is a reconstruction. Where the book states population figures, dates or brain-size numbers, I have not verified them here and they should be checked before reuse.

## Design implications for cooperation in real groups
- **Map the collective brain before changing anything.** Who learns from whom, in practice? Where are single points of failure for critical skills? Which subteams are Tasmania? [E for the mechanism at lab scale; H for your group]
- **Increase connectivity before increasing headcount.** Cross-team pairing, rotations, demos and observable work raise the effective size of the learning network more cheaply than hiring. [H]
- **Identify and protect prestige.** Find whom people actually watch and copy (not the org chart). Give those people visibility, ask them to model the target behaviours, and shield them from being displaced by dominant personalities. [E for the prestige/dominance distinction; H for the intervention]
- **Route norms through models, not memos.** Ensure a prestigious, self-similar model is visibly doing the behaviour and visibly succeeding; and make leaders' CREDs point the right way, since one visible costly exception (shipping despite the "quality" rule) transmits the opposite norm. [E for the biases; H for effect size in your setting]
- **Use conformity deliberately.** Make target behaviours look like majority behaviour where they are (public counts) and avoid advertising low adoption, because "most people don't do this" is itself transmitted. [E for conformist bias; H for the tactic]
- **Fill the norm slot early.** Norm psychology attaches to whatever pattern is first and visible; the first weeks of observed behaviour set norms that later rules will fight. [H]
- **Design sanctions around reputation and inclusion, not just money.** Mild public peer disapproval is a strong lever and a dangerous one; pair it with a legitimate process to avoid antisocial punishment. [E for the sensitivity; V for the constraint]
- **Audit before removing "pointless" practices.** Ask whether the payoff is delayed or diffuse; remove only when you can trace why it existed. [H]
- **Do not expect explanation to produce adherence.** Budget for modelling, repetition and enforcement; treat "we explained it" as zero adoption. [E for the psychology; H as a practice rule]
- **Copy norm packages from successful peer groups, then adapt;** and build copying channels (shadowing, narrated work) from long-tenured members, who are storage. [H]
- **Value commitment: prefer prestige-based to dominance-based influence** even where dominance would be faster, because dominance suppresses the information flow the collective brain runs on. [V]

- **02-darwins-unfinished-symphony** — Agrees on cumulative culture and gene-culture coevolution; Laland stresses strategic social learning and niche construction and is more cautious about cultural group selection. The experimental complement to Henrich's synthesis.

## Retrieval practice

1. (Recall) Name four of Henrich's cultural learning biases and give one situation where each could spread a *bad* practice.
<details>
Success/skill bias (the visibly successful person's shortcut gets copied), prestige bias (a respected person's idiosyncratic habit spreads), conformity (a bad majority practice becomes self-reinforcing), self-similarity bias (people copy same-role peers rather than the best available model), CREDs (a leader's costly exception transmits the opposite norm). Any four with plausible examples.
</details>

2. (Explain why) Why did Burke and Wills starve while eating the same nardoo fern that local Aboriginal groups thrived on?
<details>
They lacked the culturally accumulated processing steps (leaching and roasting) that neutralise the plant's thiaminase. The knowledge was not derivable by intelligent individuals in the time available; it had been accumulated over generations. The case shows competence is a property of the inherited cultural package.
</details>

3. (Apply) Your team of eight has one engineer who knows the deployment system. She is respected, quiet and leaving in three months. Using chapter 12, what do you do and why?
<details>
This is a Tasmania risk: a complex skill held by one node with no copying channel. Increase the effective learning network: pair her with two people who shadow real deployments (observation of a skilled model, not documentation alone), have her narrate decisions, and create repeated low-stakes occasions for copying. Documentation helps transmission fidelity but does not replace modelling.
</details>

4. (Spot the misconception) "Henrich shows that bigger populations always produce more complex technology, so growing our organisation will make it more innovative."
<details>
Two errors. First, the claim is about the size and connectivity of the *learning network*, not census size; growth without connection can shrink the effective collective brain. Second, the population-size claim is contested (Vaesen et al., 2016; Collard et al.), with mixed archaeological support; the lab evidence supports the mechanism at small scale only.
</details>

5. (Recall) What distinguishes prestige from dominance in behaviour and in consequences for information flow?
<details>
Dominance: fear-based, enforced by threat, marked by expansive posture and others' avoidance; produces compliance under observation. Prestige: freely conferred deference to skilled/knowledgeable people, marked by attention, approach and imitation, reciprocated by generosity. Prestige enables copying and honest signalling; dominance suppresses them because people avoid rather than approach the dominant.
</details>

6. (Explain why) Why does Henrich say "faith" in tradition is adaptive rather than a cognitive flaw?
<details>
Because cultural packages contain adaptations whose causal logic is opaque and whose payoffs are delayed (manioc detoxification, food taboos). Individuals who edited practices according to their own understanding did worse than those who copied faithfully, so selection favoured over-imitation and deference to tradition.
</details>

7. (Apply) A community moderation team wants members to flag misinformation. They have written clear guidelines and a tutorial. Adoption is 5 percent. Diagnose with Henrich.
<details>
Explanation is a weak transmission channel. Ask: is a prestigious, self-similar member visibly flagging and visibly benefiting? Does the interface make flagging look like majority behaviour, or does it advertise low adoption (conformity working against you)? Are there any CREDs from leaders? Is there any norm enforcement (expectation plus mild sanction) or only information? Fix by modelling, visibility of adoption, and social expectation, not by better tutorials.
</details>

8. (Recall) List three channels of cultural group selection besides warfare.
<details>
Differential migration into successful groups; differential imitation of successful groups' norms and institutions; differential reproduction or group growth; prestige-biased transmission between groups; differential group survival through non-violent means (e.g. famine resilience).
</details>

9. (Spot the misconception) "Because norm psychology evolved to sustain cooperation, people will naturally enforce the cooperative norms we set."
<details>
Norm psychology is content-neutral. People internalise and enforce whatever pattern is visible and expected, including harmful norms, and in some norm environments they punish cooperators (antisocial punishment, Herrmann et al., 2008). The designer must fill the norm slot deliberately and channel enforcement through a legitimate process.
</details>

10. (Explain why) In what sense did culture "steal our guts," and what does it imply for how much a group should invest in onboarding?
<details>
Cultural food processing (cooking, tools) did digestive work externally, letting selection shrink metabolically expensive gut tissue and expand brains for cultural learning, together with longer childhoods. The general principle: investment in learning capacity pays only when there is a rich stock of transmissible know-how. Onboarding length should scale with the depth of what the group actually has to transmit.
</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- **"Humans are dumb, culture is smart."** Henrich's point is comparative: individual cognition is not what separates us, and our cognition is itself built by and for culture. He is not claiming individual reasoning is worthless; he is claiming it is a minor input to cumulative know-how.
- **"The collective brain means the wisdom of crowds."** The wisdom of crowds is about aggregating independent judgments. The collective brain is about non-independent, selective copying over generations; it depends on connection and on people copying the *right* models, not on independence.
- **"Cultural group selection means groups fight and the losers die."** War is one channel; migration, imitation and differential growth are the others and probably dominate.
- **"Gene-culture coevolution means behaviour is genetically determined."** The direction of influence in the book runs mostly from culture to genes; and the cases (lactase, pigmentation) show genes responding to culturally created environments, not genes fixing behaviour.
- **"Over-imitation shows people are irrational."** It is an adaptive default that preserves opaque-but-useful practice; the same default also produces cargo cults. Hold both.
- **"The population-size story is settled."** It is the book's most contested empirical claim; lab support for the mechanism, disputed archaeology, and a reply to critics that reframes the variable as network connectivity rather than raw numbers.
- **"Institutions are designed by smart people."** Henrich argues most were copied and modified under group competition, with designers rationalising afterwards. Ostrom is the partial counterweight later in the course.
