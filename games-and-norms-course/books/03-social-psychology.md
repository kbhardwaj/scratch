# Social Psychology — Tom Gilovich, Dacher Keltner, Serena Chen & Richard E. Nisbett (6th ed., W. W. Norton, 2023)
Level: Mechanisms  ·  Priority: Core
Est. reading time saved: ~25h  ·  Your time with this file: ~35 min

## The book in one sentence
People's behavior in groups is driven far more by the immediate situation, by how they construe it, and by automatic mental processes than by stable character — and the same machinery that makes cooperation, conformity and prejudice easy to trigger also makes them designable.

## The book in one paragraph
This is the standard undergraduate survey of social psychology, organized around three recurring themes the authors flag in the opening chapter: the *power of the situation* (small features of context move behavior more than personality), *construal* (people respond to their interpretation of a situation, not the situation itself), and *automatic versus controlled processing* (fast, unconscious judgments run alongside slow deliberate ones, and often win). Layered on top is a persistent attention to *culture* (independent vs. interdependent selves, honor cultures, analytic vs. holistic cognition — Nisbett's signature contribution) and to *evolutionary* explanations for why the machinery exists at all. The book walks from the self and social cognition, through attitudes and persuasion, to social influence, relationships, prejudice, groups, aggression, and finally morality, altruism and cooperation. The 6th edition adds a chapter on the lived experience of being stereotyped and updates throughout for the post-2011 replication reckoning, which the authors treat seriously in the methods chapter but do not always carry through into every classic they still cite.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
Game theory (Dixit, Camerer) tells you what the strategic structure of a group situation is; Skyrms, Bicchieri and Ostrom tell you how norms and institutions stabilize it. This book supplies the missing layer: *what actual humans do when they walk into that structure* — how they read other people's intentions, what makes them conform or defect, why groups make worse decisions than their members, and which levers (identity, descriptive norms, contact, accountability, framing) reliably move behavior. Read it with one question: **which of these effects are large and robust enough to build a design on, and which are folklore that failed to replicate?**

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

### Ch 1 — An Invitation to Social Psychology
- Core claims: (1) Situations are more powerful than we intuit, and we systematically underestimate them — the *fundamental attribution error* is introduced here as the book's master theme. (2) Behavior follows construal: two people in the "same" situation are in different situations if they read it differently. (3) Two processing systems run in parallel; the automatic one is fast, associative and often outside awareness. (4) Culture and evolution are the two backdrops against which all of this varies or holds constant.
- Key examples: Darley & Batson's (1973) seminarians who, told they were late, mostly stepped over a slumped stranger — even those about to lecture on the Good Samaritan. Ross's *naive realism*: people assume they see the world as it is, so disagreement must reflect the other side's bias.
- So-what: the default diagnosis of a failing group ("wrong people") is usually wrong; start with the situation and with how members construe it. Construal cuts both ways: relabeling a prisoner's dilemma from "Wall Street Game" to "Community Game" roughly doubles cooperation (Liberman, Samuels & Ross 2004) — a direct hinge to Camerer and Bicchieri.

### Ch 2 — The Methods of Social Psychology
- Core claims: correlation vs. experiment, internal vs. external validity, self-selection, and (in recent editions) a frank section on replication, p-hacking and preregistration.
- So-what: carry the habit, not the details — when a finding looks too cute, ask about sample size, preregistration and direct replication. The Evidence section below applies that filter.

### Ch 3 — The Social Self
- Core claims: the self is partly a product of others' reactions (looking-glass self, reflected appraisals) and of the culture's model of personhood. Markus & Kitayama's (1991) *independent* vs. *interdependent* self-construal: Western selves are defined by internal traits and autonomy, many East Asian and other selves by roles and relationships. Self-esteem is largely a *sociometer* (Leary) tracking social acceptance. People self-enhance (better-than-average effect), self-verify, and self-present, and the balance shifts by culture.
- Key studies: Markus & Kitayama's self-description tasks; Heine and colleagues on lower self-enhancement in Japan; Tesser's self-evaluation maintenance (we prefer close others to excel in domains not central to our own identity).
- So-what: group members will defend identity before they defend interests. Any cooperation design that requires someone to admit they were wrong, or to be outperformed in their signature domain, is fighting the self-system; design credit and comparison so that status threats are minimized [E]. Interdependent-self cultures make group norms easier to invoke and individual dissent costlier.

### Ch 4 — Social Cognition: Thinking about People and Situations
- Core claims: (1) People rely on *schemas* and *heuristics* (availability, representativeness, anchoring) that are efficient but produce predictable errors. (2) Information is weighted by order and vividness; *confirmation bias* and *motivated reasoning* shape what evidence is sought. (3) *Framing* matters: the same outcome described as a loss vs. a gain flips choices (Kahneman & Tversky). (4) Priming — the idea that incidental cues activate concepts and change behavior — is presented, with more caveats in recent editions.
- Key studies: Tversky & Kahneman (1974); Ross, Lepper & Hubbard (1975) belief perseverance; Lord, Ross & Lepper (1979) biased assimilation; Bargh's elderly-word priming (1996), now a textbook failed replication (Doyen et al. 2012).
- So-what: members overweight vivid anecdotes over base rates — one visible free-rider dominates the group's model of "what everyone does." Make the true descriptive norm vivid and concrete rather than leaving it to inference [E]. Build nothing on behavioral priming.

### Ch 5 — Social Attribution: Explaining Behavior
- Core claims: (1) People are causal explainers; Kelley's covariation model describes the logic they roughly follow (consensus, distinctiveness, consistency). (2) The *fundamental attribution error* / *correspondence bias*: observers attribute others' behavior to disposition even when situational constraints are obvious. (3) The *actor-observer difference* (we explain our own behavior situationally). (4) *Self-serving attributions* (credit for success, situations for failure). (5) Culture: East Asians make more situational attributions and show a weaker FAE (Morris & Peng, 1994; Choi, Nisbett & Norenzayan, 1999). (6) Attribution style matters for outcomes: pessimistic (internal, stable, global) explanations for bad events predict depression and poor performance.
- Key studies: Jones & Harris (1967) — readers infer a writer's true opinion from a pro-Castro essay even when told the position was assigned. Ross, Amabile & Steinmetz (1977) — quiz-show questioners are judged more knowledgeable than contestants despite obviously choosing the questions. Both replicate well.
- So-what: this chapter is the psychological reason cooperation fails silently. When a teammate misses a deadline, others read "unreliable person," not "overloaded person"; when someone free-rides once, they are typed as a free-rider, and reciprocity (the engine in Skyrms and Camerer) turns punitive prematurely. Designs that force situational information into view — visible workload, explicit reasons attached to lapses, blameless post-mortems — counteract the error [E]. Conversely, if you *want* a norm to feel like "who we are," attribution will happily supply the dispositional story [H].

### Ch 6 — Emotion
- Core claims: emotions are brief, functional, broadly universal in expression (Ekman) with cultural *display rules*; they coordinate interaction — embarrassment repairs status, gratitude cements reciprocity, anger signals a violated norm (Keltner's emotions-as-commitment-devices view). Happiness: adaptation, social ties, affective forecasting errors.
- So-what: guilt, gratitude and outrage are the emotional substrate of reciprocity and norm enforcement. A group where violations produce no visible emotional response has no real norms [H].

### Ch 7 — Attitudes, Behavior, and Rationalization
- Core claims: (1) Attitudes predict behavior weakly in general, better when specific, strong, accessible, and when behavior is under volitional control (theory of planned behavior). (2) Behavior shapes attitudes: *cognitive dissonance* (Festinger) — people change beliefs to justify effort, choices, and counter-attitudinal acts done for insufficient reward; *self-perception* (Bem) — people infer attitudes from their behavior when internal cues are weak. (3) Overjustification: rewarding an already-enjoyed activity can undermine intrinsic motivation.
- Key studies: Festinger & Carlsmith (1959) — $1 vs. $20 for lying about a boring task; the $1 group later liked it more. Aronson & Mills (1959) effort justification for group initiation. Lepper, Greene & Nisbett (1973) — children rewarded for drawing later drew less. Induced-compliance and effort-justification dissonance replicate reasonably well; the free-choice paradigm has a statistical artifact (Chen & Risen 2010); overjustification is real but moderated (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999).
- So-what: this is the chapter on norm *internalization*. Small, public, freely chosen commitments produce belief; heavy incentives produce compliance without belief; cost of entry increases loyalty; paying people for what they already do can crowd out the motive (cf. Ostrom on external rules displacing local norms) [E].

### Ch 8 — Persuasion
- Core claims: (1) Dual-process models (Petty & Cacioppo's elaboration likelihood model; Chaiken's heuristic-systematic model): persuasion runs through a *central* route (argument quality, when motivated and able) or a *peripheral* route (source attractiveness, message length, consensus cues). (2) Source, message and audience factors: credibility, sleeper effect, fear appeals work when paired with an efficacious action, two-sided messages for informed audiences. (3) Resistance: *inoculation* (McGuire), forewarning, reactance. (4) Media effects are mostly about *agenda setting* and reinforcement, not conversion.
- So-what: a cooperation norm pitched to a busy group travels the peripheral route — what matters is who says it, how consistently, and whether it looks like everyone already agrees. To make it stick under attack, pre-expose members to weakened versions of the arguments against it (inoculation), which has replicated well including in the misinformation literature (van der Linden, Roozenbeek) [E].

### Ch 9 — Social Influence (5e numbering)
This is the load-bearing chapter for the course. Three forms: conformity, compliance, obedience.
- Core claims: (1) *Conformity* has two motives — *informational* (others are a source of truth; Sherif's autokinetic norms, which persisted across "generations" of participants) and *normative* (fear of disapproval; Asch's line judgments). (2) Conformity rises with group size up to about 3–4, unanimity, expertise/status of the group, and cultural interdependence; a single dissenter collapses it. (3) *Minority influence* works through consistency and self-confidence (Moscovici). (4) *Compliance* tactics: reciprocity (door-in-the-face), consistency (foot-in-the-door, low-balling), scarcity, and *descriptive vs. injunctive norms* (Cialdini's focus theory: what people do vs. what they approve of). (5) *Obedience*: Milgram's 65 percent full compliance in the baseline; obedience drops with proximity of the victim, distance of the authority, disobedient peers, and loss of the experimenter's institutional backing. The authors' explanation stresses the incremental "tuning in to the authority, tuning out the learner" and the absence of a graceful exit rather than sadism.
- Key studies and replication status: Asch (1951/1956) — about a third of trials conformed, ~75 percent of participants at least once; Bond & Smith (1996) meta-analyzed 133 Asch-type studies from 17 countries: robust, somewhat lower in the US since the 1950s, higher in collectivist cultures. Milgram (1963/1974) — Burger's (2009) partial replication to 150 volts found 70 percent continuing vs. roughly 82 percent in Milgram; also replicated in Poland (Doliński et al. 2017). Archival work (Perry; Haslam & Reicher) shows the experimenter improvised beyond the scripted prods and that many complied because they believed in the science — *engaged followership* rather than blind obedience. Cialdini's Petrified Forest sign ("many visitors take wood") backfired, a clean demonstration that descriptive norms undercut injunctive ones. Schultz et al. (2007): descriptive-norm energy feedback pulled high users down and low users *up* (boomerang) unless an injunctive smiley was added; replicated at scale by Opower (Allcott 2011), effects around 2 percent of consumption.
- So-what: most of Bicchieri's operational toolkit lives here. If you want a behavior, make its prevalence visible (descriptive norm) *and* signal approval (injunctive norm) *and* make sure there is at least one visible dissenter whenever the group is drifting toward a bad consensus. If you want to protect a group from a bad authority, install exits and peers early; Milgram's participants disobeyed most reliably when someone else did first [E]. Foot-in-the-door: ask for a tiny visible contribution before the real one [E].

### Ch 9 (6e) / Ch 10 (5e) — Relationships and Attraction
- Core claims: proximity and mere exposure (Zajonc), similarity and attractiveness drive liking; equity theory describes stable relationships as balanced exchanges; *communal* vs. *exchange* relationships (Clark & Mills) differ in whether keeping score is appropriate; attachment styles persist; Gottman's contempt finding.
- So-what: the communal/exchange distinction is a design variable. Exchange norms suit strangers; communal norms suit teams that cooperate repeatedly, and introducing scorekeeping into a communal group damages it [E]. Mere exposure is the cheapest cohesion tool there is [E].

### Ch 10 (6e) / Ch 11 (5e) — Stereotyping, Prejudice, and Discrimination
- Core claims: (1) Three components: stereotype (cognitive), prejudice (affective), discrimination (behavioral). (2) *Realistic group conflict* (Sherif's Robbers Cave, 1954: competition over scarce goods produces hostility; superordinate goals reduce it), *minimal group paradigm* (Tajfel, 1971: arbitrary categorization alone produces in-group favoritism), and *social identity theory* (self-esteem is bound up with group standing). (3) Cognitive origins: categorization, out-group homogeneity, illusory correlation, subtyping. (4) Automatic prejudice: implicit associations (IAT) run even in people who explicitly reject bias. (5) Reduction: *contact* under Allport's conditions (equal status, common goals, cooperation, institutional support), jigsaw classrooms (Aronson), cross-categorization.
- Status: minimal-group favoritism is highly robust. Robbers Cave is a single, partly stage-managed field study (an earlier attempt failed); a demonstration, not an estimate. Contact: Pettigrew & Tropp (2006) meta-analysis of 500+ studies finds a reliable moderate effect; Paluck, Green & Green (2019) note the rigorous field experiments are fewer and smaller. IAT: predicts individual behavior weakly, and changing implicit measures does not reliably change behavior (Oswald et al. 2013; Forscher et al. 2019).
- So-what: in-group/out-group lines form on almost nothing, so subgroup boundaries inside a team (frontend/backend, old-timers/newcomers) will generate favoritism unless a superordinate identity and cross-cutting collaboration are engineered [E]. Cooperation across a boundary requires *structured* contact with a shared goal, not just proximity [E]. Do not rely on implicit-bias training to change behavior; change decision procedures instead [E].

### Ch 11 (6e, new) — Living in a Prejudiced World
- Core claims (reconstructed from Norton's description and the 5e material it expands): the target's side — attributional ambiguity (Crocker & Major: was that feedback about my work or my group?), stereotype threat (Steele & Aronson 1995), vigilance and health costs of chronic discrimination, coping (disidentification, collective action, affirmation), microaggressions.
- So-what: stereotyped members spend cognitive resources monitoring for bias, and ambiguity about *why* an outcome happened is itself corrosive. Transparent criteria and high standards paired with assurance of capability (the wise-feedback finding, Yeager et al. 2014) reduce the tax [E, moderate]. Belonging interventions replicate at smaller scale than first reported [H].

### Ch 12 — Groups
- Core claims: (1) *Social facilitation*: others' presence improves well-learned performance and impairs novel performance (Zajonc). (2) *Social loafing*: effort drops when contributions are pooled and unidentifiable (Latané, Williams & Harkins 1979); it shrinks when effort is identifiable, the task meaningful, the group small; weaker in collectivist cultures (Karau & Williams 1993). (3) *Group polarization*: discussion pushes the group toward a more extreme version of its initial lean, via persuasive arguments and social comparison. (4) *Groupthink* (Janis): cohesive, insulated groups with directive leaders suppress dissent; antidotes are devil's advocates, leader silence until the end, subgroups, second-chance meetings. (5) *Deindividuation*: anonymity and arousal loosen normative restraint. The Stanford Prison Experiment is now treated as a demonstration with severe methodological problems (guards were coached — Le Texier 2019). (6) *Power*: Keltner's approach/inhibition theory — power raises action orientation and lowers perspective taking.
- Replication status: facilitation and loafing are well established (Bond & Titus 1983; Karau & Williams 1993). Polarization is among the field's most robust effects. Groupthink as a *syndrome* has weak support — case studies cannot isolate cohesion, and lab tests find cohesion alone does not degrade decisions (Esser 1998; Baron 2005); the individual mechanisms (self-censorship, pressure on dissenters, illusion of unanimity) are real, the package is a heuristic. Power's effects on empathy replicate inconsistently.
- So-what: this chapter is the diagnostic checklist for a team that "should" cooperate but underdelivers. Loafing → make individual contributions visible and meaningful, keep working units small [E]. Polarization → any homogeneous group that deliberates will grow more extreme, which is good for commitment and dangerous for judgment; seed dissent and outside input before, not after, the decision [E]. Groupthink antidotes remain sensible even though the syndrome is contested: separate idea generation from evaluation, have the leader withhold their view, assign a critic [E for the mechanisms, H for the package]. Deindividuation → anonymity in online communities predicts norm violation unless a group identity is salient (the SIDE model: anonymity can *increase* conformity to a salient group norm) [E].

### Ch 13 — Aggression
- Core claims: aggression rises with negative affect (Berkowitz's revision of frustration-aggression), heat, provocation, alcohol and weapons cues; it is shaped by *culture of honor* (Nisbett & Cohen 1996: Southern US men respond to insults with more anger and cortisol; archival homicide data match), by social learning (Bandura), and by dehumanization. Media violence effects are small and contested. Punishment deters only when swift and certain, not severe.
- So-what: honor norms are a self-enforcing equilibrium wherever no third party enforces — herding economies, unmoderated online spaces. Legitimate, quick, certain third-party enforcement removes the need for self-help retaliation [E for the pattern, H for the intervention].

### Ch 14 — Morality, Altruism, and Cooperation
- Core claims: (1) *Bystander effect*: Latané & Darley's (1968) five-step model — notice, interpret as emergency (pluralistic ignorance), assume responsibility (diffusion), know how, act. The Kitty Genovese story that motivated it was largely wrong in its details (Manning, Levine & Collins, 2007), but the lab effect is robust (Fischer et al., 2011 meta-analysis; interestingly the effect shrinks or reverses in genuinely dangerous emergencies, and CCTV studies of real fights find that someone almost always intervenes — Philpot et al., 2020). (2) Motives for helping: empathy-altruism (Batson) vs. egoistic negative-state relief (Cialdini); kin selection and reciprocal altruism as evolutionary backdrop; costly signaling and reputation. (3) Situational determinants: mood, rural vs. urban, religiosity, victim characteristics, being watched (eye images — an effect that has largely failed to replicate; Northover et al., 2017). (4) *Cooperation*: prisoner's dilemma and public goods games, tit-for-tat (Axelrod), reputation and indirect reciprocity, the effect of construal (Wall Street vs. Community game), communication, and punishment. Ultimatum game rejections vary by culture (Henrich et al., 2001 cross-cultural work, which links directly to book 01). (5) Morality (added emphasis in 6e title): moral intuitions (Haidt), moral foundations, and the role of emotions like disgust and elevation.
- Replication status: bystander/diffusion of responsibility robust in lab and in low-danger field settings; empathy-altruism is theoretically contested but the basic empathy → helping link holds; the construal effect on PD cooperation is robust and large; cooperation-game results are among the most replicable in psychology because they are behavioral, incentivized and simple (Camerer et al., 2016, found ~60 percent of economics-lab effects replicate, higher than the psychology rate).
- So-what: the five-step bystander model is a design checklist for "why did no one act on the obvious problem." Fix each step: make problems unambiguous, assign responsibility to a named person, give people a script for intervening, and remove the audience effect by making the first move cheap [E]. For cooperation: naming the game ("this is a shared commons") shifts behavior more than changing the payoffs slightly; reputation systems substitute for repeated interaction among strangers; cheap talk before a public goods round reliably raises contributions (Sally, 1995 meta-analysis: communication is the single strongest lever) [E].

### Culture (cross-cutting theme, not a chapter)
- Nisbett's analytic/holistic cognition program runs throughout: East Asian participants attend more to context and relationships, make more situational attributions, tolerate contradiction, and show weaker self-enhancement. Individualism–collectivism moderates conformity (higher in collectivist samples), social loafing (lower), and the salience of injunctive norms. The WEIRD critique (Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan, 2010) is embraced in the methods chapter and applies to most of the book's own studies.
- So-what: nearly every design implication below has a culture parameter; for multinational teams, norm-based and identity-based levers work differently and should be tested locally [H].

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. **Situations dominate, and we do not believe it.** The fundamental attribution error means groups blame people for what structures produced. Fix structures first; fix "the people" last.
2. **Construal is the lever.** How a group labels an interaction (community vs. market, gift vs. transaction, us vs. them) changes behavior as much as the payoffs do.
3. **Norms have two channels.** Descriptive (what people do) and injunctive (what they approve). They must point the same way; a visible descriptive norm of defection beats any injunctive appeal.
4. **Conformity is informational as much as normative** — people follow the group because the group is usually right — and a single dissenter breaks it. Protect and manufacture dissent.
5. **Obedience is about the relationship to authority and the absence of an exit,** not about evil people. Institutions that want good behavior must supply peers, exits and legitimacy for refusal.
6. **Behavior changes attitudes** through dissonance and self-perception: get small, freely chosen, public commitments; avoid overpaying for behavior you want people to own.
7. **Groups amplify their starting lean** (polarization), hide individual effort (loafing), and can suppress dissent under directive leaders. Deliberation design is not optional.
8. **Boundaries form on nothing** (minimal groups); cooperation across them requires structured, equal-status contact around a shared goal, not proximity or training.
9. **Bystander inaction is a sequence of five failures,** each of which can be engineered away.
10. **Much of the famous material is folklore.** Ego depletion, behavioral priming, power posing, the Stanford Prison Experiment as usually told, and stereotype threat in its strong lab form do not survive scrutiny; conformity, obedience, attribution errors, dissonance, polarization, loafing, minimal groups and cooperation-game effects do.

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **Fundamental attribution error / correspondence bias** — overweighting disposition, underweighting situation, when explaining others — bites in blame allocation after any team failure.
- **Naive realism** — the sense that one's own view is unmediated reality — bites in any disagreement; each side sees the other as biased.
- **Construal** — the subjective interpretation that behavior actually responds to — bites whenever a designer assumes the objective incentive is what people see.
- **Descriptive vs. injunctive norm** — what is done vs. what is approved — bites when a well-meant message reveals that misbehavior is common.
- **Informational vs. normative influence** — following because others know vs. to avoid disapproval — bites in whether private ballots will fix a conformity problem (they fix only the normative part).
- **Pluralistic ignorance** — everyone privately doubts, everyone believes everyone else is fine — bites in silent teams and in "everyone drinks/overworks here."
- **Diffusion of responsibility** — the more potential actors, the less any one acts — bites in every unowned task.
- **Cognitive dissonance / effort justification / overjustification** — belief follows behavior when external pressure is small; rewards can crowd out intrinsic motive — bites in incentive design.
- **Elaboration likelihood (central vs. peripheral route)** — argument vs. cue-based persuasion — bites in how much a norm message can rely on content vs. source and consensus.
- **Social loafing / social facilitation** — effort drops when pooled; performance shifts under observation — bites in team sizing and visibility.
- **Group polarization** — deliberation extremizes — bites in homogeneous decision groups and online communities.
- **Groupthink** — Janis's contested syndrome of concurrence-seeking — bites as a checklist, not a diagnosis.
- **Deindividuation / SIDE model** — anonymity loosens personal restraint but heightens conformity to whatever group identity is salient — bites in online community design.
- **Minimal group paradigm / social identity** — arbitrary categorization produces favoritism — bites in subteam rivalries.
- **Contact hypothesis (Allport's conditions)** — equal status, shared goals, cooperation, institutional support — bites when "get them in a room" is proposed as a fix.
- **Communal vs. exchange relationships** — need-based vs. score-based norms — bites when metrics are introduced into a team.
- **Independent vs. interdependent self** — cultural models of the person — bites in every lever above for mixed-culture groups.

## Evidence strength & limits
Social psychology is the field where the replication crisis broke (Bem 2011; Open Science Collaboration 2015 replicated only about 36–39 percent of published psychology findings, with social psychology the weakest area). The textbook's 6th edition acknowledges this in its methods chapter, but chapter narratives still lean on some classics. A working classification, based on verified replication reports and meta-analyses plus my background knowledge (flagged where I could not verify in this session):

**Robust (build on these):**
- Asch-type conformity: Bond & Smith 1996 meta-analysis of 133 studies (verified); culturally moderated but present everywhere.
- Milgram obedience, broadly: Burger 2009 (70 percent to 150V, verified) and Doliński et al. 2017 (Poland; background knowledge). The *interpretation* — blind obedience vs. engaged followership — is contested; the behavior is not.
- Fundamental attribution error / correspondence bias: replicated across decades and paradigms; cultural moderation is itself a replicated finding.
- Cognitive dissonance in induced-compliance and effort-justification paradigms (moderate effect sizes; free-choice paradigm has a statistical artifact).
- Group polarization; social loafing (Karau & Williams 1993) and social facilitation (Bond & Titus 1983) meta-analyses.
- Minimal group in-group favoritism; intergroup contact (Pettigrew & Tropp 2006, with the caveat that rigorous field experiments are fewer and smaller).
- Bystander effect in the lab and in low-risk field settings (Fischer et al. 2011), with the real-world caveat that dangerous situations usually get intervention.
- Descriptive-norm interventions on behavior such as energy use (Allcott 2011 Opower; effects small, ~2 percent, but reliable at scale).
- Mere exposure; anchoring; framing (loss vs. gain); the construal effect on prisoner's dilemma cooperation; communication raising public goods contributions.
- Cooperation-game results generally (Camerer et al. 2016 economics replication project, background knowledge).

**Weak, contested or failed:**
- **Ego depletion** (Baumeister): Hagger et al. 2016 multi-lab registered replication, 23 labs, N = 2,141, d = 0.04 with CI including zero (verified). Vohs et al. 2021 second RRR also found a near-zero effect (background knowledge). Treat as unsupported in its strong form.
- **Behavioral priming** (elderly words → slow walking; money priming; cleanliness → moral judgment): Doyen et al. 2012 failed to replicate Bargh's walking study; many-labs efforts found most social priming effects at or near zero (background knowledge). Semantic and evaluative priming in reaction-time tasks are fine.
- **Power posing**: Ranehill et al. 2015 (N = 200, blinded) found the self-reported feeling of power but no hormonal or behavioral effects (verified); Carney later disowned the hormone claims. The felt-power effect is real and trivial.
- **Stereotype threat** on women's math performance: Flore & Wicherts 2015 meta-analysis found d = −0.22 before, and d = 0.07 (not significant) after, correcting for publication bias (verified); a preregistered large-N replication (Flore et al. 2018) found no effect. The phenomenon is likely real in some populations and high-stakes contexts but far smaller and more conditional than textbooks suggest; the 6e chapter on living with prejudice should be read with this in mind.
- **Stanford Prison Experiment**: guards were coached, the results were not systematically recorded, and the study was never a controlled experiment (Le Texier 2019; Haslam & Reicher's BBC prison study found leadership and identity, not roles, drove behavior). Use as a story about demand characteristics, not evidence.
- **Groupthink** as a causal syndrome: unsupported by controlled studies (Esser 1998); individual components are real.
- **Implicit Association Test** as a predictor of individual discrimination and as a target for intervention: weak (Oswald et al. 2013; Forscher et al. 2019).
- **Eye-image ("watching eyes") effects on honesty and donations**: mostly failed replications (Northover et al. 2017).
- **Robbers Cave**: a single study with a prior failed attempt; a demonstration, not an estimate.
- **Facial feedback**, **media violence → aggression**, **values-affirmation and belonging interventions**: marginal, small, or heterogeneous and context-dependent respectively.

**What the authors argue vs. show:** the three themes are organizing commitments, not findings; the evolutionary framing is background plausibility, rarely tested directly; the culture material rests heavily on East Asian vs. North American comparisons.

## Design implications for cooperation in real groups
- Diagnose failures situationally before dispositionally: map workload, information, incentives and ambiguity before concluding "we have free-riders." [E]
- Label the game. Name the interaction in communal or commons terms and repeat the label; frame contributions as "what members do here." [E]
- Make good behavior's prevalence visible (descriptive norm) and pair it with approval signals (injunctive norm); never broadcast that misbehavior is common in an attempt to shame it. [E]
- Guarantee at least one credible dissenter in every deliberating group; have leaders speak last; separate proposal from evaluation. [E for mechanisms; H for the full groupthink package]
- Expect homogeneous groups to polarize; inject outside views before decisions, not after. [E]
- Keep effort identifiable and working units small (2–5) to prevent loafing; make the task's meaning explicit. [E]
- Use small, voluntary, public commitments and modest rewards to produce internalized norms; avoid paying heavily for behavior you want members to own. [E]
- Do not introduce scorekeeping into a communal team without expecting relationship damage; if metrics are needed, make them collective. [E for the mechanism; H for your specific group]
- Engineer cross-cutting collaboration and a superordinate identity whenever subgroups exist; structured contact around shared goals, not diversity training or implicit-bias training, is what has evidence. [E]
- Fix bystander steps: make problems unambiguous, assign named ownership, give scripts for intervening, make the first move cheap. [E]
- Inoculate norms: pre-expose members to the arguments they will hear against the norm, with rebuttals. [E]
- Provide swift, certain, mild third-party enforcement so that honor-style self-help does not fill the vacuum. [H]
- For mixed-culture groups, test norm levers locally; conformity, loafing and self-enhancement all vary by independent/interdependent culture. [H]
- Do not build on ego depletion, behavioral priming, power posing or eye images. [E — evidence that they fail]
- Treat fairness, transparency of criteria and belonging as values worth honoring even where the performance evidence is mixed. [V]

## Connections
- **01-secret-of-our-success**: agrees on prestige-biased and conformist social learning as the engine of culture; Henrich supplies the origin story for the conformity and norm psychology this book documents. The cross-cultural ultimatum game work (Henrich et al. 2001) is cited in both.
- **02-darwins-unfinished-symphony**: Laland's social learning strategies (copy when uncertain, copy the majority) are the evolutionary version of informational conformity.
- **04-games-of-strategy**: this book is the empirical foil — the FAE, construal effects and norm following explain why real players depart from the equilibria Dixit et al. derive.
- **05-micromotives-and-macrobehavior**: Schelling's tipping and critical-mass models are the aggregate consequence of the conformity thresholds and pluralistic ignorance described here.
- **06-behavioral-game-theory**: Camerer's social-preference and coordination findings are the incentivized, more replicable cousins of the cooperation chapter; the construal ("Community Game") result is common ground.
- **07-stag-hunt**: Skyrms's signaling and trust dynamics assume the reciprocity and reputation psychology this book measures.
- **08-grammar-of-society**: Bicchieri's empirical and normative expectations map directly onto descriptive and injunctive norms and pluralistic ignorance; she draws heavily on Cialdini and Asch. Tension: Bicchieri is more optimistic about changing norms by correcting beliefs alone; this book emphasizes that identity and dissonance also matter.
- **09-governing-the-commons**: Ostrom's design principles (monitoring, graduated sanctions, collective choice) are institutional answers to loafing, diffusion of responsibility and reputation; her finding that external rules crowd out local norms parallels overjustification.
- **10-networks-crowds-markets**: information cascades formalize informational conformity; homophily is the network face of similarity-attraction and in-group bias.
- **11-bounds-of-reason**: Gintis wants social norms and psychology inside a unified game-theoretic framework; this book's replication problems are a reason to be careful about which psychological inputs that framework takes as given.

## Retrieval practice
1. (Recall) What are the three organizing themes of the book, and which classic study illustrates the first one?
<details>Power of the situation, construal, automatic vs. controlled processing. The seminarian "Good Samaritan" study (Darley & Batson 1973) illustrates situational power: being in a hurry mattered far more than what participants were about to preach on.</details>

2. (Explain-why) Why did the "please don't take wood, many visitors do" sign in Petrified Forest increase theft?
<details>It communicated a descriptive norm (many people steal) that contradicted the injunctive norm (don't steal). People follow what others do more than what they are told to approve; the sign normalized the behavior.</details>

3. (Apply) A twelve-person team produces less output per person than when it was five. Name two mechanisms and one fix for each.
<details>Social loafing (pooled, unidentifiable effort — fix: individual contributions visible, smaller sub-units) and diffusion of responsibility (no one owns the task — fix: named ownership per item). Also possible: coordination loss, which is not a psychological mechanism.</details>

4. (Spot the misconception) "Milgram showed that most people are secretly willing to hurt others when told to." What is wrong here?
<details>Obedience rates dropped sharply with victim proximity, absent authority, or a disobeying peer; participants were distressed and mostly complied because they trusted the institution and had no graceful exit. Archival work suggests engaged followership, not sadism. The behavior replicates; "secretly willing" is the wrong reading.</details>

5. (Recall) State the difference between informational and normative conformity and give the paradigmatic study for each.
<details>Informational: others are a source of truth (Sherif's autokinetic effect). Normative: fear of disapproval (Asch's lines, where the answer was obvious).</details>

6. (Apply) You want engineers to write tests without paying a bonus. Which chapter mechanisms would you use, and which would you avoid?
<details>Use: small public commitments (dissonance/self-perception), descriptive norms (show that most PRs include tests), injunctive signals, foot-in-the-door (ask for one test first). Avoid: large bonuses (overjustification, compliance without belief) and messaging that reveals most people skip tests.</details>

7. (Explain-why) Why is group polarization dangerous for a homogeneous hiring committee, and what would you change?
<details>Discussion supplies persuasive arguments mostly on the side the group already leans, and members shift to be at least as extreme as the perceived norm. Collect independent judgments before discussion, seed dissent, and have the chair speak last.</details>

8. (Spot the misconception) "Our implicit-bias training will reduce discriminatory hiring." Evaluate.
<details>Changing implicit measures does not reliably change behavior (Forscher et al. 2019); the IAT predicts individual behavior weakly. Structured procedures (criteria set in advance, blind stages) and cooperative contact have evidence; training mostly does not.</details>

9. (Recall) Which of these failed to replicate: ego depletion, Asch conformity, power posing hormones, fundamental attribution error, stereotype threat on women's math?
<details>Failed or weak: ego depletion (Hagger 2016 d = 0.04), power posing hormones (Ranehill 2015), stereotype threat on women's math (Flore & Wicherts 2015 corrected d ~0.07). Robust: Asch conformity, FAE.</details>

10. (Apply) A newly merged team of two former rivals is not sharing information. What does the intergroup chapter prescribe, with what conditions?
<details>Structured contact meeting Allport's conditions: equal status, a superordinate goal that neither subgroup can achieve alone, genuine cooperation, and visible institutional support; plus cross-cutting assignments and a shared identity label. Proximity or a social event alone will not do it.</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- Taking "the power of the situation" to mean people are infinitely malleable. Effect sizes are moderate; the point is that situations are *underestimated*, not all-powerful.
- Treating every classic in the book as settled. The methods chapter warns about replication; readers skip it and then cite Zimbardo.
- Reading conformity and obedience as human weakness to be eliminated. The book's own account is that informational conformity is usually adaptive and that authority works because institutions are usually legitimate; the design goal is dissent channels and exits, not zero conformity.
- Assuming descriptive-norm messaging always helps. It backfires (the boomerang) for people already better than the norm unless an injunctive signal is added.
- Using groupthink as a diagnosis. It is a checklist of symptoms; the causal package is unsupported.
- Reading the culture material as "East vs. West." The mechanism is independent vs. interdependent self-construal, which varies within countries, regions, classes and even by momentary priming.
- Concluding from the bystander literature that crowds make people passive. In genuinely dangerous real emergencies, someone almost always intervenes; the effect is strongest in ambiguous, low-risk situations.
