# The Bounds of Reason: Game Theory and the Unification of the Behavioral Sciences — Herbert Gintis (Princeton, 2009; revised ed. 2014)
Level: Synthesis  ·  Priority: Core (read this file as a critical exercise, not as gospel)
Est. reading time saved: ~14h  ·  Your time with this file: ~35 min

## The book in one sentence
Game theory is the indispensable grammar of social interaction, but it cannot by itself predict what people do: you must add a model of the individual (beliefs, preferences, constraints), an account of where beliefs and preferences come from (evolution and culture), and a social-level device (norms as "choreographers" implementing correlated equilibria) — and once you do, economics, psychology, sociology, anthropology and biology can share a single framework.

## The book in one paragraph
Gintis, an economist who spent decades running cross-cultural experiments and working with evolutionary biologists, argues that the behavioral sciences are in "disarray": economics assumes self-interested, hyper-rational agents; sociology assumes norm-following socialized agents; psychology catalogues anomalies; biology models fitness rather than choice. Each is partly right and mutually inconsistent. His proposed fix has three layers. First, keep rational-choice theory, but interpret it modestly: it is a theory of *consistent* preferences (including altruistic, spiteful, and other-regarding ones), not of selfishness or cognitive perfection. Second, keep game theory, but recognize that its central solution concept, Nash equilibrium, cannot be justified from rationality alone: even common knowledge of rationality only gets you rationalizability, and the extra assumptions needed to reach Nash — shared conjectures, common priors — are *social* facts, not deductions. Third, fill the gap with Aumann's correlated equilibrium: a social norm acts as a "choreographer," an external signal that tells each player what to do, and following it is a best response if others follow it too. Norms, in this view, are the missing piece that lets game theory work; the readiness to follow norms is itself a product of gene–culture coevolution. The last chapters sketch how property rights (territoriality, endowment effects) evolved and lay out the unification program. Reviewers admired the ambition and the careful exposition of epistemic game theory but questioned whether the program is achieved or only announced.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
Everything before this file gave you pieces: cultural learning (Henrich, Laland), individual psychology (Gilovich et al.), the formal language of games (Dixit et al., Schelling), lab evidence on where people depart from the formal model (Camerer), and accounts of how norms, institutions and networks stabilize behavior (Skyrms, Bicchieri, Ostrom, Easley & Kleinberg). Gintis claims all of that fits into one framework. Read it asking: **does the synthesis actually do work, or does it just rename what the other books already showed?** For your design brief, the practical payoff is one sharp idea — that a norm is a coordinating signal that makes a good equilibrium *focal and self-enforcing*, so designing the signal is often more powerful than designing incentives — and one sharp caution: the formal machinery that makes this idea precise leans on assumptions (common priors, shared conjectures) that the rest of the reading list shows are exactly what fails in real groups.

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

### Ch 1 — Decision Theory and Human Behavior
- Core claims: The rational actor model (Savage-style expected utility over consistent preferences) is a theory of *preference consistency*, not of selfishness, materialism or conscious calculation. Beliefs, preferences and constraints (the "BPC model") are the minimal ingredients for any science of choice. Most alleged refutations of rationality are either (a) refutations of the selfishness add-on, which Gintis discards, or (b) performance errors under uncertainty, which he treats as a separate issue from the consistency of preferences.
- Key model/example: Gintis reframes prospect-theory anomalies (loss aversion, reference dependence — Kahneman & Tversky 1979) as facts about the *state-dependence* of preferences rather than as failures of rationality: if the current endowment is part of the "state," a loss-averse agent can still have consistent preferences. Time inconsistency (hyperbolic discounting) is likewise treated as a preference structure, not irrationality.
- So-what: for design purposes, drop the question "are people rational?" and ask "what are their beliefs, what do they actually value (including fairness and status), and what constrains them?" That triple is your diagnostic checklist for any group.

### Ch 2 — Game Theory: Basic Concepts
- Core claims: Standard toolkit — normal and extensive forms, dominance, Nash equilibrium, mixed strategies, subgame perfection, and crucially **correlated equilibrium** (Aumann 1974, 1987), presented not as a footnote but as a first-class concept.
- Key model: Correlated equilibrium. A third party (or a public signal) draws a joint recommendation from a distribution over strategy profiles and privately tells each player their part. If, given the recommendation and what it implies about others' recommendations, no player wants to deviate, the distribution is a correlated equilibrium. Every Nash equilibrium (and every mixture of Nash equilibria) is a correlated equilibrium, but many correlated equilibria are not Nash. Worked example (the Chicken/Hawk–Dove game): two Nash equilibria in pure strategies (one swerves, one doesn't) and a mixed one with a bad expected payoff. A traffic light that says "you go / you wait" with equal probability gives each driver the other's pure-equilibrium payoff half the time — higher on average than the mixed equilibrium, and self-enforcing, because when the light says "wait" you believe the other was told "go."
- So-what: the traffic light is the template for every norm Gintis will discuss. A designer's first question becomes: what public signal tells people which of several equilibria we are playing?

### Ch 3 — Game Theory and Human Behavior
- Core claims: Experimental economics shows people are not selfish (ultimatum, dictator, public goods with punishment, trust games), but does not show they are inconsistent. The evidence supports "strong reciprocity": a predisposition to cooperate conditionally and to punish defectors at personal cost, even in one-shot anonymous settings. Preferences are partly other-regarding and partly *character-virtue*-like (honesty, promise-keeping valued for their own sake).
- Key studies: Ultimatum game (Güth, Schmittberger & Schwarze 1982); public goods with costly punishment (Fehr & Gächter 2000, 2002); cross-cultural ultimatum/dictator games in fifteen small-scale societies (Henrich, Boyd, Bowles, Camerer, Fehr, Gintis et al. 2001, 2004) showing the *level* of offers varies with market integration and cooperation in daily life, while pure self-interest is rejected everywhere. Gintis also reads Andreoni-style warm-glow and Rabin-style fairness models as instances of the BPC framework.
- So-what: your group is not full of free riders waiting to be bribed; it is full of conditional cooperators who will punish and who need to *see* others cooperating. Design for visibility of contribution and for cheap, legitimate sanctioning.

### Ch 4 — Rationalizability and Common Knowledge of Rationality
- Core claims: What does rationality plus common knowledge of rationality (CKR) actually license? Only rationalizability (iterated elimination of never-best-responses; Bernheim 1984, Pearce 1984). In many games — most coordination games, Chicken, matching pennies — rationalizability leaves almost everything on the table. Nash equilibrium requires something more: that players' *conjectures about each other* coincide. Nothing in individual rationality delivers that.
- Key example: In a 2x2 coordination game every strategy is rationalizable; CKR says nothing about which equilibrium is played. In the Traveler's Dilemma (Basu 1994) iterated dominance predicts the lowest claim, which humans reliably do not choose — Gintis uses this both against naive Nash and against the assumption that people reason to the fixed point.
- So-what: "rational agents will find the equilibrium" is a myth. If you want a group to land on an equilibrium, something outside the players' rationality has to point at it.

### Ch 5 — Extensive Form Rationalizability
- Core claims: Extends the analysis to sequential games. Backward induction (subgame perfection) requires CKR to hold even after moves that CKR says would never happen — an internal contradiction first pressed by Binmore (1987) and Reny. Gintis argues that forward induction and the "epistemic" reading of extensive-form rationalizability are more defensible but still do not deliver unique predictions.
- Key example: The centipede game (Rosenthal 1981): backward induction says take the pot at the first node; subjects (McKelvey & Palfrey 1992) go several rounds. Gintis's reading: the observed behavior is rational given beliefs that the partner might be a reciprocator, and CKR is simply false in the lab.
- So-what: in multi-stage relationships (onboarding, contracts, escalation ladders), the "unravelling" prediction — nobody cooperates because the last step will be defected — routinely fails. Reputation and conditional beliefs, not the logic of backward induction, govern what happens.

### Ch 6 — The Mixing Problem: Purification and Conjectures
- Core claims: Mixed-strategy Nash equilibria are conceptually troubled: at the equilibrium each player is indifferent among the strategies being mixed, so there is no reason to mix in exactly the equilibrium proportions. Harsanyi's (1973) purification (small private payoff perturbations make the mixing emerge from pure strategies) is the standard fix; Gintis prefers to read mixed equilibria as *conjectures*: what each player believes about the population of opponents, sustained by social process rather than individual choice.
- So-what: a mixed equilibrium in your group (say, a fraction of members who monitor and a fraction who do not) is a *population* fact stabilized by norms and learning, not a randomization each person performs. That points to a Skyrms-style population dynamics reading (see 07-stag-hunt).

### Ch 7 — Bayesian Rationality and Social Epistemology
- Core claims: The Savage axioms make rational agents Bayesian, but they say nothing about where priors come from. The common-prior assumption (Harsanyi doctrine) that underpins much of game theory is not derivable from rationality; it is a substantive claim that agents share a *culture* — a common stock of beliefs. Gintis proposes that the source of common priors is social: shared cultural transmission, imitation and language make beliefs converge. This is where he most directly connects epistemic game theory to Henrich-style cultural evolution.
- Key model: Aumann's (1976) "agreeing to disagree" theorem — Bayesians with a common prior and common knowledge of each other's posteriors must have equal posteriors — is read as showing how much the common prior does. Gintis's own move: replace "common prior" with "shared cultural frame," which is empirically checkable.
- So-what: if two sub-teams keep reaching different conclusions from the same data, the problem may be different priors, i.e., different cultures. Aligning beliefs is a cultural intervention (shared training, shared stories, shared metrics), not an information intervention.

### Ch 8 — Common Knowledge and Nash Equilibrium
- Core claims: The chapter's technical spine. Aumann & Brandenburger (1995) showed that for two-player games, mutual knowledge of rationality plus mutual knowledge of the conjectures (and for more players, common prior plus common knowledge of conjectures) yields Nash equilibrium. Gintis's point: these are *epistemic* conditions on what players know about each other, and no plausible theory of individual reasoning produces them. Hence Nash equilibrium is not "what rational agents do" but "what agents do when a social process has aligned their conjectures." Correlated equilibrium is the natural object once you take that seriously, because Aumann (1987) showed that Bayesian rationality with a common prior yields exactly correlated (not Nash) equilibrium.
- Key result stated plainly: if everyone is Bayesian-rational and shares a prior over the states that determine what each player does, then the joint distribution of play *is* a correlated equilibrium. The common prior is the choreographer in disguise.
- So-what: the notion "an agreement is stable because it is a Nash equilibrium" should be replaced by "an agreement is stable because there is a shared, trusted signal everyone conditions on." Ask what your group's signal is: a written policy, a leader's word, a visible dashboard, a ritual.

### Ch 9 — Reflective Reason and Equilibrium Refinements
- Core claims: The equilibrium-refinement program of the 1980s (perfect, proper, sequential, stable equilibria; Selten, Myerson, Kreps & Wilson, Kohlberg & Mertens) tried to fix multiplicity by adding rationality-flavored conditions. Gintis argues it failed on its own terms: refinements conflict, depend on arbitrary tremble structures, and are not justified by any epistemic story. The "local best response" criterion he offers instead is deliberately weak. His broader point: multiplicity is real, and its resolution is a *social* matter (which equilibrium the culture selects), not a logical one.
- So-what: do not expect cleverness to pick the equilibrium. Expect history, focal points (Schelling), and explicit norms to pick it — and design the norm.

### Ch 10 — The Analytics of Human Sociality
- Core claims: The philosophical heart of the book. Social norms are modeled as choreographers implementing correlated equilibria of the underlying game. Two features are essential. (1) *Epistemic*: the norm supplies the common conjecture — each person knows what others will do because the norm says so. (2) *Motivational*: humans have an evolved, culturally sharpened disposition to follow norms (a "normative predisposition") and to punish violators, so that the choreographer's instructions are followed even where the underlying game gives some incentive to deviate. This lets norms sustain outcomes that are correlated equilibria only when the "normative" payoff term is included. Gintis compares this with Durkheim and Parsons on the internalization of norms and with Lewis (1969) and Bicchieri (2006) on conventions.
- Key example: A norm like "the person who was here first has the right of way" or "contribute your share on the visible board" is a public signal. If each person believes the others follow it and mildly prefers to comply (or fears sanction), it is self-enforcing. The traffic light, the office rotation, the seating convention — all one mechanism.
- So-what: this is the single most useful chapter. It gives you a two-part test for any norm you want to install in a group: does it *tell people unambiguously what to do* (epistemic), and *do people have any reason to comply given that others do* (motivational)? Most failed norms fail one of the two.

### Ch 11 — The Evolution of Property Rights
- Core claims: Private property is not a modern invention but an extension of territoriality found across animals: the "bourgeois" strategy (Maynard Smith 1982) — defend if you are the incumbent, yield if you are the intruder — is an evolutionarily stable, low-conflict convention. It is also a correlated equilibrium of Hawk–Dove with "who arrived first" as the correlating signal. Gintis links this to the endowment effect (Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler 1990) as a psychological correlate: valuing what you hold more than what you do not is what makes the incumbent's defense credible.
- Key model: Hawk–Dove with a possession cue. Uncorrelated play yields costly fights; conditioning on possession yields a stable convention in which fights are rare. Gintis works through when the "anti-bourgeois" (paradoxical) convention could arise and why it rarely does.
- So-what: ownership norms (who "owns" a codebase, a channel, a decision) reduce conflict primarily by being *clear and salient*, not by being fair. Ambiguous ownership is a Hawk–Dove game without a correlating signal.

### Ch 12 — The Unification of the Behavioral Sciences
- Core claims: A programmatic essay. Five principles: gene–culture coevolution (from biology and anthropology); the sociopsychological theory of norms (sociology, with the choreographer as its formalization); game theory (universal lexicon); the rational actor model (from economics, purged of selfishness); and complexity theory (societies as complex adaptive systems, requiring agent-based modeling as a complement to analytic equilibrium). Gintis argues these are mutually compatible and jointly cover the behavioral sciences' explananda. He also contrasts this with sociology's over-socialized agent and economics' under-socialized agent.
- So-what: use the five principles as a checklist for whether your diagnosis of a group has left out a layer — evolved dispositions, culture, norms, rational responses to incentives, and system-level dynamics that no one intends.

### Ch 13 — Summary
- Short recap of the argument, ending with norms as correlated equilibria and the BPC model as a shared basis. Nothing new; useful as a fast re-read.

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. **Nash equilibrium is a social achievement, not a rational deduction.** Rationality and common knowledge of rationality yield only rationalizability; Nash needs aligned conjectures that must come from somewhere outside the players' heads.
2. **Norms are choreographers.** A social norm is a public signal that tells each person what to do and, in so doing, makes everyone's conjectures consistent. Following it is a best response when others follow it. This is Aumann's correlated equilibrium with the norm as correlating device.
3. **The BPC model (beliefs, preferences, constraints) is the minimal common language.** Rational choice means consistent preferences, which can be altruistic, spiteful, fairness-driven or norm-following; "rational" is not "selfish."
4. **Common priors are culture.** The Harsanyi doctrine is not a theorem about rationality; it is an empirical claim that people share a belief-forming culture. Belief alignment is a cultural intervention.
5. **Norm-following is motivated, not just informed.** The choreographer works because humans have an evolved and culturally trained disposition to comply with norms and to punish violators (strong reciprocity), not merely because the norm is informative.
6. **Equilibrium multiplicity is resolved socially.** Refinements failed; history, focal points and norms select equilibria.
7. **Property is a convention with a cue.** Ownership norms resolve Hawk–Dove conflicts by conditioning on a salient signal (possession), and the endowment effect is the psychological correlate that makes them stick.
8. **Unification is a five-part program** (gene–culture coevolution, norms, game theory, rational actor, complexity), offered as a framework rather than a finished theory.

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **BPC model** — beliefs, preferences, constraints; the rational actor model stripped of selfishness — bites when someone claims a group "isn't rational": look for the preference or belief you have mis-specified.
- **Rationalizability** — strategies that survive iterated deletion of never-best-responses — bites when you expect "smart people will converge": they may not, because rationalizability is often everything.
- **Common knowledge of rationality (CKR)** — everyone is rational, everyone knows it, everyone knows everyone knows, ad infinitum — bites in extensive-form games where an off-path move contradicts CKR.
- **Correlated equilibrium** — a distribution over joint actions, mediated by a signal, from which no one profits by deviating — bites whenever you can install a signal (schedule, rota, dashboard, referee).
- **Choreographer** — Gintis's name for the correlating device when it is a social norm — bites when a norm is vague: a vague norm is a choreographer that does not choreograph.
- **Common prior / Harsanyi doctrine** — all players' beliefs derive from one prior updated on private information — bites when sub-groups disagree persistently despite shared data.
- **Purification** — reading a mixed equilibrium as pure strategies under small private payoff noise — bites when you see stable fractions of behaviors in a population.
- **Strong reciprocity** — conditional cooperation plus costly punishment of defectors, even one-shot — bites when designing sanctions: people will pay to punish, so give them a channel that is legitimate and proportionate.
- **Normative predisposition** — evolved readiness to internalize and follow norms — bites when you rely on incentives alone: you leave the cheapest motivational lever unused.
- **Gene–culture coevolution** — genes shape learning; culture shapes selection on genes — bites as background: why norm-following exists at all.

## Evidence strength & limits
**What is robust.** The formal results Gintis leans on are theorems: Aumann (1974, 1987) on correlated equilibrium and Bayesian rationality; Aumann & Brandenburger (1995) on the epistemic conditions for Nash; Bernheim and Pearce on rationalizability. The experimental facts in chapter 3 — ultimatum rejections, conditional cooperation, costly punishment, cross-cultural variation in offers — replicate well and have been confirmed in large multi-lab efforts. The rejection of the pure self-interest hypothesis is about as secure as anything in behavioral science.

**What is contested.**
- *Strong reciprocity as an evolved trait.* Gintis (with Bowles, Boyd, Fehr) argues it evolved by gene–culture coevolution and group selection. Binmore, and Binmore & Shaked (2010, *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*), argue the one-shot lab evidence is consistent with subjects mis-applying heuristics tuned for repeated interaction, that learning erodes cooperation in many designs, and that the "strong reciprocity" literature overstated its case; Guala (2012, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*) argued field evidence for *costly* punishment in small-scale societies is thin — sanctioning in the wild is mostly cheap (gossip, ostracism). This is background knowledge, not verified in this session, but it is a well-known dispute.
- *Whether the "modest rational actor" is falsifiable.* Once preferences may be other-regarding, state-dependent and norm-inclusive, critics (a line associated with Binmore's and Sugden's methodological writings; also raised in the philosophy-of-economics literature on the "rescue" of rational choice via social preferences) ask what observation could refute the BPC model. Gintis's answer — revealed-preference axioms such as transitivity are testable and sometimes fail — is correct but weaker than the book's rhetoric suggests.
- *The choreographer as explanation vs. redescription.* Peter Vanderschraaf's review (*Economics and Philosophy*, 2011; existence verified, content from search snippets and background) engages the correlated-equilibrium account sympathetically — he had himself developed the idea that conventions are correlated equilibria — while pressing that the account still needs an explanation of *which* correlated equilibrium a society lands on and how the choreographer's signal is learned. That is the same gap that Nash multiplicity left, moved one level up.
- *The unification thesis.* Reviews by Jean-Robert Tyran (*Journal of Economics*, 2010), Juliette Rouchier (*JASSS*, 2010) and Alexander Field (*Journal of Economic History*, 2011) — existence verified via search results — broadly praise the exposition of epistemic game theory and the framing of game theory as necessary but insufficient, and note (Rouchier explicitly) that Gintis claims complementarity rather than full coverage. The recurring charge, in these and in later discussion, is that the "unification" is *programmatic*: chapter 12 lists five compatible principles and asserts that they suffice, but the book never derives a sociological or anthropological result from the framework that those disciplines lacked. Nobody in sociology has to change a model because of chapter 12.
- *Binmore's specific critique.* I could not locate or verify a Binmore review of this book. What is verified in the broader literature: Binmore's own work (*Natural Justice*, 2005; "Do conventions need to be common knowledge?", *Topoi*, 2008) also treats conventions as equilibria selected by social process and is skeptical of common-knowledge assumptions — so Binmore and Gintis are closer on the *epistemic* critique than on *preferences*. Their real fight is over strong reciprocity and group selection. Don Ross (2006, *Politics, Philosophy & Economics*) defended Binmore's theory of justice against Gintis's earlier criticisms — verified via search snippet. Treat any claim that "Binmore panned The Bounds of Reason" as unverified.
- *Sugden.* No Sugden review of this book was found. Sugden reviewed Gintis et al.'s *Moral Sentiments and Material Interests* (Economica; existence verified, content not). His standing critique of the social-preferences program — that it stuffs sociality into individual utility functions and thereby misses team reasoning and mutual advantage — applies to chapter 3 and is worth carrying in mind, but attribute it to his general position, not to a review of this book.
- *Complexity theory* (principle five) is asserted, not used. There are no agent-based models in the book.

**What Gintis argues vs. shows.** He *shows* (with theorems) that Nash cannot be grounded in rationality alone and that correlated equilibrium is what Bayesian rationality plus a common prior gives you. He *argues* that social norms are the empirical realization of the correlating device, that norm-following is an evolved disposition, and that this closes the gap between economics and sociology. The first two are plausible and partly supported by other books on this list; the third is a promissory note.

## Design implications for cooperation in real groups
- **Name the signal.** For any coordination problem, specify the public cue that tells each person which equilibrium is in play (rota, calendar, written rule, referee, dashboard). Ambiguity is the failure mode, not bad incentives. [E — correlated-equilibrium logic plus Schelling/Lewis convention evidence]
- **Run the two-part norm test.** Before installing a norm, check (a) epistemic: can every member state what the norm requires of *them* and of *others* in the situation? (b) motivational: given others comply, does anyone gain by deviating, and if so, is there a cheap sanction? [H — the test follows from the model; whether it predicts norm survival in your group is to be tested]
- **Stop trying to reason people into equilibrium.** Meetings that try to derive the "obviously rational" plan reproduce the refinement program's failure. Pick a focal option, announce it, and make it the default. [E — refinement literature; Camerer's coordination-game evidence]
- **Align priors culturally, not by more data.** Persistent disagreement across sub-teams with the same data indicates different priors; fix with shared training, shared metrics definitions and shared narrative rather than more dashboards. [H]
- **Treat ownership as a conflict-reducing convention and make it salient.** Assign clear owners even when the assignment is somewhat arbitrary; ambiguity produces Hawk–Dove fights. [E — bourgeois-strategy results; organizational evidence is weaker]
- **Budget for legitimate punishment.** Members will sanction free riders at a cost to themselves; give them a low-cost, proportionate, public-enough channel (Ostrom's graduated sanctions) so punishment does not go underground. [E — costly punishment in the lab; field evidence favors cheap sanctions, so prefer gossip-grade channels over formal penalties]
- **Do not model the group as selfish, and do not model it as saintly.** Use BPC: what do members believe about each other, what do they value (including status and fairness), what constrains them? Most "incentive" fixes fail because they change constraints while beliefs about what others will do stay the same. [E]
- **Prefer the norm to the incentive when both are available.** Norms exploit an existing motivational disposition and are cheaper to maintain; incentives can crowd out that disposition. [H — crowding-out evidence is real but context-dependent]
- **Do not expect the unification to tell you which layer matters.** The framework is a checklist, not a decision procedure; the ordering of interventions must come from the other books and from your own diagnostic. [V — a methodological commitment of this course]

- **04-games-of-strategy** (Dixit et al.): supplies the toolkit Gintis assumes; Gintis is the critical commentary on chapter-level claims like "rational players reach Nash."
- **05-micromotives-and-macrobehavior** (Schelling): focal points are choreographers without an author; Schelling anticipated the entire "social selection of equilibria" theme. Agreement.
- **06-behavioral-game-theory** (Camerer): Camerer's evidence on social preferences, coordination and learning is the empirical base for chapters 3 and 4; Camerer's learning models (EWA) are an alternative, non-epistemic account of how conjectures align that Gintis under-uses. Partial tension: Camerer explains convergence by learning; Gintis by norms.
- **07-stag-hunt** (Skyrms): Skyrms's dynamics answer the question Gintis leaves open — which correlated equilibrium gets selected and how correlation arises (local interaction, signaling, network formation). Skyrms is the mechanism behind the choreographer. Complementary; Skyrms is more empirical about origins.
- **08-grammar-of-society** (Bicchieri): the closest cousin. Bicchieri's norm = conditional preference plus empirical and normative expectations; Gintis's norm = correlating signal plus normative predisposition. They agree that norms are equilibria of games transformed by expectations. Bicchieri's account is more precise about *when a norm fails* (expectations mismatch) and about measurement; Gintis's is more precise about the equilibrium concept.
- **09-governing-the-commons** (Ostrom): Ostrom's design principles are the field evidence for what a working choreographer looks like — clear boundaries, monitoring, graduated sanctions, congruence with local conditions. Ostrom shows the choreographer is built and repaired by the participants, which Gintis's "external signal" framing obscures.
- **10-networks-crowds-markets** (Easley & Kleinberg): network structure determines who sees the signal and who observes whom; a choreographer that is not visible across the network does not correlate play. Cascades and network games are correlated equilibria sustained by local observation. Complementary.

## Retrieval practice

1. **Recall.** What does common knowledge of rationality license as a solution concept, according to Gintis, and why is that not Nash equilibrium?
<details>Rationalizability: iterated elimination of never-best-responses. Nash additionally requires that players' conjectures about each other coincide (Aumann & Brandenburger 1995 conditions), which individual rationality cannot generate.</details>

2. **Explain-why.** Why is a traffic light a better analogy for a social norm than a rule book?
<details>A traffic light is a correlating device: it sends each driver a private-but-related instruction and it is a best response to obey given that others obey. It produces a correlated equilibrium that beats the mixed Nash outcome. A rule book without a shared signal or shared belief that others follow it has no such self-enforcing property.</details>

3. **Apply.** A remote team has two viable release cadences; half the engineers assume weekly, half assume fortnightly, and merges keep colliding. Diagnose in Gintis's terms and propose a fix.
<details>A coordination game with two equilibria and no choreographer; conjectures are misaligned. Fix: install a public signal (a published, pinned cadence with a visible calendar) so each engineer's best response, given the signal, is to conform. Incentives are unnecessary; the epistemic part is what is missing.</details>

4. **Spot the misconception.** "Gintis shows that people are irrational, so game theory is useless for real groups."
<details>Wrong on both counts. Gintis defends the rational actor model (as preference consistency) and game theory (as lexicon); his target is the claim that rationality alone yields Nash play. He wants game theory embedded in a theory of norms, not discarded.</details>

5. **Recall.** State the two components a norm must have to function as a choreographer.
<details>Epistemic (it tells each player what to do and what others will do, aligning conjectures) and motivational (players have a disposition, or face sanctions, so that compliance is a best response given others' compliance).</details>

6. **Explain-why.** Why does Gintis say the common-prior assumption is really a claim about culture?
<details>Bayesian rationality says nothing about where priors come from; a common prior across people can only arise if they share a belief-forming process. In humans that process is cultural transmission, so "common prior" is an empirical claim about shared culture.</details>

7. **Apply.** Two product squads look at identical metrics and reach opposite conclusions week after week. Which Gintis chapter is relevant and what intervention does it suggest?
<details>Chapter 7 (Bayesian rationality and social epistemology). Persistent disagreement on shared data signals different priors. Intervention: cultural alignment (shared definitions, joint training, shared narratives), not more data.</details>

8. **Spot the misconception.** "Because ownership norms reduce conflict, the fairest allocation of ownership will reduce conflict the most."
<details>The bourgeois-strategy result says conflict falls because the cue (possession) is salient and mutually recognized, not because it is fair. A fair but ambiguous allocation can produce more fights than a clear arbitrary one. Fairness matters for other reasons (legitimacy, willingness to sanction), which Ostrom and Bicchieri supply.</details>

9. **Explain-why.** What is the main charge that the unification is "programmatic rather than achieved"?
<details>Chapter 12 lists five compatible principles and asserts they cover the behavioral sciences, but the book derives no new result in sociology, anthropology or psychology from the framework; it re-describes existing findings in a common vocabulary. Compatibility of principles is not the same as a unified theory that makes predictions the parts could not.</details>

10. **Apply.** You have installed a clear contribution norm on a visible board; conjectures are aligned; but compliance is still low. Which part of Gintis's account has failed and which other reading helps?
<details>The motivational part: given others comply, some members still profit by deviating and no sanction bites. Ostrom (graduated sanctions, monitoring by participants) and Bicchieri (normative expectations: do people believe others *expect* them to comply?) supply the repair; Camerer's learning evidence warns that cooperation decays without feedback.</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- **"Gintis rejects rational choice."** He rejects the selfishness assumption and the claim that rationality yields Nash; he keeps consistent-preference rationality as the common core.
- **"Correlated equilibrium replaces Nash because it fits data better."** The argument is conceptual, not empirical: correlated equilibrium is what Bayesian rationality plus a common prior gives you (Aumann 1987), and Nash needs stronger, socially supplied conditions. The book does not run a horse race on data.
- **"The choreographer is a person."** It is any correlating signal: a norm, a convention, a clock, a possession cue. Leaders can be choreographers, but the concept is about the signal, not the authority.
- **"The book proves that norms are internalized by evolution."** It argues that a normative predisposition evolved by gene–culture coevolution, drawing on the Bowles–Gintis–Boyd–Richerson program; that claim is contested (Binmore & Shaked; Guala) and the book does not itself provide the evolutionary models.
- **"The unification is done."** It is a framework and a checklist. Reviewers consistently note that it is an agenda.

## Critical assessment: which claims hold up against the rest of the reading list
The purpose of this file in the course is to assess an attempted synthesis. Claim by claim:

**Claim 1 — Rationality plus common knowledge of rationality does not yield Nash equilibrium; Nash rests on socially supplied alignment of conjectures.**
*Verdict: holds up, and is the book's most durable contribution.* This is a theorem-backed claim (Aumann & Brandenburger 1995) and every empirical source on the list supports the corollary that people do not reason their way to equilibrium. Camerer's coordination experiments show convergence is slow, path-dependent and history-driven; Schelling showed focal points do the selecting; Skyrms showed that dynamics, not deduction, pick equilibria. Nothing on the list contradicts it. For the design brief, this is the license to stop trying to *argue* a group into a coordinated plan.

**Claim 2 — Social norms are choreographers implementing correlated equilibria.**
*Verdict: holds up as a model, with a caveat about what it leaves unexplained.* Bicchieri's account is formally compatible: a norm transforms a game by adding conditional preferences and expectations, and the resulting behavior is an equilibrium of the transformed game conditioned on a shared cue. Lewis-style conventions (in Skyrms and Bicchieri) are the same idea. Where the choreographer model is *weaker* than Bicchieri's is diagnosis: Bicchieri distinguishes empirical from normative expectations and tells you which one to measure when a norm fails, whereas Gintis's signal-plus-predisposition story treats compliance as a lump. Where it is weaker than Ostrom's is authorship: Ostrom's commons show choreographers being written, monitored and revised by the players themselves, which the "external signal" framing hides. Where it is weaker than Skyrms's is origins: Gintis says a norm *is* a correlating device; Skyrms shows *how* correlation emerges from local interaction, signaling and network formation without anyone designing it. So the claim is true, but the three Order-level books are each more useful for diagnosis than the formalization is.

**Claim 3 — The BPC model (consistent preferences, possibly other-regarding) is the right common core, and behavioral anomalies are mostly mis-specified preferences or beliefs, not irrationality.**
*Verdict: partially holds up; overstated.* Camerer's evidence on social preferences is consistent with adding fairness and reciprocity terms to utility, and that move has been productive. But Gilovich et al. and the wider social-psychology literature (construal, framing, motivated reasoning, self-serving bias) describe *beliefs* that shift with presentation and mood, which is not tidily captured by state-dependent preferences; Camerer's own learning chapter shows behavior that is adaptive and path-dependent rather than fixed-point rational. Henrich adds that preferences themselves are culturally acquired and vary systematically across societies, which the BPC model can accommodate as data but does not predict. The honest summary is: BPC is a serviceable *bookkeeping* language, and the falsifiability worry is real — once anything can be a preference, the model constrains little without auxiliary theory. Those auxiliary theories are what the rest of this reading list is.

**Claim 4 — Common priors are culture; belief alignment is a cultural process.**
*Verdict: holds up, and is well supported by Henrich and Laland.* Cultural learning biases (conformity, prestige) are exactly the mechanisms by which priors converge within a group and diverge between groups. Henrich's cross-cultural game results — which Gintis co-authored — are direct evidence that "priors" about fair offers are socially shared. Easley & Kleinberg add the structural qualifier: belief convergence follows network structure, so a group with weak cross-ties can host two cultures and two priors. The claim survives; the design implication (align culture, not data) is sound.

**Claim 5 — Norm-following is an evolved disposition (strong reciprocity), and this supplies the motivational half of the choreographer.**
*Verdict: contested; the evidence supports a weaker version.* The lab evidence for conditional cooperation and willingness to punish is robust. The evolutionary claim — that costly punishment evolved via gene–culture coevolution and cultural group selection — is disputed by Binmore & Shaked and by Guala's review of field evidence, which finds that real-world sanctioning is mostly cheap (gossip, withdrawal, reputation) rather than costly. Henrich's book supports cultural group selection and a norm psychology, so the *direction* is consistent with the Origins level, but Ostrom's field cases are the strongest evidence and they show *graduated*, low-cost, participant-run sanctioning rather than heroic costly punishment. Design lesson: rely on the cheap version.

**Claim 6 — Equilibrium refinements failed; selection is social.**
*Verdict: holds up.* The refinement program is widely regarded as having run out of road; Camerer's and Schelling's evidence is that history and salience select. No book on the list dissents.

**Claim 7 — Property rights are conventions with a salient cue (bourgeois strategy), with the endowment effect as psychological support.**
*Verdict: holds up as a model; the psychological link is shakier than presented.* The Hawk–Dove-with-possession analysis is standard evolutionary game theory and consistent with Skyrms. The endowment effect itself is robust in many designs, but its magnitude and interpretation have been contested (some of the loss-aversion literature has faced questions about experimental procedures and boundary conditions), and treating it as the evolved psychology of property is a hypothesis, not a finding. Ostrom's cases show that stable property regimes in commons are *rule systems* with monitoring, not merely possession cues, so the cue-only story is too thin for institutional design.

**Claim 8 — The five principles unify economics, psychology, sociology, anthropology and biology.**
*Verdict: does not hold up as stated; holds up as a reading guide.* The charge that the unification is programmatic is fair. Every empirical achievement Gintis cites in support belongs to one of the other books on this list, done in that book's own idiom: Henrich's cultural evolution, Camerer's behavioral games, Ostrom's institutions, Skyrms's dynamics, Bicchieri's norms. Chapter 12 shows these are *compatible*, which is worth something — it tells you that you are not forced to choose between "people follow norms" and "people respond to incentives" — but compatibility is not unification. There is no result in the book that a sociologist or anthropologist could not have obtained without it, and the complexity principle is not used at all. The most generous reading is Rouchier's: game theory as a *complement* that keeps social theory honest about incentives and expectations, and social theory as the supplier of the priors, preferences and signals that game theory needs. On that reading the book is right, and modest — but then the title's "unification" is marketing.

**Net assessment for the course.** Carry out of Gintis two things and leave the rest: (1) the epistemic critique of Nash and the choreographer idea, which sharpen every Order-level book into a single design question — *what is the signal, and why does each person comply given the others do?* — and (2) the discipline of writing down beliefs, preferences and constraints before proposing an intervention. Do not carry out the claim that the behavioral sciences have been unified, or that norm-following is settled evolutionary fact. The synthesis is a map of how the other ten books fit together; it is not a substitute for any of them.
