# The Bounds of Reason: Game Theory and the Unification of the Behavioral Sciences — Herbert Gintis (Princeton, 2009; revised ed. 2014)
Level: Synthesis  ·  Priority: Core (read this file as a critical exercise, not as gospel)
Est. reading time saved: ~14h  ·  Your time with this file: ~35 min

## The book in one sentence
Game theory is the indispensable grammar of social interaction, but it cannot by itself predict what people do: you must add a model of the individual (beliefs, preferences, constraints), an account of where beliefs and preferences come from (evolution and culture), and a social-level device (norms as "choreographers" implementing correlated equilibria) — and once you do, economics, psychology, sociology, anthropology and biology can share a single framework.

## The book in one paragraph
The behavioral sciences are in "disarray": economics assumes self-interested, hyper-rational agents; sociology assumes norm-following socialized agents; psychology catalogues anomalies; biology models fitness rather than choice. Each is partly right and they are mutually inconsistent. Gintis's fix has three layers. First, keep rational choice, read modestly: a theory of *consistent* preferences (including altruistic and other-regarding ones), not of selfishness or cognitive perfection. Second, keep game theory, but admit that Nash equilibrium cannot be justified from rationality alone: common knowledge of rationality only yields rationalizability, and the extra assumptions — shared conjectures, common priors — are *social* facts, not deductions. Third, fill the gap with Aumann's correlated equilibrium: a social norm is a "choreographer," an external signal telling each player what to do, and following it is a best response if others do too. The readiness to follow norms is itself a product of gene–culture coevolution. Later chapters sketch how property rights evolved and lay out the unification program. Reviewers admired the ambition and the exposition of epistemic game theory but questioned whether the program is achieved or only announced.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
Everything before this file gave you pieces; Gintis claims they fit into one framework. Read it asking: **does the synthesis actually do work, or does it just rename what the other books already showed?** The practical payoff is one sharp idea — a norm is a coordinating signal that makes a good equilibrium *focal and self-enforcing*, so designing the signal often beats designing incentives — and one caution: the machinery that makes this precise leans on assumptions (common priors, shared conjectures) that the rest of the list shows are exactly what fails in real groups.

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

### Ch 1 — Decision Theory and Human Behavior
- Core claims: The rational actor model (Savage-style expected utility over consistent preferences) is a theory of *preference consistency*, not of selfishness, materialism or conscious calculation. Beliefs, preferences and constraints (the "BPC model") are the minimal ingredients for any science of choice. Most alleged refutations of rationality are either (a) refutations of the selfishness add-on, which Gintis discards, or (b) performance errors under uncertainty, which he treats as a separate issue from the consistency of preferences.
- Key model/example: prospect-theory anomalies (loss aversion, reference dependence — Kahneman & Tversky 1979) are reframed as *state-dependent* preferences: if the current endowment is part of the "state," a loss-averse agent can still be consistent. Hyperbolic discounting is treated the same way.
- So-what: drop "are people rational?" and ask "what do they believe, what do they value (including fairness and status), what constrains them?" That triple is your diagnostic checklist.

### Ch 2 — Game Theory: Basic Concepts
- Core claims: Standard toolkit — normal and extensive forms, dominance, Nash equilibrium, mixed strategies, subgame perfection, and crucially **correlated equilibrium** (Aumann 1974, 1987), presented not as a footnote but as a first-class concept.
- Key model: Correlated equilibrium. A public signal draws a joint recommendation from a distribution over strategy profiles and privately tells each player their part. If, given the recommendation and what it implies about others' recommendations, no player wants to deviate, the distribution is a correlated equilibrium. Every Nash equilibrium is one; many correlated equilibria are not Nash. Worked example (Chicken): two pure Nash equilibria (one swerves, one doesn't) and a mixed one with a poor expected payoff. A traffic light saying "go / wait" with equal probability gives each driver the good outcome half the time — better on average than the mixed equilibrium, and self-enforcing, because when it says "wait" you believe the other was told "go."
- So-what: the traffic light is the template for every norm Gintis will discuss. A designer's first question becomes: what public signal tells people which of several equilibria we are playing?

### Ch 3 — Game Theory and Human Behavior
- Core claims: Experimental economics shows people are not selfish (ultimatum, dictator, public goods with punishment, trust games), but does not show they are inconsistent. The evidence supports "strong reciprocity": a predisposition to cooperate conditionally and to punish defectors at personal cost, even in one-shot anonymous settings. Preferences are partly other-regarding and partly *character-virtue*-like (honesty, promise-keeping valued for their own sake).
- Key studies: Ultimatum game (Güth, Schmittberger & Schwarze 1982); public goods with costly punishment (Fehr & Gächter 2000, 2002); cross-cultural games in fifteen small-scale societies (Henrich, Boyd, Bowles, Camerer, Fehr, Gintis et al. 2001, 2004): offer *levels* vary with market integration and everyday cooperation, while pure self-interest is rejected everywhere.
- So-what: your group is not full of free riders waiting to be bribed but of conditional cooperators who will punish and need to *see* others cooperating. Design for visible contribution and cheap, legitimate sanctioning.

### Ch 4 — Rationalizability and Common Knowledge of Rationality
- Core claims: Rationality plus common knowledge of rationality (CKR) licenses only rationalizability (iterated elimination of never-best-responses; Bernheim 1984, Pearce 1984). In most coordination games, Chicken and matching pennies, that leaves almost everything on the table. Nash requires that players' *conjectures about each other* coincide, and nothing in individual rationality delivers that.
- Key example: In a 2x2 coordination game every strategy is rationalizable. In the Traveler's Dilemma (Basu 1994) iterated dominance predicts the lowest claim, which humans reliably do not choose.
- So-what: "rational agents will find the equilibrium" is a myth. If you want a group to land on an equilibrium, something outside the players' rationality has to point at it.

### Ch 5 — Extensive Form Rationalizability
- Core claims: Sequential games. Backward induction requires CKR to hold even after moves that CKR says would never happen — a contradiction pressed by Binmore (1987) and Reny. Forward induction is more defensible but still does not deliver unique predictions.
- Key example: Centipede game (Rosenthal 1981): backward induction says take the pot at once; subjects (McKelvey & Palfrey 1992) go several rounds. Gintis's reading: rational given beliefs that the partner may be a reciprocator; CKR is simply false in the lab.
- So-what: in multi-stage relationships the "unravelling" prediction routinely fails. Reputation and conditional beliefs, not backward induction, govern what happens.

### Ch 6 — The Mixing Problem: Purification and Conjectures
- Core claims: At a mixed Nash equilibrium each player is indifferent among the mixed strategies, so nothing motivates mixing in exactly the equilibrium proportions. Harsanyi's (1973) purification is the standard fix; Gintis prefers reading mixed equilibria as *conjectures* about the opponent population, sustained by social process.
- So-what: a mixed pattern in your group (a fraction who monitor, a fraction who do not) is a *population* fact stabilized by norms and learning, not a randomization each person performs (see 07-stag-hunt).

### Ch 7 — Bayesian Rationality and Social Epistemology
- Core claims: Savage's axioms make rational agents Bayesian but say nothing about where priors come from. The common-prior assumption (Harsanyi doctrine) is not derivable from rationality; it is a substantive claim that agents share a *culture*. Gintis locates the source of common priors in cultural transmission, imitation and language — his most direct link to Henrich-style cultural evolution.
- Key model: Aumann's (1976) agreeing-to-disagree theorem shows how much work the common prior does. Gintis's move: replace "common prior" with "shared cultural frame," which is empirically checkable.
- So-what: if two sub-teams keep reaching different conclusions from the same data, the problem may be different priors, i.e., different cultures. Aligning beliefs is a cultural intervention (shared training, shared stories, shared metrics), not an information intervention.

### Ch 8 — Common Knowledge and Nash Equilibrium
- Core claims: The technical spine. Aumann & Brandenburger (1995): for two players, mutual knowledge of rationality plus mutual knowledge of conjectures yields Nash; for more players, add a common prior and common knowledge of conjectures. These are *epistemic* conditions on what players know about each other, and no theory of individual reasoning produces them. So Nash is not "what rational agents do" but "what agents do once a social process has aligned their conjectures." Correlated equilibrium is the natural object, because Aumann (1987) showed Bayesian rationality with a common prior yields exactly correlated (not Nash) equilibrium.
- Key result stated plainly: if everyone is Bayesian-rational and shares a prior over the states that determine what each player does, then the joint distribution of play *is* a correlated equilibrium. The common prior is the choreographer in disguise.
- So-what: replace "stable because it is a Nash equilibrium" with "stable because there is a shared, trusted signal everyone conditions on." Ask what your group's signal is: a written policy, a leader's word, a dashboard, a ritual.

### Ch 9 — Reflective Reason and Equilibrium Refinements
- Core claims: The 1980s refinement program (perfect, proper, sequential, stable equilibria; Selten, Myerson, Kreps & Wilson, Kohlberg & Mertens) tried to fix multiplicity with rationality-flavored conditions. Gintis: it failed on its own terms — refinements conflict, depend on arbitrary tremble structures, and lack an epistemic justification. Multiplicity is real and its resolution is *social*, not logical.
- So-what: do not expect cleverness to pick the equilibrium. Expect history, focal points (Schelling), and explicit norms to pick it — and design the norm.

### Ch 10 — The Analytics of Human Sociality
- Core claims: The heart of the book. Social norms are choreographers implementing correlated equilibria of the underlying game. Two features are essential. (1) *Epistemic*: the norm supplies the common conjecture — each person knows what others will do because the norm says so. (2) *Motivational*: humans have an evolved, culturally sharpened "normative predisposition" to follow norms and punish violators, so instructions are followed even where the bare game rewards deviation; norms can then sustain outcomes that are equilibria only once the normative payoff term is included. Gintis links this to Durkheim and Parsons on internalization and to Lewis (1969) and Bicchieri (2006) on conventions.
- Key example: "First here has right of way" is a public signal. If each person believes others follow it and mildly prefers to comply (or fears sanction), it is self-enforcing. Traffic light, office rota, seating convention — one mechanism.
- So-what: the most useful chapter. Two-part test for any norm you want to install: does it *tell people unambiguously what to do* (epistemic), and *do people have reason to comply given that others do* (motivational)? Most failed norms fail one of the two.

### Ch 11 — The Evolution of Property Rights
- Core claims: Property extends animal territoriality: the "bourgeois" strategy (Maynard Smith 1982) — defend as incumbent, yield as intruder — is an evolutionarily stable, low-conflict convention, and a correlated equilibrium of Hawk–Dove with "who arrived first" as the signal. The endowment effect (Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler 1990) is read as the psychological correlate that makes the incumbent's defense credible.
- Key model: Hawk–Dove with a possession cue. Uncorrelated play yields costly fights; conditioning on possession yields a convention in which fights are rare.
- So-what: ownership norms (who "owns" a codebase, a channel, a decision) reduce conflict primarily by being *clear and salient*, not by being fair. Ambiguous ownership is a Hawk–Dove game without a correlating signal.

### Ch 12 — The Unification of the Behavioral Sciences
- Core claims: A programmatic essay. Five principles: gene–culture coevolution (biology, anthropology); the sociopsychological theory of norms (sociology, formalized as the choreographer); game theory (universal lexicon); the rational actor model (economics, purged of selfishness); complexity theory (societies as complex adaptive systems, needing agent-based models). Gintis argues these are mutually compatible and jointly sufficient, against sociology's over-socialized and economics' under-socialized agent.
- So-what: use the five principles as a checklist for layers your diagnosis has left out — evolved dispositions, culture, norms, incentives, and system-level dynamics nobody intends.

### Ch 13 — Summary
- Recap ending with norms as correlated equilibria and the BPC model as shared basis. Nothing new.

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. **Nash equilibrium is a social achievement, not a rational deduction.** Rationality and common knowledge of rationality yield only rationalizability; Nash needs aligned conjectures that must come from somewhere outside the players' heads.
2. **Norms are choreographers.** A norm is a public signal that tells each person what to do and thereby aligns conjectures; following it is a best response when others follow it. Aumann's correlated equilibrium with the norm as correlating device.
3. **The BPC model (beliefs, preferences, constraints) is the minimal common language.** Rational choice means consistent preferences, which can be altruistic, spiteful, fairness-driven or norm-following; "rational" is not "selfish."
4. **Common priors are culture.** The Harsanyi doctrine is not a theorem about rationality; it is an empirical claim that people share a belief-forming culture. Belief alignment is a cultural intervention.
5. **Norm-following is motivated, not just informed.** Humans have an evolved, culturally trained disposition to comply with norms and punish violators (strong reciprocity).
6. **Equilibrium multiplicity is resolved socially.** Refinements failed; history, focal points and norms select equilibria.
7. **Property is a convention with a cue.** Ownership norms resolve Hawk–Dove conflicts by conditioning on a salient signal (possession), and the endowment effect is the psychological correlate that makes them stick.
8. **Unification is a five-part program** (gene–culture coevolution, norms, game theory, rational actor, complexity), offered as a framework rather than a finished theory.

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **BPC model** — beliefs, preferences, constraints; the rational actor model stripped of selfishness — bites when someone claims a group "isn't rational": look for the preference or belief you have mis-specified.
- **Rationalizability** — strategies that survive iterated deletion of never-best-responses — bites when you expect "smart people will converge": they may not, because rationalizability is often everything.
- **Common knowledge of rationality (CKR)** — everyone is rational, everyone knows it, everyone knows everyone knows, ad infinitum — bites in extensive-form games where an off-path move contradicts CKR.
- **Correlated equilibrium** — a distribution over joint actions, mediated by a signal, from which no one profits by deviating — bites whenever you can install a signal (schedule, rota, dashboard, referee).
- **Choreographer** — Gintis's name for the correlating device when it is a social norm — bites when a norm is vague: a vague norm is a choreographer that does not choreograph.
- **Common prior / Harsanyi doctrine** — all players' beliefs derive from one prior updated on private information — bites when sub-groups disagree persistently despite shared data.
- **Strong reciprocity** — conditional cooperation plus costly punishment, even one-shot — bites when designing sanctions: people will pay to punish, so give them a legitimate, proportionate channel.
- **Normative predisposition** — evolved readiness to internalize and follow norms — bites when you rely on incentives alone: you leave the cheapest motivational lever unused.

## Evidence strength & limits
**What is robust.** The formal results are theorems: Aumann (1974, 1987), Aumann & Brandenburger (1995), Bernheim and Pearce. The chapter 3 experimental facts — ultimatum rejections, conditional cooperation, costly punishment, cross-cultural variation — replicate well. Rejection of pure self-interest is about as secure as anything in behavioral science.

**What is contested.**
- *Strong reciprocity as an evolved trait.* Gintis (with Bowles, Boyd, Fehr) argues it evolved by gene–culture coevolution and group selection. Binmore & Shaked (2010, *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*) argue the one-shot lab evidence is consistent with subjects mis-applying heuristics tuned for repeated interaction and that learning erodes cooperation in many designs; Guala (2012, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*) argued field evidence for *costly* punishment in small-scale societies is thin — sanctioning in the wild is mostly cheap (gossip, ostracism). Background knowledge, not verified this session, but a well-known dispute.
- *Whether the "modest rational actor" is falsifiable.* Once preferences may be other-regarding, state-dependent and norm-inclusive, critics (a line associated with Binmore's and Sugden's methodological writings) ask what observation could refute the BPC model. Gintis's answer — revealed-preference axioms such as transitivity are testable and sometimes fail — is correct but weaker than the rhetoric suggests.
- *The choreographer as explanation vs. redescription.* Peter Vanderschraaf's review (*Economics and Philosophy*, 2011; existence verified, content from snippets and background) is sympathetic — he had himself modeled conventions as correlated equilibria — while pressing that the account still owes an explanation of *which* correlated equilibrium a society lands on and how the signal is learned. The Nash multiplicity gap, moved one level up.
- *The unification thesis.* Reviews by Jean-Robert Tyran (*Journal of Economics*, 2010), Juliette Rouchier (*JASSS*, 2010) and Alexander Field (*Journal of Economic History*, 2011) — existence verified via search — praise the exposition of epistemic game theory and note (Rouchier explicitly) that Gintis claims complementarity rather than full coverage. The recurring charge is that the "unification" is *programmatic*: chapter 12 lists five compatible principles and asserts they suffice, but derives no result that sociology or anthropology lacked. Nobody has to change a model because of chapter 12.
- *Binmore.* I could not locate a Binmore review of this book. Binmore's own work (*Natural Justice*, 2005; "Do conventions need to be common knowledge?", *Topoi*, 2008) also treats conventions as equilibria selected by social process and is skeptical of common-knowledge assumptions — so Binmore and Gintis are closer on the *epistemic* critique than on *preferences*; their real fight is strong reciprocity and group selection. Don Ross (2006, *Politics, Philosophy & Economics*) defended Binmore's theory of justice against Gintis's criticisms (verified via snippet). Treat "Binmore panned The Bounds of Reason" as unverified.
- *Sugden.* No Sugden review of this book was found (he reviewed *Moral Sentiments and Material Interests* in Economica; content not verified). His standing critique of the social-preferences program — that it stuffs sociality into individual utility functions and misses team reasoning and mutual advantage — applies to chapter 3, but attribute it to his general position.
- *Complexity theory* (principle five) is asserted, not used; the book contains no agent-based models.

**What Gintis argues vs. shows.** He *shows* (theorems) that Nash cannot be grounded in rationality alone and that correlated equilibrium is what Bayesian rationality plus a common prior gives you. He *argues* that norms are the empirical correlating device, that norm-following is an evolved disposition, and that this closes the economics–sociology gap. The first two are plausible and partly supported elsewhere on this list; the third is a promissory note.

## Design implications for cooperation in real groups
- **Name the signal.** For any coordination problem, specify the public cue that tells each person which equilibrium is in play (rota, calendar, written rule, dashboard). Ambiguity is the failure mode, not bad incentives. [E — correlated-equilibrium logic plus Schelling/Lewis evidence]
- **Run the two-part norm test.** (a) Epistemic: can every member state what the norm requires of *them* and of *others*? (b) Motivational: given others comply, does anyone gain by deviating, and if so, is there a cheap sanction? [H — follows from the model; whether it predicts norm survival in your group is to be tested]
- **Stop trying to reason people into equilibrium.** Meetings that derive the "obviously rational" plan reproduce the refinement program's failure. Pick a focal option, announce it, make it the default. [E — refinement literature; Camerer's coordination evidence]
- **Align priors culturally, not by more data.** Persistent disagreement across sub-teams on the same data indicates different priors; fix with shared training, metric definitions and narrative, not more dashboards. [H]
- **Make ownership salient.** Assign clear owners even when the assignment is somewhat arbitrary; ambiguity produces Hawk–Dove fights. [E — bourgeois-strategy results; organizational evidence weaker]
- **Budget for legitimate punishment.** Members will sanction free riders at a cost to themselves; give them a low-cost, proportionate channel (Ostrom's graduated sanctions) so punishment does not go underground. [E — lab; field evidence favors cheap sanctions, so prefer gossip-grade channels over formal penalties]
- **Model the group as neither selfish nor saintly.** Use BPC: what do members believe about each other, what do they value (including status and fairness), what constrains them? Most "incentive" fixes change constraints while beliefs about what others will do stay the same. [E]
- **Prefer the norm to the incentive when both are available.** Norms exploit an existing disposition and are cheaper to maintain; incentives can crowd it out. [H — crowding-out is real but context-dependent]
- **Do not expect the unification to tell you which layer matters.** The framework is a checklist, not a decision procedure; ordering of interventions comes from the other books and your own diagnostic. [V]

## Retrieval practice

1. **Recall.** What does common knowledge of rationality license as a solution concept, according to Gintis, and why is that not Nash equilibrium?
<details>Rationalizability. Nash additionally requires that players' conjectures coincide (Aumann & Brandenburger 1995), which individual rationality cannot generate.</details>

2. **Explain-why.** Why is a traffic light a better analogy for a social norm than a rule book?
<details>A traffic light is a correlating device: it sends each driver a private-but-related instruction, and obeying is a best response given that others obey. A rule book without a shared signal or shared belief that others follow it is not self-enforcing.</details>

3. **Apply.** A remote team has two viable release cadences; half the engineers assume weekly, half assume fortnightly, and merges keep colliding. Diagnose in Gintis's terms and propose a fix.
<details>Two equilibria, no choreographer, misaligned conjectures. Fix: a public signal (a published, pinned cadence with a visible calendar) so conforming is each engineer's best response. The epistemic part is what is missing; incentives are unnecessary.</details>

4. **Spot the misconception.** "Gintis shows that people are irrational, so game theory is useless for real groups."
<details>Wrong on both counts. Gintis defends rational choice (as preference consistency) and game theory (as lexicon); his target is the claim that rationality alone yields Nash play. He wants game theory embedded in a theory of norms, not discarded.</details>

5. **Recall.** State the two components a norm must have to function as a choreographer.
<details>Epistemic (it tells each player what to do and what others will do, aligning conjectures) and motivational (players have a disposition, or face sanctions, so that compliance is a best response given others' compliance).</details>

6. **Explain-why.** Why does Gintis say the common-prior assumption is really a claim about culture?
<details>Bayesian rationality says nothing about where priors come from; a shared prior can only arise from a shared belief-forming process, which in humans is cultural transmission.</details>

7. **Apply.** Two product squads look at identical metrics and reach opposite conclusions week after week. Which Gintis chapter is relevant and what intervention does it suggest?
<details>Chapter 7. Persistent disagreement on shared data signals different priors. Intervention: cultural alignment (shared definitions, joint training), not more data.</details>

8. **Spot the misconception.** "Because ownership norms reduce conflict, the fairest allocation of ownership will reduce conflict the most."
<details>Conflict falls because the cue is salient and mutually recognized, not because it is fair; a fair but ambiguous allocation can produce more fights than a clear arbitrary one. Fairness matters for other reasons (legitimacy, willingness to sanction), which Ostrom and Bicchieri supply.</details>

9. **Explain-why.** What is the main charge that the unification is "programmatic rather than achieved"?
<details>Chapter 12 lists five compatible principles and asserts they suffice, but derives no new result in any discipline; it re-describes existing findings in a common vocabulary. Compatibility is not a unified theory that predicts what the parts could not.</details>

10. **Apply.** You have installed a clear contribution norm on a visible board; conjectures are aligned; but compliance is still low. Which part of Gintis's account has failed and which other reading helps?
<details>The motivational part: some members still profit by deviating and no sanction bites. Ostrom (graduated sanctions, participant monitoring) and Bicchieri (normative expectations: do people believe others *expect* them to comply?) supply the repair; Camerer warns cooperation decays without feedback.</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- **"Gintis rejects rational choice."** He rejects selfishness and the claim that rationality yields Nash; he keeps consistent-preference rationality as the core.
- **"Correlated equilibrium replaces Nash because it fits data better."** The argument is conceptual: correlated equilibrium is what Bayesian rationality plus a common prior gives you (Aumann 1987); Nash needs stronger, socially supplied conditions. The book runs no horse race on data.
- **"The choreographer is a person."** It is any correlating signal: a norm, a convention, a clock, a possession cue. The concept is about the signal, not the authority.
- **"The book proves that norms are internalized by evolution."** It argues a normative predisposition evolved by gene–culture coevolution; that claim is contested (Binmore & Shaked; Guala) and the book does not itself provide the evolutionary models.
- **"The unification is done."** It is a framework and a checklist. Reviewers consistently note that it is an agenda.

## Critical assessment: which claims hold up against the rest of the reading list
The purpose of this file in the course is to assess an attempted synthesis. Claim by claim:

**Claim 1 — Rationality plus common knowledge of rationality does not yield Nash equilibrium; Nash rests on socially supplied alignment of conjectures.**
*Verdict: holds up; the book's most durable contribution.* Theorem-backed (Aumann & Brandenburger 1995), and every empirical source on the list supports the corollary that people do not reason their way to equilibrium: Camerer's coordination experiments show slow, path-dependent convergence; Schelling's focal points and Skyrms's dynamics do the selecting. For the design brief, this is the license to stop trying to *argue* a group into a coordinated plan.

**Claim 2 — Social norms are choreographers implementing correlated equilibria.**
*Verdict: holds up as a model, with a caveat about what it leaves unexplained.* Bicchieri's account is formally compatible: a norm transforms a game via conditional preferences and expectations, and behavior is an equilibrium of the transformed game conditioned on a shared cue; Lewis-style conventions in Skyrms are the same idea. The choreographer model is *weaker* than Bicchieri's on diagnosis (she separates empirical from normative expectations and tells you which to measure when a norm fails; Gintis treats compliance as a lump), weaker than Ostrom's on authorship (her commons show choreographers written, monitored and revised by the players themselves), and weaker than Skyrms's on origins (he shows *how* correlation emerges without anyone designing it). True, but each Order-level book is more useful for diagnosis than the formalization.

**Claim 3 — The BPC model (consistent preferences, possibly other-regarding) is the right common core, and behavioral anomalies are mostly mis-specified preferences or beliefs, not irrationality.**
*Verdict: partially holds up; overstated.* Camerer's social-preference evidence fits adding fairness and reciprocity terms to utility, and that move has been productive. But Gilovich et al. (construal, framing, motivated reasoning) describe *beliefs* that shift with presentation and mood, not tidily captured as state-dependent preferences; Camerer's learning chapter shows adaptive, path-dependent behavior rather than fixed-point rationality; Henrich shows preferences are culturally acquired and vary across societies, which BPC accommodates as data but does not predict. BPC is a serviceable *bookkeeping* language, and the falsifiability worry is real: once anything can be a preference, the model constrains little without auxiliary theory — which is what the rest of this reading list is.

**Claim 4 — Common priors are culture; belief alignment is a cultural process.**
*Verdict: holds up, well supported by Henrich and Laland.* Conformist and prestige-biased learning are exactly how priors converge within groups and diverge between them; the cross-cultural game results Gintis co-authored show "priors" about fair offers are socially shared. Easley & Kleinberg add the qualifier that convergence follows network structure, so a group with weak cross-ties can host two priors. The design implication (align culture, not data) is sound.

**Claim 5 — Norm-following is an evolved disposition (strong reciprocity), and this supplies the motivational half of the choreographer.**
*Verdict: contested; the evidence supports a weaker version.* Lab evidence for conditional cooperation and willingness to punish is robust. The evolutionary claim is disputed by Binmore & Shaked and by Guala's field review, which finds real-world sanctioning mostly cheap (gossip, withdrawal, reputation). Henrich supports cultural group selection and a norm psychology, so the *direction* fits the Origins level, but Ostrom's cases — the strongest field evidence — show graduated, low-cost, participant-run sanctioning rather than heroic costly punishment. Design lesson: rely on the cheap version.

**Claim 6 — Equilibrium refinements failed; selection is social.**
*Verdict: holds up.* The refinement program is widely regarded as having run out of road; Camerer's and Schelling's evidence is that history and salience select. No book on the list dissents.

**Claim 7 — Property rights are conventions with a salient cue (bourgeois strategy), with the endowment effect as psychological support.**
*Verdict: holds up as a model; the psychological link is shakier than presented.* Hawk–Dove-with-possession is standard evolutionary game theory, consistent with Skyrms. The endowment effect is robust in many designs but its magnitude and interpretation are contested, and treating it as the evolved psychology of property is a hypothesis. Ostrom's cases show stable commons regimes are *rule systems* with monitoring, not possession cues alone; the cue-only story is too thin for institutional design.

**Claim 8 — The five principles unify economics, psychology, sociology, anthropology and biology.**
*Verdict: does not hold up as stated; holds up as a reading guide.* The charge that the unification is programmatic is fair. Every empirical achievement cited belongs to one of the other books on this list, done in that book's own idiom. Chapter 12 shows these are *compatible* — you are not forced to choose between "people follow norms" and "people respond to incentives" — but compatibility is not unification. No result in the book is one a sociologist or anthropologist could not have obtained without it, and the complexity principle is not used at all. The most generous reading is Rouchier's: game theory as a *complement* that keeps social theory honest about incentives and expectations, and social theory as supplier of the priors, preferences and signals game theory needs. On that reading the book is right and modest — but then the title's "unification" is marketing.

**Net assessment for the course.** Carry out two things: (1) the epistemic critique of Nash and the choreographer idea, which sharpen every Order-level book into one design question — *what is the signal, and why does each person comply given the others do?* — and (2) the discipline of writing down beliefs, preferences and constraints before proposing an intervention. Do not carry out the claim that the behavioral sciences have been unified, or that norm-following is settled evolutionary fact. The synthesis is a map of how the other ten books fit together, not a substitute for any of them.
