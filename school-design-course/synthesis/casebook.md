# Founder's casebook: 24 dilemmas for the school-design course
Practice layer for the anabolic phase (see `synthesis/learning-design.md` section 4d). Each case is a
realistic decision a founder or head of school will face. The case gives you concrete numbers and
people, names the tension it exercises, and gives a model answer that cites the knowledge base by
slug and tags each decision **[E]** evidence, **[H]** hypothesis to test, or **[V]** value
commitment.

The casebook extends the eighteen seed cases in 4d. Some seeds are kept almost as written (the
vendor, the struggling PF lesson, the misconception that got stronger, the dashboard, the evolution
unit, the curiosity audit). Some are merged or sharpened (the gradebook and the no-grades revolt;
the knowledge-sequence fight became a coherence audit). New cases cover hiring, discipline, AI
tutoring, a failed PDSA cycle, a middle-school motivation collapse, a TLC going through the motions,
students who parrot answers, and a hook that ate the lesson.

## How to use a case

1. Read only the scenario and the question. Set a 10-minute timer.
2. Write your answer (150-250 words). It must do four things: **name the tension** (id and name),
   **apply its decision rule**, **state the switch signal** that would change your mind, and **tag
   each decision** [E]/[H]/[V].
3. Read the considerations and revise if you want to. Note which consideration you missed.
4. Read the model answer and the novice error. Score yourself 0-2 on each of: tension named, rule
   applied, switch signal stated, tagging correct, concrete next step. 8 or more out of 10 is a
   strong answer.
5. If you scored below 6, open the "Institutional design implications" section of the first two
   sources listed and redo the case in two days (spacing).

The model answers are defensible, not uniquely correct. A different answer that names the tension,
respects the evidence and tags honestly can score as well. The novice error is there because most
of the learning is in seeing the plausible wrong move clearly.

## k01. The polished demo lesson

**Tension:** t06 Selecting teachers vs. developing them  
**Sources:** `10-creating-the-schools-our-children-need`, `07-embedding-formative-assessment`, `00-foundational-papers`, `05-10-to-25`

### Scenario

You have one grade-6 math position left for August and must send an offer by Friday. Two finalists remain. Marcus has eleven years of experience and a single-year "highly effective" value-added rating from his last district. His demo lesson was the best your panel has seen: crisp slides, lively pair talk, all 28 borrowed students visibly busy. In the debrief your math lead, Tomas, pointed out that Marcus never checked whether students could solve a problem on their own. Marcus smiled and said, "Respectfully, I've been doing this a long time," and moved on. Priya has two years of experience and an engineering degree. Her demo was flat: she over-explained, the pace dragged, and two students drifted. But 22 of 26 exit tickets were correct. In her debrief she asked for the single most important change, came back the next morning to re-teach a ten-minute segment with it, and the second attempt was visibly tighter. Your founding board member Elena, a tech recruiter, texts you: "Always hire the A player. Don't overthink it." Tomas leans toward Priya but says he could live with either.

### Question

Whom do you hire, what does the choice reveal about your theory of teacher quality, and which two authors are arguing in your head?

### Considerations

- How well do pre-hire signals (experience, one-year value-added, one demo lesson) predict effectiveness?
- What does visible engagement tell you about learning, compared with the exit tickets?
- Your school's plan for teacher quality is a three-year development system built on TLCs. Which hiring signal does that system depend on?
- Is one debrief exchange enough to judge Marcus, and could you give both candidates the same second cycle before Friday?
- What are the real risks of hiring Priya, and how would you support her?

### Model answer

Hire Priya, unless Marcus passes a fair second test before Friday. Offer him the same deal: one piece of feedback, a ten-minute re-teach tomorrow. If he takes the feedback on board, reconsider. If he declines, you have your answer.

The reasoning starts with what the signals are worth. Credentials, years of experience and a single year of value-added predict later effectiveness only weakly, and single observations are unreliable (`10-creating-the-schools-our-children-need`, Kane, Rockoff & Staiger; MET) [E]. The demo lesson rewarded visible activity, which is a poor proxy for learning (`00-foundational-papers`) [E]. Priya's exit tickets are thin evidence, but they are evidence about learning.

The deeper point is that your school does not rely on hiring to supply quality. It relies on development. TLCs delivered at scale produced roughly two months of extra progress in the EEF trial (`07-embedding-formative-assessment`) [E], and the mechanism is teachers trying a technique, reporting back and taking peer feedback. So the trait you most need to see at hiring is how a candidate responds to feedback. Priya showed it twice, which is the nearest thing to a work sample you have. That coachability predicts growth is still [H]. Log it in the open-hypotheses register and check it: score every hire's response to feedback at interview, then compare against their observation trajectory over year one.

Plan for Priya's weaknesses. Her pacing is a known gap, so give her the shared, worked-example-heavy curriculum to cut her planning load, and a coach for the first term [H].

To Elena: in software you can measure output cheaply and fire quickly, so "hire the A player" works. In teaching, one lesson tells you little about output, and a veteran who shuts down peer feedback can quietly undermine a TLC culture [H]. Treat Marcus's reaction as one data point, not a character verdict. That is why the second cycle matters. The staff culture you want, where getting better is the norm for everyone, is [V].

### What a novice would do wrong

Hire Marcus because the lesson looked like great teaching, reading engagement and polish as learning. Or reject him over one prickly sentence without testing it, which swaps one noisy signal for another. Either way, the novice never asks which trait their own development system needs.

## k02. The literacy adoption

**Tension:** t03 Standardization vs. community design (primary); t02 Knowledge vs. curiosity/inquiry  
**Sources:** `06-beyond-the-science-of-reading`, `10-creating-the-schools-our-children-need`, `01-how-learning-happens`, `07-embedding-formative-assessment`, `03-intellectual-lives-of-children`

### Scenario

Your K-5 has twelve teachers. Your literacy lead, Dana, proposes one knowledge-building ELA curriculum across K-5 with a separate systematic phonics track in K-2. The ELA units are sequenced (grade 1 studies the human body and early civilizations; grade 2 the War of 1812 and insects) and texts cluster around each topic. Materials cost $38,000, plus four days of training. Seven of the twelve teachers push back. Most come from a balanced-literacy district. They love their leveled classroom libraries and their weekly "skill of the week" mini-lessons (main idea, inference, author's purpose) taught on short unrelated passages. They say a common program will "kill their creativity and the kids' love of reading." Two say privately they will leave if it is imposed. A parent on your founding council forwards an op-ed arguing that "reading is a skill, not a subject." The first state reading test, in grade 3, is eighteen months away. Dana says a partial adoption is worse than none.

### Question

Do you adopt the common curriculum, let teachers choose, or compromise? Which two authors are arguing in your head?

### Considerations

- What does the evidence say about topic knowledge, decoding and strategy instruction as drivers of comprehension?
- Which layer of the literacy program is a mechanism to standardize, and which is open to teacher and community choice?
- What can you honestly promise the board about grade-3 scores in eighteen months?
- What do teachers mean by "creativity", and which part of it can survive a common spine?
- Adoption is not implementation. What support makes the curriculum actually get taught?

### Model answer

Adopt the common spine. Be explicit about what is tight and what is loose, and invest in making it work rather than in winning the argument.

The evidence is lopsided on the mechanism. Comprehension of a text depends heavily on knowing its topic (Recht & Leslie) [E]. Systematic phonics is necessary for most children [E]. Strategy instruction gives quick gains that stop growing with more practice, so weekly skill-of-the-week lessons crowd out knowledge (`06-beyond-the-science-of-reading`, `01-how-learning-happens`) [E]. Knowledge compounds only when topics are sequenced across grades. Teacher-by-teacher choice destroys that compounding (`10-creating-the-schools-our-children-need`) [E for the mechanism]. That a whole-school spine yields large gains on general reading tests is [H], and effects on distal tests often lag two to three years. Say that to the board now, not in eighteen months.

Tension t03 says: standardize the mechanisms and the knowledge sequence; codesign the rest. So state the tight layer (unit sequence, text sets, phonics scope and sequence, daily content-embedded writing) and the loose layer (how teachers run discussion, read-aloud extensions, classroom routines). Keep the classroom libraries for independent choice reading, but stop using levels to ration which content a child meets (`06`) [E]. On t02, curiosity is protected by norms such as question journals and time to follow up, not by content-free time [H]. That speaks directly to the "love of reading" worry.

Implementation is where adoptions die (`06`, SRI observations). Fund curriculum-specific coaching, not just four launch days, and have the TLCs work on hinge questions for the new units (`07-embedding-formative-assessment`) [H]. Measure robust comprehension in observation, knowledge on curriculum-embedded checks, and decoding on a screener three times a year.

If two teachers leave, you accept the cost. The shared rich curriculum is an equity commitment: every child gets the same content whatever they bring from home [V]. Name that in the register, along with who disagrees and why.

### What a novice would do wrong

Let each teacher choose, calling it professional autonomy, and lose coherence. Or impose the program by memo and treat purchase as implementation. Or layer the new curriculum on top of the old skills block, doubling the load and teaching neither well.

## k03. The no-grades revolt

**Tension:** t07 Testing as measurement vs. testing as learning (primary); t03 Standardization vs. community design  
**Sources:** `07-embedding-formative-assessment`, `01-how-learning-happens`, `05-10-to-25`, `08-extraordinary-learning-for-all`, `11-improvement-in-action`

### Scenario

Your grades 6-8 school gives comment-only feedback on all formative work. Grades appear twice a year, on summative assessments, reported as levels against standards. In October, 41 of 180 families sign a letter demanding letter grades on all assignments. Dr. Lin, a surgeon, leads it: "My daughter has no idea whether she is doing well. Without grades there's no accountability, and high schools want GPAs." Another parent, Mrs. Ortiz, says her son "stopped trying because nothing counts." Sixty parents turn up to the meeting. Your grade-7 team brings data: 74% of students revise their work after comments, against roughly 31% at the schools the teachers came from. Board chair Alan calls you afterwards: "Just add letter grades to everything. It's a PR problem, not a hill to die on." Your monthly family newsletter has never explained the feedback policy beyond one paragraph in the August handbook. Nobody has checked whether students act on the comments in every class, or only in grade 7.

### Question

What do you change, what do you keep, and how do you run the next month? Which two authors are arguing in your head?

### Considerations

- What does Butler's work say happens to comments when a grade is attached?
- Which parent concern is legitimate and unmet by the current system?
- What is the switch signal for tension t07, and has it fired?
- Which parts of assessment are open to family codesign and which are mechanisms?
- What is going on with Mrs. Ortiz's son, and how would you find out?

### Model answer

Keep the mechanism and fix the communication and reporting. The t07 switch signal has fired: parents are experiencing every check as a hidden judgment, which means the communication failed. It does not mean the mechanism failed.

The mechanism is well supported. Grades attached to comments tend to swamp them: students look at the grade and ignore the comment (Butler) [E]. About a third of feedback interventions make performance worse, mostly when feedback focuses on the self rather than the task (Kluger & DeNisi; `07-embedding-formative-assessment`) [E]. The 74% revision rate is local, uncontrolled evidence that the policy does what it is meant to [H]. Share it anyway.

Dr. Lin's underlying complaint is fair. Families deserve an accurate picture of where their child stands [V], and a twice-yearly level does not give them one. So change the reporting, not the grading of formative work. Send a short monthly progress note per subject naming the standard, the child's current distance from it, and the next step (`05-10-to-25`) [H]. Share annotated exemplars so families can see what "secure" looks like. Keep summative grades at fixed points, and say plainly that high school transcripts will carry them. For a sixth grader, the GPA concern is years away.

Use t03 here. Invite families to codesign the reporting format: frequency, layout, and conference structure (`08-extraordinary-learning-for-all`) [V/H]. Whether formative work is graded is not on the table, and you say why in one paragraph of plain language, not a literature review.

Treat Mrs. Ortiz's son as data. Find out whether his comments ask him to do anything, and whether he receives them as respect or as judgment. Some classes may be writing comments with no required action, which is a known lethal mutation [H].

Run it as a PDSA. Predict that the share of parents agreeing "I know how my child is doing" rises from its current level to at least 70% within two reporting cycles. Keep tracking revision rates as the balancing measure (`11-improvement-in-action`).

### What a novice would do wrong

Cave and grade everything, destroying the mechanism to fix a communication problem. Or defend the policy with citations, treating parents as a misconception to correct, and miss that the reporting gap is real.

## k04. The project-based evangelist and the data

**Tension:** t01 Explicit instruction vs. productive failure (primary); t05 Mastery vs. identity and creativity  
**Sources:** `00-foundational-papers`, `01-how-learning-happens`, `04-productive-failure`, `09-in-search-of-deeper-learning`, `05-10-to-25`

### Scenario

Ms. Okafor teaches grade-8 science and came to you from a well-known project-based network. Her six-week unit, "Design a water filter for our town," has teams researching, prototyping and pitching to a city engineer. Students adore it: her classes score 4.6 out of 5 on the student-experience survey, the highest in the school. The other grade-8 teacher, Mr. Hale, taught the same chemistry (mixtures, solubility, the particle model) through three weeks of explicit instruction and worked examples, then a one-week build. On the common unit test Ms. Okafor's two sections averaged 52% and Mr. Hale's two averaged 71%. On a delayed quiz six weeks later the numbers were 41% and 63%. Ms. Okafor says the test measures the wrong things: "They learned to collaborate and think like engineers." She wants to extend the approach to grade 7 next term and has already drafted two units. Several of her parents have emailed to praise her. Mr. Hale, quieter, says he would like his students to do more real projects but doesn't know how to fit them in.

### Question

What do you decide about grade 7, and what do you ask of each teacher? Which two authors are arguing in your head?

### Considerations

- How much can you conclude from two teachers and four sections?
- Which of Kapur's four quadrants does Ms. Okafor's unit fall into?
- Her claimed outcomes (collaboration, engineering thinking) are unmeasured. What is the honest status of that claim?
- What does the t05 rule say about authentic products in the core?
- How do you hold Ms. Okafor to a high standard without losing her?

### Model answer

Don't extend the unit to grade 7 yet, and don't ban projects. Ask both teachers to co-design one unit that keeps the product and builds in the knowledge.

Be careful about what the data show. Four sections and two teachers confound method with teacher and class, so on its own the comparison is [H]. But the direction matches a strong body of evidence that minimally guided work leaves novices without the knowledge (`00-foundational-papers`, Kirschner, Sweller & Clark; `01-how-learning-happens`) [E]. The delayed-quiz gap matters most, because it measures learning rather than performance. In Kapur's terms this unit looks like unproductive success: high engagement, weak durable knowledge (`04-productive-failure`) [E for the framework]. The survey score doesn't change that: student preference is a poor proxy for learning [E].

Ms. Okafor's claim about collaboration and engineering thinking may be true, but it is unmeasured [H]. If the school values those outcomes [V], define them and assess them with a rubric, so they stop working as an unfalsifiable shield.

Tension t05 says: mastery first in the core, with one authentic product per course per term. So the water filter stays, and it becomes the unit's culminating product. Require a written guidance plan: what is taught explicitly, when, with which worked examples, and where hinge questions check the particle model before building starts (`00`) [H for the review process]. If she wants students to struggle productively, one PF-style introduction to solubility is defensible, provided a consolidation lesson follows (`04`) [E].

Make the teachers co-designers: her project craft, his explicit sequence. Write a prediction: the delayed quiz reaches 65% or higher in both sections, while the experience survey stays at 4.3 or above. Decide about grade 7 when the result is in.

Talk to Ms. Okafor with the mentor mindset (`05-10-to-25`): hold a high bar, be transparent about why, and say you believe she can meet it. Her gift for engagement is real, and you want it attached to learning (`09-in-search-of-deeper-learning`) [V].

### What a novice would do wrong

Side with the data and forbid projects, losing a strong teacher and the identity dimension of the core. Or side with the enthusiasm and scale it. Or treat a two-teacher comparison as proof in either direction.

## k05. The board wants gains in year one

**Tension:** t04 Accountability vs. improvement  
**Sources:** `11-improvement-in-action`, `10-creating-the-schools-our-children-need`, `07-embedding-formative-assessment`, `06-beyond-the-science-of-reading`

### Scenario

Your charter school opens with 320 students in K-8. 58% qualify for free lunch, and entering students in grades 3-8 average around the 35th percentile on last year's state test. At the August board meeting, chair Robert, a private-equity partner, proposes a target: raise state-test proficiency from 31% to 45% by spring. He wants a monthly dashboard showing progress against the target and a $60,000 teacher bonus pool paid out by grade-level gains. Two other board members nod. One asks why you need "all this TLC stuff" if you hire well. Your plan rests on a knowledge-rich curriculum, formative assessment built through TLCs, and systematic phonics with a screening protocol. The sources suggest those produce real but modest gains, often around 0.1 SD over two years at whole-school scale, and knowledge curricula may lag on distal tests. Your authorizer reviews the charter in year three. Robert ends the meeting by saying: "What gets measured gets managed." You have three weeks to bring back a response.

### Question

What do you bring back to the board in three weeks? Which two authors are arguing in your head?

### Considerations

- How large a gain is a 14-point proficiency jump in one year, next to realistic effect sizes?
- What happens to indicators when they carry high stakes?
- What does the evidence say about teacher merit pay?
- Which measures could the board legitimately watch monthly?
- What is the t04 switch signal, and how would you use it over two years?

### Model answer

Bring back two measurement systems that stay separate, a realistic accountability target, and a year-one improvement aim the board can watch. Decline the bonus pool.

Start with honest numbers. A whole-school formative-assessment program realistically yields about 0.1 SD over two years (`07-embedding-formative-assessment`, EEF) [E]. Knowledge-rich curricula show consistent but often small short-run effects on distal reading tests, with a lag of two to three years (`06-beyond-the-science-of-reading`) [H]. A 14-point jump in proficiency in year one would be extraordinary. Chasing it would push the school toward test preparation and narrowing, which raises scores in the short term without raising learning [H]. Robert's slogan is half right: what gets measured gets managed, and that is the risk. High-stakes indicators get corrupted (Campbell's law; `11-improvement-in-action`) [E].

Propose this. Accountability measures (state test, authorizer metrics) are reported to the board twice a year with a two-year target set against comparable schools. Improvement measures (decoding screener pass rates in K-2, cumulative quiz accuracy, attendance, on-track indicators by subgroup) go to the board monthly, in aggregate, as leading indicators. They are never used to evaluate or pay teachers (tension t04 rule) [E/V]. Give a precise year-one aim, for example: raise the share of K-2 students at the decoding benchmark from the fall baseline to 75% by May, with the gap between subgroups halved.

On the bonus pool: randomized US merit-pay trials have mostly found null effects (`10-creating-the-schools-our-children-need`, POINT; Fryer) [E]. Grade-level payouts also turn practical measures into stakes. Offer to put the money into tutoring for students below benchmark instead. On "why TLCs if we hire well": pre-hire signals predict effectiveness weakly (`10`) [E].

Give the board a switch signal it can hold you to. If the practical measures improve for two years and state results do not, your drivers are wrong and you will change them. If the reverse happens, someone is gaming the measures (`11`). Respecting the board's accountability to the authorizer is a [V] you should say out loud.

### What a novice would do wrong

Accept the target and quietly pivot to test prep. Or refuse any numeric targets and look unaccountable. Or send the board teacher-level practical measures, which turns the improvement system into surveillance within a term.

## k06. The chair in the classroom

**Tension:** t10 Adolescent autonomy vs. adult direction  
**Sources:** `05-10-to-25`, `12-why-we-remember`, `11-improvement-in-action`

### Scenario

Jamal, 13, is in grade 8. In a science lesson, Mr. Brandt told him in front of the class to put his phone away "for the third time, like I said, you're not special." Jamal said "get out of my face," stood up and pushed his chair over. No one was hurt. Mr. Brandt wants a three-day suspension: "If he's back tomorrow, I've lost that room." Jamal's advisor, Ms. Reyes, tells you he has been sleeping at his aunt's for two weeks because of trouble at home. Your handbook says physical aggression carries "up to a three-day suspension." Two other teachers are watching how you handle it. One of them says in the staff room, "If nothing happens, the kids run the building." Jamal's mother is coming in at 3 p.m. It is now 11:40, and you haven't spoken to Jamal. Your school's suspension rate so far this year is low, but the four suspensions to date have all been boys of color, and one board member has already asked about it.

### Question

What do you do between now and 3 p.m., and over the next two weeks? Which two authors are arguing in your head?

### Considerations

- What would the enforcer, protector and mentor responses each look like?
- How reliable will everyone's memory of the incident be by 3 p.m.?
- What does the evidence on empathic discipline show, and how strong is it?
- What does Mr. Brandt need, and what does he need to hear?
- What system-level signal is sitting in the suspension data?

### Model answer

Hold the standard and give support. That means a real consequence that doesn't humiliate Jamal, a repair with Mr. Brandt, and a separate coaching conversation with Mr. Brandt.

First, before 3 p.m., get short written accounts from Mr. Brandt, Jamal and two student witnesses. Memory is reconstructive and drifts with retelling, so decisions should rest on contemporaneous records collected with neutral questions (`12-why-we-remember`) [E].

With Jamal, open with sincere questions rather than a verdict. Explain the purpose of the rule and state the standard: pushing furniture in a classroom is not acceptable, whatever came before it. Adolescents are acutely sensitive to status and respect. Messages that treat them as controlled or incompetent provoke resistance, while transparency about intent reduces it (`05-10-to-25`) [E for the broad pattern]. Empathic, relationship-preserving discipline reduced suspensions in a field experiment (Okonofua et al. 2016) [E, one well-designed study; H for your version]. A proportionate consequence might be a day of in-school reflection, the phone kept at the office for two weeks, and a facilitated repair conversation with Mr. Brandt before he returns to that class [H]. Suspend only if there is an ongoing safety risk. Tell his mother the same thing you tell Jamal, and ask what support he needs at home. Connect him with Ms. Reyes for a check-in plan.

Mr. Brandt's worry about authority is real, so back him visibly on the standard. Separately, and privately, coach him: "you're not special" in front of peers lowered Jamal's status and helped escalate things. The mentor stance applies to adults too: a high bar for how he speaks to students, and support in finding other phrases [V/H]. Check the phone policy is being applied the same way in every class (`12`, divided attention) [E].

Then look at the system. All four suspensions have been boys of color. Treat that as variation to investigate, not a coincidence. Run a blameless review of the referral process by subgroup (`11-improvement-in-action`) [V/H]. The t10 switch signals apply: if compliance rises while effort quality falls, you have drifted to enforcer.

### What a novice would do wrong

Suspend for three days to back the teacher (enforcer). Or waive any consequence because of the home situation and undercut Mr. Brandt (protector). Or decide on remembered accounts and never look at the pattern in the data.

## k07. The vendor's 0.6

**Tension:** t09 Structural reform vs. instructional reform (primary); t04 Accountability vs. improvement  
**Sources:** `10-creating-the-schools-our-children-need`, `01-how-learning-happens`, `11-improvement-in-action`, `07-embedding-formative-assessment`

### Scenario

A vendor pitches "MathLeap," an adaptive practice platform for grades 3-8. The headline slide reads "Effect size 0.6: equivalent to 1.5 extra years of learning." Their white paper describes 212 students in four suburban schools over ten weeks. The outcome was the platform's own end-of-module assessment, and the only comparison was to "national norms." The platform costs $45 per student per year, about $14,400 for your 320 students. The vendor recommends three 30-minute sessions a week, replacing independent practice. Your math lead, Keisha, is excited. She says the adaptive engine "personalizes to each child." Teachers already have their two hours a month of TLC time committed to hinge questions. A parent who works in ed-tech has offered to "connect you with the founders." The vendor is offering a 40% discount if you sign by the end of the month, and a free dashboard for the board. Keisha notes the current practice routine has no built-in spacing: homework sets only cover the current week's topic.

### Question

Do you buy, pilot, or pass, and what do you need to know first? Which two authors are arguing in your head?

### Considerations

- How much should the outcome measure, the sample and the missing control group discount a 0.6?
- What instructional mechanism would the platform have to enable to help?
- What does it displace, in minutes and in teacher attention?

The pattern matches Kapur's own boundary conditions. The consolidation phase, where student methods are compared with the canonical one, is the active ingredient. High-fidelity implementations produce the benefit (g roughly 0.37-0.58 on conceptual and transfer outcomes), and low-fidelity ones show little or none (`04-productive-failure`, Sinha & Kapur 2021) [E]. Nine of fourteen lessons skipped the comparison, and the average consolidation was a third of plan. The 54% measures that mutation. Luis's conclusion tests the wrong thing, though his instinct to protect the default is sound. Explicit instruction is the school default for good reasons (`00-foundational-papers`; `01-how-learning-happens`) [E].

The t01 switch signal says: if observation shows weak consolidation, revert to explicit for that unit. Apply it, but narrowly. Ayesha's 71% is two lessons and one teacher, so it is [H], but it shows the design can work in your context.

For next term, choose the two concepts that best meet all four conditions: a conceptual goal, relevant prior knowledge, multiple plausible approaches, and a scripted consolidation. Then make consolidation structural rather than a matter of willpower:
- split each PF lesson across two periods, generation in one and the full consolidation in the next, so the bell can't eat it
- timebox generation at 20 minutes, visibly
- build a task bank with anticipated student solutions and a consolidation script, starting with Ayesha's (`04`) [H]
- run the remaining two concepts explicitly

Aim PD at the hard teacher moves: monitoring without rescuing, choosing which solutions to display, and running the compare-and-contrast (`04`) [H]. Hinge questions at the end of consolidation show whether the canonical idea landed (`07-embedding-formative-assessment`).

Run it as a PDSA (`11-improvement-in-action`). Predict that PF classes reach 65% or higher on the delayed transfer quiz, with observed consolidation of at least 20 minutes in 90% of lessons. Evaluate on delayed and transfer items, never on in-lesson success (`04`) [E]. Judging methods by durable learning is itself a [V].

### What a novice would do wrong

Accept "PF doesn't work" on evidence from a version that left out its active ingredient. Or insist teachers carry on unchanged because "research says it works." Either way, no one asks what was actually implemented.

## k17. The misconception that got stronger

**Tension:** t08 Confront misconceptions vs. avoid them  
**Sources:** `02-scienceblind`, `13-learning-scientific-concepts`, `12-why-we-remember`, `04-productive-failure`, `07-embedding-formative-assessment`

### Scenario

Noor teaches grade-8 physics. She designed a "confront the misconception" lesson on forces. She presented the claim "a moving object needs a force to keep it moving" and had groups spend 20 minutes building the strongest case for it, drawing on everyday experience: pushed carts stop, thrown balls fall. The class made a large poster of the best arguments. In the last ten minutes she explained Newton's first law. A hinge question before the unit found 62% of students choosing the "needs a force to keep moving" distractor. The day after the lesson, 68% chose it; one week later, 71%. The poster is still on the wall; students point to it when they argue. Noor is bewildered. She had read that misconceptions must be confronted, and her students were more engaged than she had ever seen them. A colleague says the lesson proves you should "never show students wrong ideas, just teach the right one." Noor has two more force lessons before the unit test.

### Question

What went wrong, and what should the next two lessons look like? Which two authors are arguing in your head?

### Considerations

- What did students actually rehearse for twenty minutes?
- What does the t08 rule require, and which part did the lesson violate?
- Is the colleague's conclusion (never show wrong ideas) justified?
- What do the intuitive-theory and memory literatures say about coexistence and retrieval of wrong models?
- What does the poster do every day it stays up?

### Model answer

The lesson rehearsed the misconception. Twenty minutes building the case for impetus, a poster of it, ten minutes of correction: students elaborated the wrong model far more than the right one. The t08 switch signal has fired. Distractor rates rose after a confrontation lesson, so the confrontation is being remembered as the content.

The rule for t08 is: confront, but only once the correct model is intelligible, always with immediate correction, and never leave the wrong model as the last thing seen or the most rehearsed. The lesson broke all three. Memory strengthens whatever is retrieved and elaborated (`12-why-we-remember`) [E], and the poster is a daily spaced retrieval cue for impetus. Anomalous data alone often fails to change minds, because students explain it away (`02-scienceblind`) [E]. And intuitive and scientific ideas coexist into adulthood, so the scientific one has to be built strong enough to win (`13-learning-scientific-concepts`) [E].

The colleague draws the wrong lesson: avoiding intuitions leaves them intact. The problem was sequence and proportion.

For the next two lessons:
- Take the poster down today. Replace it with a two-column contrast: "what it feels like" and "what is happening," where the second column names friction as a force.
- Open with a quick prediction on a puck sliding on ice, then on a space probe, and have students commit in writing (`04-productive-failure`, generation surfaces prior ideas) [E].
- Teach the mechanism: friction and air resistance are forces, and removing them removes the slowing. Use bridging analogies from rough to smooth surfaces [E, moderate].
- Use a short refutation text that states the intuition, says why it is tempting, and explains why it fails [E, moderate].
- Have each student write why their earlier prediction made sense and what corrects it. Confident errors followed by correction are remembered well (`12`, hypercorrection) [E].
- End every lesson on the scientific model; being wrong in public must stay safe [V].

Re-run the hinge question after two days and again after two weeks (`07-embedding-formative-assessment`) [H]. Schedule a revisit in grade 9, because reversion under load is normal.

### What a novice would do wrong

Conclude from the colleague's comment that misconceptions should never be named. Or give students even more debate time "because they were so engaged." Or leave the poster up because it looks like good student work.

### Scenario

At your request, upper-school principal Denise set up practical measures for the improvement system: weekly exit-ticket accuracy by class, the percentage of students revising work after feedback, and daily attendance. For one term, teachers entered data carefully and the weekly improvement huddles were lively; people compared routines and borrowed ideas. In January Denise began showing a ranked table of classes at staff meetings. In mid-year evaluations she wrote that two teachers were "bottom quartile on revision rate." Within three weeks the dashboard revision rate jumped from 48% to 81%. Your spot-check of 30 "revised" pieces finds 19 with one-word edits. Two teachers have stopped submitting exit-ticket data, citing "tech issues." The huddles are now quiet and short. Denise is pleased with the jump and has asked you to present it to the board as a success. She believes she is "using data to drive instruction," as her previous district trained her to.

### Question

What do you do this week, and how do you prevent this from happening again? Which two authors are arguing in your head?

### Considerations

- What does the jump from 48% to 81% actually show?
- What rule does tension t04 set for improvement measures?
- What do you owe the two teachers named in evaluations?
- How do you correct Denise without making her the villain?
- What can you still trust in the January-onward data?

### Model answer

The jump is not improvement. It is the measure being gamed. Stop the ranking now, withdraw the evaluative use, and rebuild the conditions that made the data honest, while treating Denise as a capable leader who used the wrong tool.

This is Campbell's law on schedule: once an indicator carries stakes, people manage the indicator rather than the thing it measures (`11-improvement-in-action`) [E]. The spot-check proves it, and the missing submissions and quiet huddles are the other symptoms. The t04 rule is explicit: improvement measures are never used in evaluation. Two measurement systems have to stay separate, physically and culturally.

This week:
- Tell staff plainly that the ranking and the evaluative comments were a mistake, both end today, and the two evaluation references will be removed. Fairness requires it: a single practical measure over a few weeks is not a valid basis for a consequential judgment (`10-creating-the-schools-our-children-need`) [E; V for the fairness commitment]. Say "we" made the mistake: you built the system and didn't write the rule down.
- Flag January-onward revision data as unreliable, and don't present the jump to the board.
- Tighten the measure itself: a revision counts only if it addresses the comment, judged on a quick sample [H].

With Denise, be direct and respectful. She wants to act on data, which is right. Give her the right move. In improvement work, variation is for learning: ask the teacher whose revision rate is highest to show the routine in a huddle, rather than ranking the lowest (`11`) [E for the principle]. Her evaluation work still matters, but it runs on a separate system with multiple measures over multiple years (`10`) [E].

To prevent a repeat, write the separation into your improvement charter: which measures are improvement, who sees them at what level of aggregation, and that they never enter appraisal [V]. Add a balancing measure for trust, a two-question staff pulse on whether it's safe to report bad data, and watch it for the next term. Leaders who model surfacing failure were the common thread in Bryk's stronger cases (`11`, Menomonee Falls) [H].

### What a novice would do wrong

Present 81% to the board as a win. Or scrap practical measures altogether because "data got political." Or publicly blame Denise, which teaches everyone that surfacing problems is dangerous, the same lesson the dashboard just taught.

## k19. The maker studio nobody wants touched

Your instructional coach spent three Fridays observing. About a third of students build increasingly ambitious things with growing skill; most of them already had tinkering experience at home. About a third repeat the same simple project, such as a cardboard phone stand, for weeks. The last third mostly chat, and several can't use the basic tools safely. The coach proposes turning the studio into sequenced explicit lessons, with a skills curriculum and assessments, "to bring it in line with the default lesson." Felix says he'll quit if that happens. Do you redesign the studio, leave it alone, or something else? Which two authors are arguing in your head?

### Considerations

- What does Mehta & Fine's periphery actually look like, and is it unguided?
- Which third of the students is the studio failing, and why might it split this way?
- Where does explicit teaching belong in a creative studio, and where does it narrow exploration?
- What does the t05 rule protect, and what does protection require?
- What should you measure without turning the studio into a class?

### Model answer

Protect the studio as periphery, but don't mistake protection for leaving it undesigned. Add apprenticeship structure while keeping ideas open. Don't turn it into the default lesson.

The children being left behind are the ones the school most needs to reach, which is a [V] reason to act.

- a 15-minute skill clinic at the start of each session (soldering, cutting safely, simple circuits), with a tool-safety check-off
- a one-paragraph project proposal and a mid-point critique using a simple protocol
- mixed-age mentors, pairing the ambitious third with newer students, which also gives them status (`05-10-to-25`) [H]
- an exhibition at the end of each term for a real audience

To Felix: his instinct to protect ideas is right, and the plan keeps it. What he hasn't provided is the coaching that turns choice into craft. Present the redesign as making his studio work for all three thirds (`05`, mentor mindset) [V].

### What a novice would do wrong

Accept the coach's plan and convert the studio into a scripted class, starving the periphery. Or leave it alone because students love it, ignoring the two thirds for whom love isn't learning.

Agree to grade everything, killing the feedback mechanism for a problem it wasn't causing. Or refuse and lecture about Butler while ignoring completion and teacher workload, which are both real.

- How do you test a grade-10 project model without betting the grade on it?

### Model answer

The t05 switch signal has fired: the periphery has become the only place students say they learn, while core scores rise. The core is producing mastery without identity or creativity. Import periphery features into the core. Cut neither the core nor the periphery.

Diagnose what the periphery has. Robotics under Dr. Kim is not unguided. It has practitioner standards, design reviews, older students coaching younger ones, iteration, and a real audience. Mehta & Fine found this pattern across school types (`09-in-search-of-deeper-learning`) [H: robust as description, confounded by self-selection]. Students choose these activities, so part of the survey gap is who shows up. Yeager adds a mechanism: real responsibility and real audiences confer status, which adolescents respond to strongly (`05-10-to-25`) [E for the broad pattern].

Proposal one, cutting enrichment for universal math intervention, treats the whole cohort for a problem that affects some students. Target intervention at the students below benchmark through tutoring inside existing time [E for tutoring], and protect the periphery. Starving it is the other t05 failure signal.

Proposal two, all-project grade 10, bets a whole grade on a model the school hasn't tested. Projects without planned knowledge building leave novices behind (`00-foundational-papers`; `01-how-learning-happens`) [E]. Test-drive it instead (`08-extraordinary-learning-for-all`) [H]: one grade-10 course next year runs as a project course with a written guidance plan, knowledge checkpoints and a public exhibition, compared against the standard sections.

The core change, for every course next year: one authentic product per course per term, made for a real audience and critiqued against practitioner standards. Examples: a statistics report for the city council, a history podcast reviewed by the local museum. Ask Dr. Kim to run a short design-review workshop for core teachers [H].

Measure both sides. Keep the proficiency and AP measures, and add the "make something that matters" item and the "where do you learn" item as leading indicators. Predict: the core-class share rises from 11% to 25% within a year, with no drop in proficiency. For the board, name the value plainly: the school's definition of deep learning includes identity and creativity, not scores alone [V].

### What a novice would do wrong

Read the scores as success and dismiss the survey. Or read the survey as a mandate to rebuild grade 10 as projects. Either way, the novice misses that the answer is to move the periphery's specific features into the core.

## k24. The curiosity audit

**Tension:** t02 Knowledge vs. curiosity/inquiry  
**Sources:** `03-intellectual-lives-of-children`, `00-foundational-papers`, `06-beyond-the-science-of-reading`, `07-embedding-formative-assessment`, `11-improvement-in-action`

### Scenario

Your instructional coach, Mira, sampled 40 K-5 lessons across the fall, counting questions asked by students (not answers to teacher questions) per hour. Kindergarten averaged 9.2; grade 1, 7.5; grade 2, 4.1; grade 3, 2.0; grade 4, 1.1; grade 5, 0.6. In most lessons where a student asked something off-script, the teacher said some version of "good question, we'll get to that" and moved on; Mira found no instance of the class coming back to it. The grade 4-5 teachers say the knowledge curriculum is too packed: "we literally cannot stop for questions." Two of them propose cutting 45 minutes a day from the knowledge units for a student-chosen "Inquiry Hour." Your literacy lead, Dana, warns that this would gut the knowledge spine you built last year and hurt the students with least background knowledge most. Mira's own reading is that the numbers are about teacher moves, not curriculum. Nobody has checked whether the pacing guide leaves any slack.

### Question

How do you interpret the audit, and what do you change? Which two authors are arguing in your head?

### Considerations

- What do the question-asking studies show, and how strong are they?
- What alternative explanations for the decline exist?
- Is knowledge the enemy of curiosity, or its fuel?
- What teacher moves and structures protect student questions?
- How would you know in a term whether the change worked, and what balancing measure would you watch?

### Model answer

Treat the audit as a signal about classroom norms, not a verdict on the curriculum. Tension t02's rule says the tension is false: knowledge is the substrate, and curiosity is protected by norms, pacing slack and teacher moves. Fix those and keep the spine.

Weigh the evidence honestly. Question-asking declines from home to school and across the early grades in typical classrooms, a direction several studies share, though the samples are small (`03-intellectual-lives-of-children`, Tizard & Hughes; Engel's observations) [E for the direction; the exact rates are not population facts]. Some of the decline may be developmental: older children ask more questions in their heads or find answers elsewhere [H, a live alternative]. The strongest actionable finding is that children's curiosity rises and falls with how adults respond (`03`) [E, small experiments]. Mira's observation, that questions were deferred and never returned to, points straight at that mechanism. Curiosity also needs knowledge: you can only notice a gap in something you partly know (`00-foundational-papers`, Gruber et al.) [E]. Cutting knowledge units would starve the fuel, and hit hardest the children with the least background knowledge at home, which is Dana's point (`06-beyond-the-science-of-reading`) [E for the knowledge gap; V for the equity commitment].

What to change:
- A question parking lot in every room, with one fixed 20-minute slot a week where the class takes up the best questions [H].
- Teacher moves taught and coached in TLCs: follow-up prompts ("how could we find out?"), wait time, and modeling uncertainty (`07-embedding-formative-assessment`) [H].
- Frame each knowledge unit around a big question that students keep returning to [H].
- Check the pacing guide and build in about 10% slack for detours. If it is truly packed, cut marginal content, not whole units.
- Optionally, a weekly 60-minute idea workshop taken from time that shouldn't exist anyway, such as leftover skills practice. It is a value choice [V] with untested effects [H].

Rerun the audit next term as a leading indicator, and predict that grade 4-5 rates at least double. The balancing measure is scores on curriculum-embedded knowledge checks, which must hold steady (`11-improvement-in-action`).

### What a novice would do wrong

Take the teachers' diagnosis at face value and trade knowledge time for Inquiry Hour. Or dismiss the audit as soft data. Or not notice that the lesson data already showed the mechanism: questions deferred and never answered.
