# The tensions: where the authors disagree, what the evidence says, and how to decide

Almost none of the debates in this curriculum are settled by picking a winner. They are settled by
conditions: for whom, for what goal, at what point in expertise, with what consolidation, at what
cost in teacher time. This file states each tension with both poles steelmanned from the book
files, what the evidence actually supports, a conditional rule, the observation that should make
you change course, and the mistake founders make most often.

Sources are cited by slug. Where this file and `learning-design.md` disagreed, the book files won
and `learning-design.md` was corrected (see its reconciliation log). Evidence tags: [E] supported,
[H] hypothesis, [V] value.

The fourteen tensions:

1. Explicit instruction vs productive failure
2. Knowledge-rich curriculum vs curiosity and inquiry
3. Teacher-led instruction vs student agency and self-direction
4. Standardization and fidelity vs community co-design and local adaptation
5. Accountability measurement vs improvement measurement
6. Selecting teachers vs developing them
7. Core vs periphery
8. High standards vs support (enforcer, protector, mentor)
9. Curiosity hooks vs target learning
10. Conceptual change vs coverage
11. Confront the intuitive theory vs build on it (theory-change vs knowledge-in-pieces)
12. Testing as measurement vs testing as learning (grades vs comments)
13. Structural reform vs instructional reform
14. Name the answer in advance vs discover it locally (Wiliam's bet vs Bryk's method)

## T1. Explicit instruction vs productive failure

**Pole A: instruct first.** Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) and Sweller's cognitive load theory
(`00-foundational-papers`, `01-how-learning-happens`): novices searching a problem space spend
working memory on search rather than on schema building; the worked-example effect is among the
most replicated findings in the field; Ashman, Kalyuga & Sweller (2020) found explicit-first beat
problem-first by a wide margin on high element-interactivity material. Wexler (`06`) and Wiliam
(`10`) inherit this position for literacy and policy.

**Pole B: generate first, then instruct.** Kapur (`04-productive-failure`): a short designed
problem-solving phase before instruction activates prior knowledge, makes learners aware of what
their ideas cannot do, creates an affective state that primes them for the answer, and sets up a
consolidation that shows which features matter. Sinha & Kapur's 2021 meta-analysis (53 studies)
found g of about 0.36 on conceptual knowledge and transfer, higher with high-fidelity design.
Ranganath (`12`) supplies the memory-level rationale: errors followed by correction are learning
events.

**What the evidence says.** The two camps agree on more than the debate suggests. Both reject
unguided discovery; PF includes a full explicit-instruction phase. The real disagreement is narrow:
does a bounded generation phase improve what is learned from the instruction that follows? The
answer is conditional. PF wins on conceptual understanding and transfer, shows no difference on
procedural knowledge (g about -0.03), benefits older learners (grade 6 up) more than elementary
students, and depends on fidelity: a problem that activates relevant knowledge and admits multiple
methods, plus a consolidation that compares student solutions to the canonical one. A companion
meta-analysis found PF's advantage over other preparatory activities (contrasting cases, worked
examples before instruction) was not significant, so the safer statement is "a well-designed
preparation before instruction beats instruction-first for concepts," not "struggle beats
everything." Explicit-first wins for procedures, for learners with nothing to activate, and for very
high element-interactivity content. Many PF studies come from proponents; treat the effect as an
upper estimate for well-run implementations. [E for both mechanisms; H for the proportion of
lessons and for transfer beyond STEM concepts.]

**Rule.** IF the goal is a load-bearing concept AND students have relevant prior or intuitive
knowledge to activate AND the problem admits multiple plausible methods AND a scripted
consolidation lesson is guaranteed AND the culture makes early failure safe, THEN run a named
generate-then-consolidate sequence for the introduction of that concept, roughly one cycle per major
idea. IF the goal is procedural fluency, a foundational skill (early reading, arithmetic facts), or
the learners cannot produce any reasonable attempt, THEN explicit instruction with worked examples
first, always. IF learners are elementary-age, THEN use lighter forms (short try-it-first prompts,
contrasting cases) and expect smaller benefits. Never schedule a generation phase without its
consolidation.

**Switch signal.** Observation shows consolidation phases being rushed or skipped; delayed
conceptual and transfer items show no advantage over explicit-first classes in the same unit;
procedural fluency on practical measures falls; students in the generation phase produce one
solution or none (nothing to activate). Any of these: revert that unit to explicit-first and
redesign the task bank. The opposite signal, students who pass immediate procedural quizzes and
fail delayed transfer items (unproductive success), argues for adding a PF cycle to that concept.

**Common mistake.** Evaluating PF by in-lesson success or an immediate procedural quiz, which will
always make it look worse; or, the mirror error, letting "productive failure" become the label for
any open-ended lesson without a consolidation, which is unproductive failure with a better name.

## T2. Knowledge-rich curriculum vs curiosity and inquiry

**Pole A: knowledge first.** Wexler (`06-beyond-the-science-of-reading`), Wiliam (`10`), Kirschner
& Hendrick (`01`): comprehension is knowledge (Recht & Leslie); "skills" like finding the main idea
saturate quickly; content-light schooling widens gaps because advantaged children get knowledge at
home; a coherent sequenced spine compounds. Inquiry for novices is inefficient and favours those
who already know.

**Pole B: curiosity and children's own questions.** Engel (`03-intellectual-lives-of-children`):
children arrive as question-askers and theory-builders and school teaches them to stop; the drop
from home to school (Tizard & Hughes, about 26 questions per hour at home versus 2 at school, small
older sample) and from kindergarten to upper elementary is the central warning; adults who follow
up, admit not knowing and model curiosity keep children asking; pedagogical demonstration narrows
exploration (Bonawitz et al. 2011); intellectual life is an end in itself. Mehta & Fine (`09`) see
the same suppression in core classes.

**What the evidence says.** This is mostly a false tension once Gruber et al. (`00`) are read
properly: curiosity is an information gap, and you need knowledge to notice a gap. Knowledge is the
fuel; curiosity is the state that makes the fuel burn. The evidence for knowledge driving
comprehension is robust; the evidence that whole knowledge curricula move general standardized
reading scores is positive but often small in the short run, with plateaus reported. Engel's
question counts are small-sample and illustrative, and the causal claim that schooling rather than
development drives the decline is argued, not shown. Her Idea Workshop has no outcome evaluation.
Bonawitz's finding is real: instruction has a cost in exploration, which means you choose when to
pay it, not whether. [E for knowledge; E that question rates fall in typical classrooms; H for the
workshop; V for intellectual life as an aim.]

**Rule.** IF a decision is about what fills instructional time in K-8 core subjects, THEN a
sequenced knowledge spine wins, with content in science, history, geography and the arts from
kindergarten. IF the decision is about classroom norms, THEN protect curiosity through behaviour
rather than through content-free time: questions get followed up, a parking lot exists with a
scheduled return, teachers model not knowing, hands-on work defaults to problem-not-recipe where the
goal is exploration. IF the school wants a protected block for pursuing children's own ideas, THEN
adopt it as a [V] commitment with a [H] evaluation, sized so it never substitutes for foundational
instruction, and pre-teach the vocabulary and background so open work does not widen the gap.

**Switch signal.** Student-originated question rates per hour fall across grades in your own
sampled observations: fix norms and pacing, not the curriculum. Workshop or inquiry time shows
students without the knowledge to ask good questions (shallow, repetitive questions; the same
children dominating): the knowledge spine is too thin or too slow, or pre-teaching is missing.
Standardized reading lagging two to three years after a knowledge curriculum was expected; lagging
five years is a signal to audit implementation depth.

**Common mistake.** Treating curiosity as a scheduled special event ("wonder hour") while the rest
of the day suppresses questions; or reading Wexler as "more facts" and building a fact-list
curriculum that ignores the conceptual-change work `02` and `13` insist on.

## T3. Teacher-led instruction vs student agency and self-direction

**Pole A: the teacher directs.** Rosenshine's principles and the guidance evidence (`01`, `00`):
small steps, many questions, models, guided practice, high success rates. Being cognitively active
is what matters, not being physically or organizationally in charge (ICAP). Transcend's "active
self-direction" and "anytime, anywhere learning" leaps (`08`) are flagged in `08`'s own file as in
tension with novice-guidance evidence, and competency-based and playlist models have mixed
evaluations.

**Pole B: the student owns it.** Wiliam & Leahy's fifth strategy, activating students as owners of
their learning (`07`); Yeager's questioning practice and status theory (`05`): sincere questions
confer respect and shift ownership while the standard stays fixed; Mehta & Fine (`09`): choice,
purpose and real responsibility are why the periphery works; Engel (`03`): pursuing your own idea
is the unit of intellectual growth; Samouha et al. (`08`): student experience of agency is a
leading indicator that correlates with outcomes.

**What the evidence says.** Agency over *what to think* and agency over *how the lesson is run* are
different things. The strongest evidence supports high teacher direction of content and sequence
for novices, combined with high student generation inside that structure (self-explanation,
retrieval, peer explanation with individual accountability, self-assessment taught as a skill).
The status and motivation evidence supports giving students real roles, choices within structure,
and a voice that is taken seriously. The correlational experience-survey data (`08`) cannot
separate "agency causes outcomes" from "functional schools have both." [E for direction of content
for novices; E for status and ownership mechanisms; H for self-directed models at scale.]

**Rule.** IF the learner is a novice in the content, THEN the teacher sets the sequence, the
examples and the success criteria, and agency is expressed through generation, self-assessment,
choice among structured options, and revision. IF the learner has demonstrated expertise (schemas
visible on diagnostics), THEN fade guidance toward self-directed projects with checkpoints. In all
cases, THEN treat students as capable agents in how they are spoken to, in real responsibilities
(peer tutoring, exhibitions, decisions about school life), and in transparency about why the
teacher is doing what she is doing.

**Switch signal.** Self-directed work produces busy, satisfied students whose delayed and transfer
measures are flat: pull direction back. Teacher-led rooms produce compliance with rising
disengagement on experience surveys and falling question rates: add ownership without removing
structure (questioning, choice within structure, real audiences).

**Common mistake.** Confusing physical or organizational activity with cognitive activity; buying a
personalized-learning platform as "agency"; or the opposite, running a controlled classroom so
tightly that adolescents read it as disrespect and withdraw effort.

## T4. Standardization and fidelity vs community co-design and local adaptation

**Pole A: standardize the mechanisms.** Wiliam (`10`): "tight but loose," non-negotiable active
ingredients, shared materials, a common instructional language; Wexler (`06`): coherence across
grades requires one spine, and adopting a curriculum is not the same as teaching it; Kirschner &
Hendrick (`01`): practices die by lethal mutation; Kapur (`04`): PF's effect depends on fidelity.

**Pole B: design with the community.** Samouha, Wetzler & Henry Wood (`08`): models imposed from
outside get reverted; a diverse design team including students and the least-served families
surfaces needs experts miss and builds the coalition that sustains the school; Bryk (`11`):
"everything works somewhere, nothing works everywhere"; local variation is real and must be
understood, not overridden; Mehta & Fine (`09`): identity and purpose are part of depth and cannot
be imported.

**What the evidence says.** Implementation research supports both halves: reforms without local
ownership fade, and practices that lose their mechanism stop working. The resolution is a layered
compact. Community-based design as a *process* has no causal evaluation and comes from the
organization that sells it; communities can converge on popular but ineffective ideas (learning
styles, unstructured discovery), and `08` itself is thin on how to adjudicate. The mechanisms with
the strongest evidence (systematic phonics, retrieval and spacing, guidance for novices, formative
use of evidence, comment-only feedback) should not be up for a vote. [E that imposed models revert
and that mutation kills mechanisms; V for co-design; H for its design-quality benefits.]

**Rule.** IF the layer is purpose, culture, graduate aims, the target student experience, the
periphery, and which knowledge to prioritise where evidence is silent, THEN co-design it with a
team that includes students and the least-served families, and expect it to be revisited. IF the
layer is a mechanism with robust evidence, THEN hold it tight and adapt only the technique around
it, and write the compact so that this distinction is explicit before the first design session.
IF a community proposal would change a mechanism, THEN treat it as an evidence review, not a vote,
and say so.

**Switch signal.** Co-design starts proposing mechanism changes ("drop phonics," "no retrieval
quizzes, they stress the children"): the compact was unclear; repair the governance, do not
relitigate the evidence. Fidelity audits show techniques keeping their names and losing their
mechanisms (a "hinge question" that is a recall item; "PF" without consolidation): loosen nothing,
retrain on mechanism. Staff and families describe the school as "done to them": the open layer has
been closed by default; reopen it.

**Common mistake.** Treating the whole design as negotiable to earn buy-in, then reverting under
pressure; or treating the whole design as fixed and being reverted by a community that was never
consulted on the things that were genuinely theirs to decide.

## T5. Accountability measurement vs improvement measurement

**Pole A: accountability.** External regimes, boards and authorizers need comparable, audited,
annual measures; Mehta & Fine (`09`) show that what is assessed gets taught (IB's depth-demanding
assessments moved teaching toward depth); Wiliam (`10`) uses accountability data to expose the
weak effects of popular reforms.

**Pole B: improvement.** Bryk (`11`): practical measures are short, frequent, low-stakes, tied to
specific changes and returned within days; using accountability measures for improvement gives you
lagging data once a year, and using improvement measures for accountability corrupts them
(Campbell's law); Wiliam & Leahy (`07`) and Kirschner & Hendrick (`01`): keep formative data and
TLC observation out of evaluation, or psychological safety collapses.

**What the evidence says.** That high-stakes use corrupts indicators is well documented. That
single-year value-added and single observations are unreliable for consequential decisions is
robust (MET project). Improvement science's own track record in schools rests on case narratives
selected for success; its methods have strong roots in manufacturing and a mixed record in
healthcare, where PDSA is often done without rigor. So the wall between the two systems is [E] in
its reason and [V] in its commitment; the improvement system's effect on outcomes is [H].

**Rule.** IF a measure is used to decide whether a teacher keeps a job, a salary, or a rating, THEN
it is an accountability measure: multiple measures, multiple years, never a practical measure, never
TLC observation data. IF a measure is used to learn whether a change worked, THEN it is a practical
measure: embedded in the work, returned within days, owned by the team, never reported upward by
individual. Report accountability measures honestly to the board and families; do not manage to
them. Track balancing measures (workload, well-being) with every change.

**Switch signal.** Practical measures improve for two years while accountability measures do not:
the driver diagram is wrong or the practical measures are gamed; audit the theory. Accountability
measures improve while practical measures do not: someone is teaching to the test or the practical
measures are insensitive. A principal cites PDSA data in an evaluation conversation: the wall has
been breached; repair it publicly.

**Common mistake.** Setting a year-two state-test target and demanding monthly progress reports
against it, which converts the entire improvement system into compliance paperwork within a term.

## T6. Selecting teachers vs developing them

**Pole A: hire and fire.** The intuitive founder position, and Hanushek's deselection arithmetic
summarised in `10`: replacing the bottom 5-10% of teachers with average ones would in principle
close national gaps; teacher effects are the largest in-school variable, so get the right people.
Mehta & Fine (`09`): the rare deep teachers had learned deeply themselves, which argues for hiring
for disciplinary depth.

**Pole B: love the one you're with.** Wiliam (`10`, `07`): credentials and interviews predict
effectiveness weakly (Kane, Rockoff & Staiger); identifying weak teachers reliably is expensive and
slow; merit pay trials are null; the supply of proven-effective teachers is small; so the scalable
lever is developing the teachers you already have, in classroom formative assessment, through
teacher learning communities. The EEF trial of that model found about two months' extra progress.

**What the evidence says.** Both facts hold: teacher effects are large, and hiring signals are weak.
Development is the only lever a single school can pull at scale. But development evidence is
narrower than its rhetoric: the TLC model has one large independent trial with a modest effect;
which ingredients are necessary is untested; broader PD syntheses support sustained, practice-based,
collaborative designs. Hiring on job-relevant work samples helps at the margin. [E for weak hiring
signals and null merit pay; E for the TLC model's modest effect; H for hiring criteria.]

**Rule.** IF choosing between spending on selection (salary premiums, recruiting) and on
development (protected TLC time, coaching, materials), THEN development first, with salary set to
retain rather than to select. IF hiring, THEN use work samples and probes for disciplinary depth,
coachability, curiosity and the mentor stance, and make TLC participation a condition of
employment; do not expect the hire to do the heavy lifting. IF a teacher refuses development, THEN
treat it as a compact question, not a talent question.

**Switch signal.** After two full years of TLCs, within-school variation in observed practice and in
practical measures has not narrowed: the TLC design is broken (usually no accountability for trying
techniques, turnover, or meetings that became sharing sessions). Turnover above the local norm
despite development: the working conditions (leadership, collegial support, time) are the problem,
and salary is not the first fix.

**Common mistake.** Believing a brilliant demo lesson; or the opposite, investing in development
while tolerating a hiring process with no work sample and no probe for coachability.

## T7. Core vs periphery

**Pole A: put the energy where deep learning already lives.** Mehta & Fine (`09`): across about 30
schools, the richest learning was in debate, theatre, robotics, the newspaper, teams and some
electives, because of choice, purpose, real performance, apprenticeship to practitioners and
mixed-age community. Engel (`03`): the intellectual life survives at the edges. Yeager (`05`): the
periphery gives adolescents status and real roles.

**Pole B: the core is the school.** Wiliam (`10`): teacher time is finite and the core is where all
students spend most of it; Wexler (`06`): knowledge is built in required courses; Mehta & Fine
themselves: the finding is a diagnosis, and reform must make the core look more like the periphery,
not add more periphery. Learning-design principle 11: what happens in the median ninth-grade maths
lesson on a Tuesday in February is the test of the school.

**What the evidence says.** The periphery pattern is striking and recurs across school types, but
it is ethnographic and selection-confounded: students choose the periphery, groups are small, test
pressure is absent, and coaches and directors are highly directive, so it is not evidence for
minimal guidance. Transplanting periphery features into required courses is a well-grounded
hypothesis with no outcome data. [E that depth is rare in core classes; H for importing
apprenticeship features; V for protecting the periphery.]

**Rule.** IF allocating design effort, THEN the core comes first: every core course, every term,
one authentic product for a real audience, with drafts expected and the teacher's own disciplinary
practice visible, on top of (not instead of) explicit instruction and a knowledge spine. IF
allocating budget under pressure, THEN protect the periphery as infrastructure with mixed-age
leadership ladders, because it is cheap relative to what it delivers and it is where identity
lives. Never build a two-tier school where only some students reach the deep program.

**Switch signal.** Students say the only place they learn is a club or elective: the core has
failed; redesign core units, do not add another elective. Core practical measures rise while
experience-survey items on agency, belonging and joy fall and periphery participation drops: the
periphery is being starved or the core has become control-heavy. Authentic products in core
courses lack disciplinary rigor on moderation: the apprenticeship feature has displaced the
knowledge spine; restore the sequence and critique protocols.

**Common mistake.** Adding a 90-minute daily interdisciplinary studio and leaving the core alone,
which reproduces Mehta & Fine's finding inside the new school.

## T8. High standards vs support (enforcer, protector, mentor)

**Pole A: enforce.** No-excuses operating systems (`09`): coherent instruction, high expectations,
measurable gains for disadvantaged students that critics say Mehta & Fine under-credit; Rosenshine
(`01`): high success rates need tight structure; Wexler (`06`): rigor is an equity lever.

**Pole B: protect.** Sympathy for adolescents under stress; belonging and well-being aims (`08`);
the fear that struggle reads as "I am not a maths person" (`05` on `04`); the joylessness Mehta &
Fine found at No Excuses High.

**What the evidence says.** Yeager (`05`) argues both poles are the same error: standards without
support and support without standards both communicate disrespect and both fail. The mentor mindset
(high standards, high support, transparency about why) is supported by the wise-feedback field
experiment (large effect, small sample, limited direct replication), by empathic-discipline work
(reduced suspensions), by stress-reappraisal experiments, and by correlational parenting research.
The 2x2 as a trained construct at scale is untested. Growth-mindset interventions have small,
heterogeneous effects and work only where the adult and peer context supports them. [E for the
component mechanisms; H for mentor-mindset training at scale; V for dignity.]

**Rule.** IF designing any adult-student routine (feedback, discipline, advising, grading, the
conversation with the student who stopped working), THEN audit it against the 2x2: does it hold the
bar, does it supply the scaffold, does it say why. IF a student fails or refuses, THEN the response
is a support plan toward the same standard, not a lowered bar and not a zero. IF a routine lowers
status in public (rankings, charts, announced demerits), THEN remove it regardless of its
short-term compliance effect.

**Switch signal.** Compliance rises while the quality of effort and question rates fall: drifting
to enforcer. Satisfaction rises while effort quality and practical measures fall: drifting to
protector. Wise-feedback lines appear as boilerplate on every paper and revision rates do not move:
the script has replaced the sincerity; coach the stance, not the sentence.

**Common mistake.** Reading "high support" as lower expectations, or reading "high standards" as
consequences, and toggling between them as the student's behaviour changes.

## T9. Curiosity hooks vs target learning

**Pole A: hook them first.** Engel (`03`) and the popular reading of Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath
(`00`): a curious state improves memory even for incidental faces shown while waiting for the
answer; so open lessons with something surprising and the learning will ride along. Learning-
design principle 9 adopted this framing.

**Pole B: the hook is the objective.** The follow-up studies in `00` and `12`: the incidental
benefit is small (about a four-point recognition gain in 2014), brief (Murphy et al. 2021),
inconsistent (Galli et al. 2018), and reversed for complex school-like facts (Keller, Salvi,
Leiker, Gruber & Dunsmoor 2024): a high-curiosity state pulls processing toward the open question
and crowds out unrelated demanding material. Sweller (`00`): an unrelated hook is extraneous load.

**What the evidence says.** Curiosity for the target replicates reasonably well; the halo does not.
Most of the work uses adults and trivia, not children over weeks. The design implication is
precise: put the learning objective inside the question, resolve it with the target content, and do
not rely on attention-grabbers to prime what follows. Posner's dissatisfaction condition and
Kapur's awareness phase are the same move with the same warning. [E for target curiosity; E that
unrelated hooks can hurt, from one 2024 study; H for classroom transfer.]

**Rule.** IF opening a lesson or unit, THEN pose a question whose answer is the objective (a
prediction, a pretest, an anomaly the concept explains), right before teaching it, and close it
with the target content. IF a proposed hook is exciting and unrelated (a video, a story that leads
elsewhere), THEN cut it, especially before high element-interactivity content. IF the aim is
children's own curiosity as a disposition, THEN that belongs to norms and the protected block (T2),
not to the opening two minutes of a maths lesson.

**Switch signal.** Hinge data show students remembering the hook and not the content; students can
retell the story and not state the principle. Hooks consuming more than a few minutes of a lesson.
Conversely, lesson openings that are pure announcement with no question at all and low engagement
on the first retrieval: add a target-bearing question.

**Common mistake.** "Engagement" activities that are memorable in themselves; and hooks that raise a
question the lesson never answers.

## T10. Conceptual change vs coverage

**Pole A: cover the standards.** Pacing guides, accountability tests, and the reasonable worry that
a broad knowledge base (T2) needs breadth; Wiliam's cost lens (`10`): every hour spent revisiting a
concept is an hour not spent elsewhere.

**Pole B: fewer things, restructured, revisited.** Shtulman (`02`), Amin (`13`), Posner, Chi and
Vosniadou (`00`): misconceptions are theories; telling produces synthetic models that pass recall
tests; the old theory coexists with the new for life, so mastery means the scientific model
reliably wins, which takes mechanism, multiple representations, and returns across years; some
concepts are unteachable without prerequisites (particulate matter before photosynthesis); Mehta &
Fine (`09`): coverage produces "doing school."

**What the evidence says.** The descriptive evidence (which misconceptions, how persistent,
coexistence in experts, lecture leaving them intact) is among the most replicated in science
education. The intervention evidence is thinner: refutation texts, bridging analogies, mechanism-
focused teaching and interactive engagement have moderate support; no school-scale multi-year
trial exists; cognitive conflict alone often fails because students explain the anomaly away.
Learning-progression research supports long progressions around few core concepts. [E for the
problem; E/H for the fixes; H for any specific spiral schedule.]

**Rule.** IF a concept is load-bearing (later ideas depend on it) and has a documented intuitive
rival (force, energy, matter, heat, evolution, proportion), THEN plan it as a multi-year progression
with a diagnostic, a mechanism, a named change type, standard representations across grades, and
assessment by prediction and transfer items; budget the time by removing topics nothing later
depends on. IF a topic is factual and no later idea depends on it, THEN cover it efficiently or cut
it. Coverage is never the reason to teach a hard concept once.

**Switch signal.** End-of-unit recall is high while delayed prediction or transfer items show the
intuitive model (the evolution unit that reverts to teleology two years later): a spiral is missing
or is revisiting without confronting. Concept-inventory pre/post gains near zero in a course with
good pacing-guide compliance: the course is covering. Students holding the scientific answer and
the intuitive answer in different contexts: normal waypoint; do not grade it as failure in
formative settings, but do plan the next return.

**Common mistake.** Reading "knowledge-rich" as a fact list and building a science curriculum that
adds facts on top of intact intuitive theories; or "teaching it harder in Year 8" when the problem
is that it is never returned to.

## T11. Confront the intuitive theory vs build on it

**Pole A: theory-change; confront and replace.** Shtulman (`02`), Vosniadou and Chi (`00`),
Posner's four conditions: intuitive knowledge is coherent enough to behave like a theory; surface
it, create dissatisfaction, supply a better mechanism, reclassify where the error is ontological.

**Pole B: knowledge-in-pieces; refine and reorganize.** diSessa, Hammer and Minstrell as presented
in `13`: intuitive knowledge is fragmented p-prims activated by context; many "misconceptions" are
productive pieces used in the wrong place; treat intuitions as resources; apparent coherence is
partly an interview artifact. Kapur's activation phase (`04`) and Engel's respect for children's
theorizing (`03`) sit closer to this pole.

**What the evidence says.** The coherence debate is empirically unresolved and method-dependent
(consistency-seeking interviews find coherence; varied-context probes find fragments). Amin's
synthesis holds that both are true at different levels of analysis, which is coherent and hard to
falsify. Both camps agree that telling alone fails, that prior ideas must be elicited, and that
hybrid states are normal. The practical difference is tone and sequence: confrontation-first risks
students explaining anomalies away; resource-first risks never reaching the restructuring.
Learning-design's tension 8 framed this as "confront vs avoid," which was too coarse; the real
choice is confront versus reorganize, and the answer is both, in sequence. [E that elicitation and
mechanism matter; contested on the underlying structure; H for any specific sequence.]

**Rule.** IF beginning a hard concept, THEN elicit and honour the intuition first (it is data and it
is the student's), identify which pieces are productive, and build placeholder structures
explicitly. IF the error is ontological (heat as substance, evolution as transformation of
individuals), THEN dedicated instruction on the category is required and reorganization alone will
not get there. IF confronting with an anomaly, THEN do it after the alternative is intelligible,
with a mechanism ready, with immediate correction, and never leave the wrong model as the last
thing seen.

**Switch signal.** After a confrontation-heavy unit, hinge-question distractor rates for the
misconception rise: the confrontation is being remembered as the content; move the anomaly later
and the mechanism earlier. After a resource-heavy unit, students hold refined intuitions but cannot
state or use the scientific model: the restructuring phase was skipped; add explicit concept work
and category instruction.

**Common mistake.** Picking a camp and teaching every concept the same way, when `13`'s verified
thesis is that force, energy, matter and species each need a different kind of change.

## T12. Testing as measurement vs testing as learning (grades vs comments)

**Pole A: assess to measure and report.** Families, boards and transcripts need grades; Wiliam
(`10`) needs comparable measures to judge reforms; Mehta & Fine (`09`) show alignment moving
teaching toward whatever the assessment demands, which cuts both ways.

**Pole B: assess to learn.** Roediger & Karpicke, Dunlosky and Bjork (`01`), Ranganath (`12`): the
testing effect is among the most robust findings in memory research; retrieval rewrites memory;
fluency and recognition mislead. Wiliam & Leahy (`07`): formative is a use of evidence, and Butler
showed grades swamp comments; Kluger & DeNisi: a third of feedback interventions lower performance.
Kapur (`04`): generation phases must be ungraded or failure produces avoidance.

**What the evidence says.** The testing effect and the grade-swamps-comment finding are both
solid, though Butler's original samples were small and specific. Grading systems that reward
revision have mentor-mindset backing and mixed direct outcome evidence. Alignment effects are
ethnographic but consistent with a long literature. The synthesis: most assessment should be
retrieval for learning and evidence for decisions; a small, clearly separated set of summative
points exists for reporting; and what the summative points demand (depth or recall) will shape
teaching. [E for retrieval and comment-only mechanisms; H for grading policy designs; V for what to
report to families.]

**Rule.** IF an assessment is formative (most of them), THEN it is frequent, low-stakes, spaced,
answered by every student, returned with comments that require action, and never graded. IF an
assessment is summative, THEN there are few of them, they are cumulative, they demand transfer and
depth rather than recall, revision toward the standard is allowed where feasible, and they are the
only things that produce a grade. IF families or students treat every check as a judgment, THEN
the communication has failed: rename, explain, and show the retrieval rationale.

**Switch signal.** Teachers asking for grades on everything "so students take it seriously":
effort is being bought with status threat; fix the feedback so it requires work instead. Students
opting out of retrieval quizzes or copying: the quizzes have become high-stakes in fact if not in
name. Summative grades stable while delayed transfer is poor: the summative points are recall-only;
redesign them.

**Common mistake.** Grade-plus-comment on formative work, which costs the most teacher time and
delivers the least learning.

## T13. Structural reform vs instructional reform

**Pole A: change the structure.** Mehta & Fine (`09`): the grammar of schooling (periods, silos,
seat time, isolated teachers, grades as currency) works against depth whatever the mission says;
Samouha et al. (`08`): derive schedule, staffing and space from the target student experience;
Engel (`03`): institutionalize the workshop or lose it, because culture does not survive pressure.

**Pole B: change the contents, not the container.** Wiliam (`10`): class size, choice, governance
and autonomy have small or expensive effects and being a new school guarantees nothing; Kirschner
& Hendrick (`01`): method, not medium; Bryk (`11`): map the process that produces the result before
redesigning the organization chart; Wiliam again: change capacity is finite and initiative overload
is the default failure.

**What the evidence says.** The structural critique is a well-supported diagnosis and an untested
prescription. The evidence that structure-only reform fails is strong. Some structures are
instructional prerequisites: TLCs need protected monthly time and peer observation slots; spacing
needs daily or near-daily contact in skill subjects; authentic products need long enough blocks to
iterate; PDSA needs weekly team time. [E that structure alone does nothing; H for each specific
structural change; V for the periphery.]

**Rule.** IF a structural change is proposed, THEN it must name the instructional or developmental
need it serves, the mechanism it enables, and what it displaces; otherwise keep the structure
conventional and spend the change capacity on instruction, assessment and adult learning. IF an
instructional default is blocked by structure (no common planning time, no observation slots, no
block long enough to iterate a product), THEN change the structure, minimally, to unblock it.

**Switch signal.** TLCs cancelled or shortened for lack of time; teachers unable to observe each
other; authentic products always rushed at the end of a term; departments in a schedule war
(blocks for depth versus daily lessons for spacing): these are structural blocks and get structural
fixes, differentiated by subject if necessary. A structural redesign occupying the leadership
agenda for a year while practical measures in classrooms are unchanged: the container has eaten
the contents.

**Common mistake.** Designing the schedule first because it is the founder's most visible decision,
then fitting instruction to it.

## T14. Name the answer in advance vs discover it locally (Wiliam's bet vs Bryk's method)

**Pole A: the answers are known; implement them.** Wiliam (`10`, `07`): decades of cognitive
science and a cost-effectiveness lens already tell you what to do (knowledge-rich curriculum,
formative assessment, TLCs); local trial-and-error wastes the scarcest resource; "everything works
somewhere" is a reason to import mechanisms, not to reinvent them. Wexler (`06`) and Kirschner &
Hendrick (`01`) take the same stance. `11`'s own file warns that improvement enthusiasts sometimes
treat local PDSA results as equal to settled science.

**Pole B: the answers are local; learn them.** Bryk (`11`): reform keeps adopting solutions and
rolling them out, getting variable and mediocre results; outcomes are produced by systems that
must be seen; what works, for whom, under what conditions is the empirical question; small
predicted tests build practical knowledge no meta-analysis contains. Samouha et al. (`08`): map,
zoom in, test-drive, look ahead.

**What the evidence says.** Both are right about their own territory. Settled mechanisms (retrieval,
guidance for novices, phonics, comment-only feedback) should not be rediscovered by PDSA. How those
mechanisms survive contact with this building, these teachers and these students is unknown in
advance and is exactly what improvement cycles are for. Bryk's own cases mostly concern structural
outcomes (graduation, credits, attendance); the method's fit for hard-to-measure depth is weaker.
Wiliam's headline effect sizes have been revised downward; his cost-effectiveness case survives.
[E for the settled mechanisms; E/H for improvement as implementation method; H for its effect on
school outcomes.]

**Rule.** IF a question is "which mechanism," THEN answer it from the evidence and adopt. IF the
question is "how does this mechanism work here, for whom is it failing, and what adaptation keeps
its active ingredient," THEN answer it with a driver diagram, small predicted tests and practical
measures. Every PDSA change idea must attach to a driver that attaches to a known mechanism; no
cycle is run to discover pedagogy from scratch.

**Switch signal.** PDSA cycles proposing to test whether retrieval practice or phonics "works for
our kids": the method has drifted into rediscovery; redirect to implementation questions. An
adopted mechanism failing for a subgroup while leaders insist the evidence says it works: Wiliam's
stance has drifted into dogma; study the variation. PDSA templates filled in after the fact:
compliance drift; stop the routine until it can be run with predictions.

**Common mistake.** Founders with a product background running the school as a hypothesis
generator, testing everything and adopting nothing; or founders with a research background adopting
everything and testing nothing.

## How to use this file

Before a contested decision, find the tension, state which pole the decision leans toward, apply the
rule, and write the switch signal into the design brief next to the decision as its [H] test. If a
decision does not fit any tension here, it is either a pure [V] (say so and name who might
reasonably disagree) or a pure [E] (cite the mechanism), and if it is neither, it is not yet a
decision.
