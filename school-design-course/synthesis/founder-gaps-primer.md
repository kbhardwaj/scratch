# Founder gaps primer: what the thirteen books do not teach you

A companion to section 6 of `synthesis/learning-design.md`. That section lists the areas a real
founder of a K-12 school needs that a learning-science curriculum does not cover. This file briefs you
on each one. It does not replace expert help; it lets you ask the lawyer, accountant,
special-education coordinator and reading specialist the right questions and spot weak answers.

**How to use it.** Read the "Why it matters" line and the numbered points for every section
(about 40 minutes). Treat the "Read next" lists as a queue. Do the finance and governance
sections first if you are within 18 months of opening.

**Tags** (the course convention, plus one):
- **[E]**: supported by reasonably strong evidence.
- **[H]**: a plausible hypothesis to test in your own context (PDSA, as in Module 9).
- **[V]**: a value commitment. Evidence can inform it but cannot settle it.
- **[Jurisdictional]**: depends on the law and funding rules where you operate. Everything
  tagged this way is written from a US vantage point unless stated otherwise, with English
  equivalents noted where useful. Check it locally before you act on it.

**Verification note.** Studies and resources marked "verified" were confirmed by web search
(title, authors, venue, headline result) in September 2026. Full texts were not read, because
page fetching was blocked. Claims marked "background" come from general knowledge of the field
and were not re-checked. Treat them as leads to confirm, not as facts to quote.

## 1. School finance and the operating model

**Why it matters.** Schools usually run out of students or cash before weak pedagogy sinks
them; under-enrollment is the most common reason given in US charter closure data. Your instructional model is only as durable as the spreadsheet that pays for it.

**Things to know**

1. **Revenue is per pupil, and it follows the child.** A public school (district or charter)
   gets most of its operating revenue as a formula amount per enrolled or attending student,
   plus weights for disability, poverty, English learners and sometimes grade level. An
   independent school gets tuition, minus financial aid "discounting," plus philanthropy. A
   microschool gets tuition, and in a growing number of US states it also gets education savings
   account (ESA) or voucher funds. Know which count day or attendance measure drives your money,
   because a school funded on average daily attendance loses revenue when attendance slips.
   [Jurisdictional]
2. **Staffing is the business.** NCES reports that US public schools spent about $16,280 per
   pupil on current expenditures in 2020-21 (in 2022-23 dollars). Salaries and benefits made up
   79% of that spending (verified: NCES Condition of Education, "Public School Expenditures").
   Expect 70-80% of your costs to be people. The levers that matter are therefore class size,
   the ratio of teaching to non-teaching staff, the teaching load and the pay scale. Each is
   also an instructional decision; Module 7's "grammar of school" debates are staffing-model
   arguments in money terms. [E]
3. **Do the classroom-level unit economics first.** Revenue per classroom is roughly per-pupil
   revenue times students per class. Subtract the fully loaded teacher cost (salary plus
   benefits, typically 20-35% on top of salary [background]) and that classroom's share of
   leaders, operations, special education, facilities and overhead. If a full classroom does
   not cover its share, adding classrooms will not rescue you. Illustrative arithmetic only:
   24 students times $12,000 is $288,000 per class. That has to fund far more than the teacher. [E for the arithmetic; the numbers are yours to supply]
4. **The enrollment ramp is the single riskiest assumption.** Most new schools open with a few
   grades and add one a year. Fixed costs (the leader, operations, the building) arrive before
   the students who pay for them, so a new school usually loses money on operations for its
   first two to four years. It survives on start-up grants, philanthropy or loans. Model three
   scenarios: target, 85% of target and 70% of target. Also model attrition between grades,
   which is often 5-15% a year in schools of choice [background]. A school that breaks even
   only at 100% enrollment is not viable. [E for the pattern, H for your numbers]
5. **Facilities are the hidden killer, especially for US charters.** In many states charters
   get little or no dedicated facilities funding, so rent or debt service comes out of
   operating dollars that districts spend on instruction. Charter per-pupil funding also often
   trails district funding in the same area; the size of the gap is contested and varies by
   state [background]. Zoning, fire code, occupancy limits, accessibility and outdoor space
   requirements can each delay opening; secure the building early and pad renovation budgets. [Jurisdictional]
6. **Know the operating models and their trade-offs.**
   - **Charter (US):** a public, tuition-free school under a performance contract with an
     authorizer, admitting by lottery if oversubscribed. It trades public accountability and
     special-education duties for autonomy over staffing, schedule and curriculum. Contracts
     are renewable (often five years [background]) and revocable.
   - **Independent/private:** tuition-funded, maximum autonomy, usually exempt from state
     tests, fully exposed to the market. Accreditation is voluntary but matters commercially.
   - **Microschool:** very small (roughly 10 to 150 students), low overhead, sometimes
     ESA-funded. It is fast to launch but fragile (one teacher leaving is a crisis) and lightly
     regulated, which cuts both ways for quality. Reported rapid growth since 2020; headline
     size estimates are unverified.
   - **District partnership (innovation school, in-district charter):** negotiated autonomy
     inside a district. You get stability and facilities, but district politics and union
     contracts can take the autonomy back.
   - **England:** free schools and academies are state-funded through trusts overseen by the
     DfE; independent schools register with the DfE and are inspected. [Jurisdictional]
7. **Cash is not budget.** Public funding arrives in lagged installments and grants reimburse
   after the fact, so a school can be solvent on paper and miss payroll in October. Build a monthly cash-flow model, keep a reserve target (many authorizers look for
   30-60 days of operating cash [background]), and get a clean independent audit every year.
   [E]
8. **Spending patterns encode values whether you intend it or not.** Roza shows district money often flows against stated priorities
   (more per pupil in advantaged schools, via salary structures). Treat your budget as your
   design brief in numbers and audit it against the brief annually. [E for
   the pattern, V for your priorities]

**Evidence status summary.** The economics are arithmetic ([E]). The funding rules are
[Jurisdictional]. Which operating model is "best" is [V] plus context. On outcomes, CREDO's 2023
National Charter School Study III (verified) found charter students gained on average about 16
more days of learning in reading and 6 in math a year than matched district peers. The spread
between strong and weak charters is far larger than that average.

**On closures (verified, with a source caveat).** A 2024 report from the National Center for
Charter School Accountability found that more than a quarter of charters closed within five
years and about 55% by year twenty. It gave insufficient enrollment as the most common reason
(47% of closures). That center is a project of a group critical of charters, so read the numbers
as an upper-bound warning, not a neutral base rate. The direction of the finding, that
enrollment kills schools, is uncontroversial.

**Common founder mistakes**
- Building the budget from the ideal staffing model instead of from realistic revenue.
- Assuming full enrollment in year one and zero attrition.
- Treating facilities as a later problem.
- No cash-flow model, only an annual budget.
- Hiring the full leadership team before there are enough students to pay for it, or hiring
  too thin, so that the founder does operations and the instructional program starves.
- Letting a charter management or back-office vendor own the numbers without the board being
  able to read them.

**Read next**
1. *Educational Economics: Where Do School Funds Go?*, Marguerite Roza (Urban Institute Press,
   2010, 128 pp.). Verified. Short and readable, and it explains why budgets betray priorities.
2. NCES Condition of Education indicator "Public School Expenditures" (nces.ed.gov). Verified.
   Use it to anchor your per-pupil and salary-share assumptions, then replace them with your
   state's figures.
3. Your intended authorizer's or regulator's financial performance framework and application
   budget template (for example, a state charter board's workbook). Unverified generic pointer.
   This is the document you will actually be judged against.

## 2. Governance, authorizing and law

**Why it matters.** Legal form decides who can close you, who you answer to, and what you owe
every child. Governance failures (captured boards, unsupervised founders, mismanaged money) are
a leading cause of closure after enrollment.

**Things to know**

1. **The board governs; the head leads.** A board holds fiduciary duties: care (being informed
   and diligent), loyalty (no self-dealing, conflicts disclosed) and obedience (fidelity to the
   mission and the law). It hires, evaluates and can remove the leader, approves the budget and
   monitors results and compliance. It does not run the school. Dysfunction runs both ways:
   boards that meddle, or founder-friendly boards that rubber-stamp. [E for the pattern]
2. **Design the board for its future job.** You need, at minimum, finance or audit skill, legal
   skill, education expertise independent of the founder, community credibility and fundraising
   capacity. Put in place term limits, a conflict-of-interest policy, an annual evaluation of the
   leader against pre-agreed goals, and a board self-evaluation. Founders who staff the board
   with friends pay for it at the first crisis. [H]
3. **Authorizing is a contract for performance (US charters).** The authorizer, which may be a
   state board, a district, a university or an independent chartering board depending on the
   state, approves the application, sets academic, financial and organizational performance
   frameworks, monitors, and decides renewal or revocation. NACSA's *Principles & Standards for
   Quality Charter School Authorizing* (2023 edition, verified) organizes this around three
   principles: maintain high standards, uphold school autonomy, and protect student and public
   interests. Read it before you write an application, because it tells you what a good
   authorizer will be checking. [Jurisdictional]
4. **Licensing and registration elsewhere.** Independent schools usually register with the
   state or national education authority and meet minimum standards on health and safety,
   curriculum breadth, staff checks and premises. In England they are inspected against the
   Independent School Standards. Accreditation (regional or association) is separate and
   voluntary. [Jurisdictional]
5. **Accountability regimes shape behavior.** US public schools, including charters, take
   state tests and appear in state accountability ratings under federal ESSA requirements.
   English state schools are inspected by Ofsted and judged on published performance
   measures. Know which numbers will be used to judge you, and read section 7 on how measures
   distort when stakes attach. [Jurisdictional; the distortion is E]
6. **Safeguarding is non-negotiable and operational.** Required elements include background
   checks for every adult with access to children (fingerprinting and registry checks in the
   US; enhanced DBS checks in England), a designated safeguarding lead, staff training,
   mandated-reporter duties, safe-recruitment practice, clear rules for adult-child contact and
   online communication, and procedures for allegations against staff. In England the statutory
   baseline is the DfE's *Keeping Children Safe in Education*, updated annually. The 2025
   edition took effect on 1 September 2025 (verified). It now names misinformation and
   disinformation as online-content risks and points schools to DfE product-safety expectations
   for generative AI. [Jurisdictional, and V: you should exceed the minimum]
7. **The rest of the legal surface.** Admissions law (lotteries, preferences, non-discrimination), special
   education (section 3), employment law, student data privacy (in
   the US, FERPA and COPPA plus state student-privacy laws; UK GDPR in England), disability
   access to premises, health requirements (immunization, medication administration, allergies),
   transport, food programs, and insurance (general liability, directors and officers, abuse and
   molestation coverage). [Jurisdictional]

**Common founder mistakes**
- Picking a legal form for ideological reasons without modeling its funding and obligations.
- A board of admirers.
- Treating the authorizer as an adversary instead of a performance partner whose framework you
  should design to.
- No written safeguarding policy or designated lead on day one. Some regulators treat this as a
  reason to refuse or close a school.
- Adopting ed-tech that collects student data without a data-processing agreement.

**Read next**
1. NACSA, *Principles & Standards for Quality Charter School Authorizing* (2023 edition,
   qualitycharters.org). Verified. It is the authorizer's view of you.
2. *Keeping Children Safe in Education* (DfE, current edition, gov.uk). Verified. It is
   English statutory guidance, but it is the clearest single safeguarding checklist in English
   even for non-UK founders; adapt it to local law.
3. A charter board governance provider's materials, for example BoardOnTrack (founded by
   charter governance specialist Marci Cornell-Feist). Existence verified; content quality not
   independently assessed. Better still, spend one hour with a lawyer who has opened a school in
   your jurisdiction.

## 3. Special education and inclusion

**Why it matters.** Roughly one in seven US public school students is served under the federal
disability law (about 7.5 million, around 15% [background, NCES figure]). Many more struggle
without a formal label. A school designed for the median child fails these students, breaks the
law, and loses families. This is also where the course's evidence on explicit instruction and
formative assessment matters most.

**Things to know**

1. **The US legal frame (IDEA and Section 504).** IDEA entitles eligible students with
   disabilities to a free appropriate public education (FAPE) in the least restrictive
   environment (LRE), delivered through an Individualized Education Program (IEP). Schools also
   have a "Child Find" duty to identify students who may need services. Section 504 of the
   Rehabilitation Act, together with the ADA, prohibits disability discrimination and requires
   accommodations (a "504 plan") for students whose disability limits a major life activity even
   if they do not qualify for an IEP. US charters are public schools and carry these
   obligations. Depending on the state they are their own local education agency (LEA) or part
   of a district's, which changes who pays. Private schools have far narrower obligations
   (district-provided "equitable services," not an individual FAPE entitlement [background]).
   In England, the equivalent frame is the SEND Code of Practice with Education, Health and Care
   Plans (EHCPs). [Jurisdictional]
2. **Budget for it realistically.** Specialists, therapists, aides and evaluations are expensive and
   rarely fully funded; one high-need placement can cost several times per-pupil revenue. Ask
   about state risk pools or consortia. [Jurisdictional]
3. **MTSS/RTI is the operating system for "all means all."** A multi-tiered system of supports
   combines universal screening of everyone, strong core (Tier 1) instruction, targeted
   small-group intervention (Tier 2), intensive individualized intervention (Tier 3), and
   frequent progress monitoring to move students between tiers. The IES practice guide on
   response to intervention in reading (Gersten et al., 2009, verified) gives five
   recommendations: screen, deliver differentiated instruction, provide intensive systematic
   small-group intervention, monitor progress, and escalate. Evidence is strongest for
   small-group foundational-skills intervention, weaker for the whole-system package. [E for intervention components, H for your implementation]
4. **Tier 1 quality is the biggest special-education lever.** Strong core instruction (explicit, sequenced, checked, well
   practiced) shrinks the number who need intensive help and stops weak teaching masquerading
   as disability. See Modules 1, 4 and 5. [E]
5. **Intensive intervention looks like explicit instruction, only more so.** It uses smaller
   groups, more time, tighter sequencing, more modeling and worked examples, more practice with
   immediate feedback, and cumulative review. Gersten, Chard, Jayanthi et al. (2009, *Review of
   Educational Research*, verified) found explicit instruction and teaching heuristics for
   problem solving among the strongest components of math instruction for students with
   learning disabilities. Well-trained adult or older-student tutoring helped too; peer-assisted
   learning helped little. [E]
6. **UDL: a useful design prompt with a contested evidence base.** Universal Design for
   Learning (CAST) asks designers to build in multiple means of engagement, representation, and
   action and expression from the start, instead of retrofitting accommodations. A 2023
   meta-analysis (King-Sears et al., *Teaching and Teacher Education*, verified) found a
   moderate pooled effect (g = 0.43) across 20 studies. The authors themselves flagged highly
   variable study quality and thin reporting. Critics, most prominently Guy Boysen (verified in
   secondary coverage), argue that UDL resembles the debunked learning-styles idea in several
   ways: a loose definition, borrowed neuroscience language, and few trials isolating its
   effects. The critique is common in the Kirschner-Hendrick evidence-informed tradition, though
   no specific Kirschner essay on UDL was verified. The practical reading is to use UDL's access principles (captioning, readable materials,
   assistive technology, flexible ways to show knowledge where the skill being assessed permits
   it), and not to use it as licence to multiply modalities or let students opt out of the hard
   core of explicit instruction. That would clash with cognitive load theory (Module 1). [H,
   with access obligations Jurisdictional]
7. **Inclusion is a design choice about placement, not only a value.** Full inclusion,
   co-teaching, pull-out and specialized settings each have trade-offs. The law requires the
   least restrictive appropriate setting for each child, not a single model for all. [V within
   Jurisdictional limits]

**Common founder mistakes**
- "We'll figure out special education once we're open." Evaluations, IEP compliance and
  staffing need to exist on day one.
- Counseling out students with disabilities, formally or informally. It is illegal in public
  schools and corrosive everywhere.
- Adopting UDL or "personalized learning" language as a substitute for screening and
  intensive intervention.
- No universal screener in K-3.

**Read next**
1. IES/WWC practice guide, *Assisting Students Struggling with Reading: Response to
   Intervention and Multi-Tier Intervention in the Primary Grades* (Gersten et al., NCEE
   2009-4045). Verified. Free.
2. IES/WWC practice guide, *Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics: Intervention in the
   Elementary Grades* (Fuchs et al., 2021). Verified. Its six recommendations are systematic
   instruction, mathematical language, representations, number lines, word problems, and timed
   activities.
3. King-Sears et al. (2023) UDL meta-analysis, read alongside Boysen's critique. Both verified
   to exist. Read them together to calibrate.

## 4. Early literacy and early numeracy

**Why it matters.** A child who cannot decode fluently by about age 8 struggles in every subject
afterwards, and early math skill turns out to be an unusually strong predictor of later
achievement. This is the best-evidenced territory in education. A founder who gets K-2 wrong has
no excuse.

### Early literacy

1. **The Simple View of Reading.** Gough and Tunmer (1986) proposed that reading comprehension
   is decoding times language comprehension: if either is near zero, so is comprehension. Scarborough's
   Reading Rope (2001) unpacks each strand. Word recognition consists of phonological
   awareness, decoding and sight recognition. Language comprehension consists of background
   knowledge, vocabulary, language structures, verbal reasoning and literacy knowledge. Word
   recognition strands become increasingly automatic; comprehension strands become
   increasingly strategic. (Both verified.) The Wexler book in this course covers the
   comprehension half; this section is about the decoding half. [E]
2. **Systematic phonics beats unsystematic phonics.** The US National Reading Panel (2000)
   meta-analysis (Ehri et al., 2001) found an overall effect of about d = 0.44 for systematic
   phonics. Against whole-language instruction with only incidental phonics the advantage was
   smaller (about 0.31), and against whole-word approaches with no systematic phonics it was
   larger (about 0.51) (verified via Stuebing et al.'s reanalysis). Effects were largest in
   kindergarten and first grade. England's Rose Review (2006, verified) recommended systematic
   synthetic phonics, taught discretely, as the prime approach to early word reading. Castles,
   Rastle and Nation's 2018 review, *Ending the Reading Wars* (verified), is the best modern
   synthesis. It is emphatic about phonics for beginners and equally emphatic that fluency,
   vocabulary and knowledge must follow. [E]
3. **England ran a national natural experiment.** A statutory Year 1 phonics screening check,
   in which children read 40 words including pseudo-words, began in 2012. The share of pupils
   meeting the expected standard rose from 58% in 2012 to 82% in 2018-19, with most of the rise
   by 2016 (verified, DfE statistics). The check shows the policy changed decoding. Whether it
   raised later comprehension is more debated, and critics argue some of the rise reflects
   teaching to the check (Campbell's law; section 7). England's strong PIRLS 2021 result is often cited in support
   [background]. [E for decoding gains, H for downstream effects]
4. **The scope and sequence problem.** "Systematic" means an explicit, cumulative order of
   grapheme-phoneme correspondences taught to mastery, decodable texts matched to what has been
   taught, daily review, and blending and segmenting practice. Common failures: phonics in name
   only; "three-cueing" (guessing words from pictures or context), which undercuts decoding;
   non-decodable leveled readers; and phonics undone by the rest of the day. Choose one
   well-specified program, train everyone on it, and do not let teachers mix programs. [E for principles, H for which program]
5. **The practice guide's four moves.** The IES K-3 foundational skills guide (Foorman et al.,
   2016, verified) recommends teaching academic language, including inferential and narrative
   language and vocabulary; building phonemic awareness linked to letters; teaching decoding,
   word analysis and spelling; and having every child read connected text every day for
   accuracy, fluency and comprehension. [E]
6. **Screen early and repeatedly, for dyslexia risk too.** Screen everyone in K-3 at least three
   times a year on phonemic awareness, letter-sound knowledge, word and pseudo-word reading and
   oral reading fluency. Monitor progress for anyone below benchmark. Many US states now mandate
   dyslexia screening. Intervene in small groups without waiting for a formal diagnosis. [E,
   screening mandates Jurisdictional]
7. **Knowledge still matters from day one.** Decoding is necessary but not sufficient. A
   knowledge-rich read-aloud and content curriculum in K-2 builds the language comprehension
   strand, which is the Wexler argument. Phonics and knowledge are complements, not rivals. [E]

### Early numeracy

1. **Early math predicts later achievement strongly.** Duncan et al. (2007, *Developmental
   Psychology*, verified), pooling six longitudinal datasets, found that school-entry math
   skills were the strongest predictor of later achievement, ahead of reading and attention,
   while socioemotional measures predicted little. Correlational evidence, but a strong signal
   for K-2 priorities. [E for the association]
2. **Number sense has teachable components.** They include subitizing (instantly seeing small
   quantities), counting with one-to-one correspondence and cardinality, comparing magnitudes,
   the mental number line, composing and decomposing numbers (for example, 7 as 5 and 2),
   and early operations. Siegler and Ramani showed brief linear board-game play improves
   magnitude understanding [background]. [E]
3. **Use developmental progressions and a real curriculum.** The IES guide *Teaching Math to
   Young Children* (Frye et al., 2013, verified) recommends teaching number and operations, and
   separately geometry, patterns, measurement and data, along developmental progressions; using
   progress monitoring; teaching children to see and describe the world mathematically; and
   dedicating daily time to math. Clements and Sarama's *Building Blocks* preschool curriculum,
   in a randomized trial (2008, *AERJ*, verified), produced effects of about 0.47 SD versus
   another math curriculum and about 1.07 SD versus control. [E]
4. **Fact fluency is a goal, not a punishment.** Rapid recall of basic facts frees working
   memory for problem solving (Module 1). Build it through strategy instruction, then brief, frequent,
   low-stakes timed practice (an IES 2021 recommendation), not public high-anxiety tests. [E]

**Common founder mistakes**
- Delegating the reading program choice to individual teachers' preferences.
- Buying a "balanced literacy" program with phonics added on.
- No screener, or screening without acting on the data.
- Treating K-2 math as counting songs rather than a sequenced curriculum.
- Assuming a child will "grow into" reading by third grade.

**Read next**
1. Castles, Rastle and Nation (2018), "Ending the Reading Wars: Reading Acquisition From Novice
   to Expert," *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 19(1). Verified.
2. IES/WWC, *Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding in Kindergarten Through
   3rd Grade* (Foorman et al., 2016). Verified.
3. IES/WWC, *Teaching Math to Young Children* (Frye et al., 2013). Verified.

## 5. Mathematics curriculum design (beyond the early years)

**Why it matters.** Math is the subject where cumulative gaps are most visible and hardest to
repair, where curriculum choice is most contested, and where the course's mechanisms (worked
examples, retrieval, interleaving, productive failure) are most directly usable.

**Things to know**

1. **Conceptual understanding and procedural fluency grow together.** Rittle-Johnson, Schneider
   and Star (2015, verified) reviewed the evidence and concluded that the relation is
   bidirectional: understanding supports procedures, and procedures support understanding. The
   "math wars," which pitted conceptual discovery approaches against procedural drill, rested on
   a false choice. The evidence-based question is sequencing and balance for these learners on
   this topic. [E]
2. **Fractions and whole-number fluency are the gateway to algebra.** The US National
   Mathematics Advisory Panel (2008, *Foundations for Success*, verified) concluded that students
   need automatic recall of arithmetic facts in the early grades. It identified fractions as the
   most important foundational skill US students were not developing, and recommended a focused,
   coherent progression toward algebra rather than a broad, shallow one. [E]
3. **Worked examples first for novices, with faded guidance.** This is the course's Module 1
   and Module 4 result applied to math. Novices learn more from studying worked examples,
   especially paired with practice problems and completion problems, than from unguided problem
   solving. Fade steps as expertise grows (expertise reversal). [E]
4. **Interleave practice.** Most textbooks block practice, with every problem in a set using the
   same method. Interleaving mixes problem types, so students must choose the strategy.
   Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig and Cheung (2020, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, verified) ran
   a randomized classroom trial with 787 seventh-graders. The same problems, reordered to
   interleave, produced 61% versus 38% on an unannounced test a month later (d = 0.83). It is
   one of the cheapest large effects available: reorder problems, not teachers. [E]
5. **Explicit instruction for students who struggle.** The Gersten et al. (2009) meta-analysis
   (section 3) and the Fuchs et al. (2021) IES guide agree on this point: systematic, explicit
   instruction with clear models, precise mathematical language, visual representations
   (especially the number line), explicit teaching of word-problem schemas and timed fluency
   practice. This is not in tension with rich problems. It governs who gets how much guidance
   when. [E]
6. **Where productive failure fits.** Kapur's work (in this course) supports a short, designed
   problem-solving phase before instruction for some conceptual targets, provided students have
   prerequisite knowledge and the consolidation afterwards is explicit. Use it deliberately for a
   few big ideas per unit, not as the default mode. [E for the boundary conditions, H for your
   units]
7. **East Asian approaches are more specific than "mastery."**
   - **Singapore:** concrete-pictorial-abstract progression and the bar model for word problems.
     It uses a small number of topics per year taught deeply, with carefully varied practice.
   - **Japan:** "structured problem solving," in which one rich problem is used in depth, student
     solutions are compared and discussed as a class, and the teacher summarizes explicitly. It
     is refined through lesson study. Stigler and Hiebert's *The Teaching Gap* (1999, verified)
     drew on the TIMSS video study and popularized both the Japanese lesson structure and lesson
     study in the US.
   - **England's "teaching for mastery"** program borrowed from Shanghai and Singapore.
   Trial evidence for transplanting whole packages is modest and mixed [background]; their
   coherence and variation principles fit the mechanisms. [H for adoption, E for
   component mechanisms]
8. **Curriculum materials are a lever, but a weaker one than people hope.** Switching textbooks
   often produces small average effects. Implementation, the teacher's knowledge of the
   materials and time spent on the right practice matter as much as the brand [background]. [H]

**Common founder mistakes**
- Choosing a curriculum by philosophy ("inquiry" or "traditional") rather than checking it
  against the mechanisms (sequence, worked examples, spacing, interleaving, fluency).
- Letting teachers each assemble their own materials. In practice this produces incoherence and
  teacher burnout.
- Tracking students into low-expectation pathways in grade 6, which blocks access to algebra.
- Dropping fluency practice because it looks old-fashioned.

**Read next**
1. IES/WWC, *Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics: Intervention in the Elementary
   Grades* (Fuchs et al., 2021). Verified. It is useful for the core program too, not only for
   intervention.
2. National Mathematics Advisory Panel, *Foundations for Success: Final Report* (US Department of
   Education, 2008). Verified. Read the executive summary for its benchmarks and the fractions
   argument.
3. Rohrer et al. (2020), "A randomized controlled trial of interleaved mathematics practice,"
   *Journal of Educational Psychology* 112(1). Verified. Short, and it will change how you set
   homework.

## 6. Hiring, the teacher labor market and retention

**Why it matters.** Teachers are most of your budget and most of your effect. Wiliam (in this
course) is right that a system cannot hire its way to quality, but a single new school still
has to hire well, and above all keep and grow the people it hires.

**Things to know**

1. **Teachers differ enormously, and it matters for life outcomes.** Chetty, Friedman and
   Rockoff (2014, *American Economic Review*, verified) linked school records to tax data for
   about a million students. A one-standard-deviation better teacher by value-added in a single
   grade raised college attendance at age 20 by about 0.82 percentage points (from a base of
   37%), and also raised earnings and reduced teen births. Individual effects are small, but
   they are large summed across a classroom and a career. [E; the methodology was debated,
   notably by Rothstein, and the authors replied]
2. **Credentials predict weakly.** Kane, Rockoff and Staiger (2008, *Economics of Education
   Review*, verified) found that certification status had small effects on student achievement
   in New York City. Differences within any certification group were large and persistent.
   Early classroom performance was the better predictor. Licensure rules may still bind you
   legally; this changes whom you prefer, not what the law requires. [E; licensure is Jurisdictional]
3. **Screening can work if it samples the job.** Jacob, Rockoff, Taylor, Lindy and Rosen (2018,
   *Journal of Public Economics*, verified) studied Washington DC's applicant pipeline.
   Academic background (such as college GPA) and structured screening scores (written tasks,
   interviews, sample lessons) both predicted later performance. Principals' hiring decisions
   barely used that information. Build a structured process around a sample lesson with a
   feedback-and-reteach round (it tests coachability), a written task on student work, a
   structured interview with scored rubrics, and reference checks that ask specific questions.
   [E for the components, H for your weights]
4. **Early-career data are noisy; decide slowly, develop quickly.** One year of value-added or
   observation scores is unreliable for an individual. Teachers improve fast in their first
   years, and Papay and Kraft (2015, *Journal of Public Economics*, verified) found improvement
   continues well beyond them. Use multiple measures over two or more years for high-stakes
   decisions, and invest heavily in coaching the novices you hire. [E]
5. **Working conditions drive both retention and growth.** Kraft and Papay (2014, *EEPA*,
   verified) found that teachers in schools with more supportive professional environments
   improved about 38% more over ten years than those in less supportive schools (75th versus
   25th percentile). Kraft, Marinell and Yee (2016, *AERJ*, verified), using NYC surveys, found
   that improvements in leadership, academic expectations, teacher relationships and safety each
   predicted lower teacher turnover. Pay matters too, but at the margin the evidence points to
   the principal, collegial support and order in the building. [E]
6. **Turnover is expensive and uneven.** The Learning Policy Institute's *Teacher Turnover: Why
   It Matters and What We Can Do About It* (Carver-Thomas and Darling-Hammond, 2017, verified)
   documents high US attrition and names dissatisfaction with administration as a leading
   reason for leaving. Charter and high-poverty schools typically run higher turnover
   [background]. Each departure costs recruiting, onboarding and lost growth. [E]
7. **The market is local and seasonal.** Shortages cluster in special education, math, science and
   languages, and in particular places. Late-summer hiring is linked to weaker hires and early
   turnover [background]. Offer early, build pipelines (residencies, student teachers, career
   changers), and know local pay scales, pensions and union context. [Jurisdictional]

**Common founder mistakes**
- Hiring for mission fit and charisma without a sample lesson.
- Hiring a founding team of novices and assuming culture will compensate for lack of expertise.
- Relying on salary to retain people when the real problems are leadership and workload.
- No coaching model, so new teachers sink and leave.
- Making tenure or dismissal decisions on a single year's noisy data.

**Read next**
1. Jacob et al. (2018), "Teacher applicant hiring and teacher performance: Evidence from DC
   Public Schools," *Journal of Public Economics* 166 (NBER w22054). Verified. It is the most
   practical paper on what to screen for.
2. Kraft, Marinell and Yee (2016), "School organizational contexts, teacher turnover, and
   student achievement," *AERJ* 53(5). Verified.
3. Chetty, Friedman and Rockoff (2014), "Measuring the Impacts of Teachers II," *AER* 104(9).
   Verified. Read the introduction and conclusion for the stakes.

## 7. Assessment design and measurement basics

**Why it matters.** You will build, buy and report assessments constantly. The course teaches
formative assessment and effect sizes, but not how to judge whether a test measures what you
think, or why numbers go bad once people are rewarded for them.

**Things to know**

1. **Reliability means consistency.** Would the student get a similar result on a different
   day, a different set of items or with a different marker? Every score has a standard error of
   measurement, and reliability rises with more items, clearer mark schemes and multiple raters.
   Short classroom quizzes are unreliable for individuals but fine for spotting class-level
   patterns, which is what formative use needs. [E]
2. **Validity belongs to the inference, not the test.** Ask what decision you will make from
   the score and what evidence supports that use. A spelling test can validly support
   "she knows these 20 words" and not support "she is a strong writer." This inference-based
   view is standard in modern measurement theory (Messick and Kane; the joint AERA/APA/NCME
   *Standards* [background]). [E]
3. **Norm-referenced and criterion-referenced tests answer different questions.** A
   norm-referenced test tells you where a student stands relative to others, as a percentile.
   A criterion-referenced test tells you whether a student has met a defined standard. Growth
   measures and value-added add a third question: how much did the student move compared with
   similar students. Families often confuse these; report them with care. [E]
4. **Standardized tests and curriculum-embedded assessment are complements.** External
   standardized tests are comparable and reliable but distant from your curriculum and slow to
   report. Curriculum-embedded assessments (unit tests, hinge questions, exit tickets) are
   close to instruction but hard to compare. Christodoulou's *Making Good Progress?* (2017,
   verified) argues schools wrongly made one assessment serve both purposes: formative checks
   should be fine-grained and specific, summative judgments need larger, better-built samples. [E for the principle]
5. **Campbell's law and Goodhart's law.** Campbell (1970s): heavy use of an indicator for
   decisions invites gaming and warps the activity it was meant to track. Goodhart (1975, in
   monetary economics; the popular "target" phrasing is Strathern's later gloss): pressure on a
   measure breaks its link to what it measured. In schools the result is score inflation. Koretz (*The Testing Charade*, 2017, verified) documents high-stakes test scores
   rising far faster than learning measured by audit tests: narrowed curriculum, test-format
   drilling, focus on "bubble kids" just below cut scores. Design your internal dashboards so
   that no single number carries high stakes for teachers. Rotate and audit measures. [E]
6. **Small schools have noisy numbers.** With 40 students in a grade, year-to-year swings in
   average scores are mostly noise from who happens to be in the cohort. Do not celebrate or
   panic over single-year changes. Look at multi-year trends, at matched-student growth and at
   confidence intervals. [E]
7. **Build interim assessments that serve learning.** Align them to your curriculum's actual
   sequence. Include enough items per key concept to be informative. Analyze them at the item
   level with teachers in data meetings, and reteach within days. [E
   for alignment and feedback; H for your design]
8. **Report honestly to families.** Say what the score means, how precise it is and what the
   school will do about it. Avoid inflated grades and vague "developing" labels that hide real
   gaps; both betray trust eventually. [V]

**Common founder mistakes**
- Buying an adaptive interim test and treating its "grade-level" labels as precise.
- Using exit tickets for teacher evaluation, which destroys their formative value.
- Reading one year's small-cohort result as proof the model works or fails.
- Grades that measure compliance (homework completion, behavior) and mix it with mastery.

**Read next**
1. Daniel Koretz, *Measuring Up: What Educational Testing Really Tells Us* (Harvard University
   Press, 2008). Verified. A non-mathematical course in measurement, written for policy
   students.
2. Daisy Christodoulou, *Making Good Progress? The Future of Assessment for Learning* (Oxford
   University Press, 2017). Verified. It bridges Wiliam and psychometrics.
3. Daniel Koretz, *The Testing Charade: Pretending to Make Schools Better* (University of
   Chicago Press, 2017). Verified. Read it for Campbell's law in action.

## 8. Behavior, culture and routines

**Why it matters.** Without order, nothing else in the course happens. The working memory you
spent Module 1 protecting is consumed by noise, transitions and social threat. Founders also import strong priors here: "no excuses" versus "restorative." 

**Things to know**

1. **Routines are cognitive-load reducers.** When entry, materials, transitions, turn-taking and
   asking for help are automatic, they cost no working memory or instructional time. Teach them
   like content: model, practice, feedback, re-practice. Bennett: behavior is taught, not told. [E for load logic; H for any given routine]
2. **Consistency across the school beats individual heroics.** The EEF guidance report
   *Improving Behaviour in Schools* (verified) recommends: know your pupils; teach learning
   behaviors, not only sanctions; train classroom management; use simple daily routines
   (greeting at the door, breakfast clubs); use targeted plans such as daily report cards for
   pupils with greater needs; and be consistent school-wide. It found a lack of evidence on zero-tolerance policies. [E]
3. **School-wide positive behavior systems have trial evidence.** School-wide Positive Behavioral
   Interventions and Supports (SWPBIS) sets clear expectations, teaches them, acknowledges them,
   gives graduated responses and uses data. Bradshaw, Mitchell and Leaf (2010, verified), in a
   randomized trial across 37 Maryland elementary schools over five years, found significant
   reductions in suspensions and office discipline referrals. Academic effects were weaker or
   absent. [E for behavior outcomes]
4. **The Good Behavior Game is a well-evidenced classroom tool.** This team-based classroom
   management game was tested in a randomized trial in Baltimore first-grade classrooms (Kellam
   and colleagues, verified). Follow-ups into young adulthood reported reduced conduct problems,
   substance-use disorders and other adverse outcomes, among the strongest long-term effects of
   any school prevention program. [E, with the usual caution about single long-run trials]
5. **Restorative practices: better climate, mixed academics.** The RAND randomized trial in
   Pittsburgh (Augustine et al., 2018, verified) found improved teacher-rated climate, fewer
   suspensions and smaller racial and income gaps in suspensions, especially in elementary
   grades, but no suspension reduction in middle schools and negative effects on middle-grade
   achievement. Restorative approaches can complement clear expectations and consequences, not
   replace them. [E for these results; H for your
   implementation]
6. **Status and dignity are part of behavior design.** Yeager (in this course) shows that
   adolescents are acutely sensitive to status and respect; humiliating or arbitrary systems
   provoke resistance. Aim for the mentor mindset: high standards, high support, reasons given. [E from Yeager, V
   for your culture]
7. **Exclusion is a blunt instrument.** Suspensions and expulsions remove disruption from the
   room but carry costs for the excluded student and raise equity and legal questions,
   especially for students with disabilities, where there are procedural protections.
   [Jurisdictional for rules; V for your thresholds]

**Common founder mistakes**
- Leaving behavior to each teacher's personality.
- Writing a values statement instead of a taught routine.
- Swinging between "no excuses" and "no consequences" when the pendulum of opinion moves.
- Not training every adult, including lunch and bus staff, on the same system.
- No escalation path for the small number of students with intensive needs.

**Read next**
1. EEF, *Improving Behaviour in Schools* guidance report (educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk).
   Verified. Free and short.
2. Tom Bennett, *Running the Room: The Teacher's Guide to Behaviour* (John Catt, 2020).
   Verified. Practical, and framed around teaching behavior explicitly.
3. RAND, *Can Restorative Practices Improve School Climate and Curb Suspensions?* (RR-2840,
   2018). Verified. Read it before adopting any restorative program.

## 9. AI tutoring and ed-tech (evidence status as of late 2026)

**Why it matters.** You come from product; you will be pitched daily and tempted to build. The
course's skepticism tools (Module 8 effect sizes and scale decay, Module 9 PDSA) pay off most
here.

**Things to know**

1. **Human high-dosage tutoring is the benchmark and it is strong.** Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan
   (2020, NBER w27476, verified) meta-analyzed 96 preK-12 tutoring RCTs and found a pooled
   effect of about 0.37 SD [96 is background; 0.37 verified]. Effects were larger with teachers
   or paraprofessionals than with volunteers or parents, larger in earlier grades, and larger
   during the school day than after school. [E]
2. **Effects shrink at scale.** Kraft, Schueler and Falken (2024, EdWorkingPaper 24-1031,
   verified) expanded the analysis to 282 RCTs. Studies resembling large US programs aimed at
   standardized test scores showed effects about a third to a half the size of the full sample,
   with a steep decline as program size increased. Expect around 0.1-0.2 SD at scale, not 0.4,
   and protect the design features (dosage of three or more sessions a week, during school, small
   groups, trained tutors, a consistent tutor) that seem to hold effects up. [E]
3. **The "2 sigma" claim is folklore.** Bloom (1984) reported that tutoring raised achievement
   by two standard deviations. Von Hippel (*Education Next*, 2024, verified) traced the claim.
   The original studies combined tutoring with extra-trained tutors, replacement of regular
   instruction, frequent formative tests with feedback and mastery learning. They were short and
   used narrow tests, and the result has not been replicated. Anyone pitching you "2 sigma via
   AI" is quoting a myth. Separately, VanLehn's 2011 review found that step-based intelligent
   tutoring systems came close to human tutors (both around 0.75-0.8 SD in the studies he
   reviewed) [background]. That was on narrow, researcher-built tests. [E that 2 sigma is not a
   reliable benchmark]
4. **Early generative-AI trials: design is everything.**
   - **Unguarded chatbots can harm learning.** Bastani et al. (*PNAS*, 2025, verified) gave
     nearly 1,000 Turkish high-school math students either plain GPT-4 or a tutor-prompted
     version during practice. Both groups improved during practice. When access was removed,
     the plain-GPT group did about 17% worse on exams than controls; the guarded tutor largely
     avoided the harm. Students used the plain version as a crutch.
   - **Carefully designed AI tutors can do well in controlled settings.** Kestin et al.
     (*Scientific Reports*, 2025, verified) ran a crossover RCT with 194 Harvard intro-physics
     students. A tutor built on the same pedagogy as the class produced larger learning gains in
     less time than an active-learning lecture session. Short, single-topic, college.
   - **An LMIC field trial was promising.** De Simone et al. (World Bank, 2025, verified)
     randomized Nigerian secondary students to six weeks of after-school, teacher-guided GPT-4
     (Copilot) English tutoring: +0.31 SD on the overall assessment and +0.23 SD in English. Short;
     the outcome measure was partly aligned to the intervention.
   - **AI assisting humans can scale expertise.** In a Tutor CoPilot RCT (Wang, Demszky, Loeb et
     al., 2024-25, verified), involving more than 700 tutors and 1,000 students, students' topic
     mastery rose 4 percentage points overall and 9 points for students of lower-rated tutors,
     at roughly $20 per tutor per year.
   - **Real-world deployment of a school AI tutor gave small gains.** Oreopoulos and Low (NBER
     w35620, August 2026, verified) ran a two-year cluster RCT in 18 Tennessee middle schools
     using Khan Academy with Khanmigo during remedial math blocks. They found about 0.06-0.08 SD
     per year (up to about 0.14 SD implied for full active use). That is similar to Khan
     Academy practice without AI. Students rarely engaged the tutor, and when they did it was
     seldom in substantive mathematical dialogue. Secondary reports of a June 2026 Stanford study
     describe similarly low usage (unverified).
   [H overall. The pattern so far: guardrails and pedagogy matter more than the model;
   engagement and usage, not capability, are the binding constraint in schools; AI supporting a
   human tutor or teacher has the cleanest evidence.]
5. **The mechanisms tell you what to demand.** Does the tool withhold answers and make the
   student generate them (Module 1)? Does it give novices worked examples and fade them (Module
   4)? Does it space and interleave? Does the teacher see what students did (Module 6)? Fluent
   answers are not learning.
6. **The most plausible near-term wins are teacher-side.** These include drafting feedback,
   generating varied practice and hinge questions, differentiating texts, and prepping tutors,
   with a human checking output. They save time and lift the median lesson without asking
   children to self-regulate with a chatbot. [H, and the most
   promising one]
7. **Every tool is a PDSA cycle, not a platform decision.** Before a pilot, write down the
   predicted effect on a practical measure (usage minutes, mastery checks, a delayed test), the
   comparison group, the duration and the kill criterion. Check data privacy (student data law;
   in England, KCSIE's pointer to the DfE generative AI product-safety expectations) and age
   limits in vendor terms. [V for the rule, Jurisdictional for privacy]

**Common founder mistakes**
- Designing the school around a product that has no independent RCT.
- Measuring success by engagement or logins instead of delayed learning.
- Giving students open chatbots for homework and then being surprised by exam results.
- Letting a vendor run the evaluation.
- Cutting human tutoring to fund AI when the human-tutoring evidence is far stronger.

**Read next**
1. Kraft, Schueler and Falken (2024), "What Impacts Should We Expect from Tutoring at Scale?"
   (EdWorkingPaper 24-1031). Verified. It recalibrates every tutoring claim you will hear.
2. Paul von Hippel, "Two-Sigma Tutoring: Separating Science Fiction from Science Fact,"
   *Education Next* (2024). Verified.
3. Bastani et al. (2025), "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning," *PNAS* 122(26),
   read alongside Oreopoulos and Low (2026), NBER w35620. Both verified. Together they show the
   two failure modes: harmful use and non-use.

## 10. Family and community engagement

**Why it matters.** Families choose your school, carry its reputation and shape attendance
and homework. Founding families are co-investors taking a risk on you, and community
credibility is a precondition for enrollment.

**Things to know**

1. **Engagement in learning beats engagement in the school.** Parental involvement in general
   (attending events, volunteering) is weakly linked to achievement. Parental engagement in
   learning (conversations about school, reading together, supporting routines, high
   expectations) is more strongly linked. The EEF guidance report *Working with Parents to
   Support Children's Learning* (verified) recommends reviewing how you work with parents,
   giving practical home-learning strategies, tailoring communications toward positive dialogue
   about learning, and sustained support for families who need it. The EEF toolkit puts average impact at about +3 months, from mixed-quality evidence. [E
   for direction, H for magnitude]
2. **Specific, timely information changes behavior cheaply.** Bergman and Chan (2021, *Journal of
   Human Resources*, verified) sent automated weekly texts to parents about missed assignments,
   grades and absences in 22 West Virginia middle and high schools. Course failures fell 27% and
   class attendance rose 12%, at trivial cost, but state test scores did not change. Rogers and
   Feller (2018, *Nature Human Behaviour*, verified) mailed parents of about 28,000 at-risk
   students their child's actual absence totals. Chronic absenteeism fell 10% or more, largely
   because parents had underestimated absences. Tell families concrete facts they do not know,
   regularly. [E]
3. **Teacher-initiated positive contact builds trust.** Early, frequent, positive contact
   (calls in the first weeks, before any problem arises) improves relationships and has shown
   effects on engagement and homework completion in small trials (Kraft and Dougherty, 2013
   [background]). [H to E]
4. **Design communication for the families you have.** Language access, channels families use (often text, not email),
   meeting times that fit shift work, no assumed printer or broadband. [V and practical]
5. **Families are partners in improvement, not only audiences.** Mapp and Kuttner's "Dual
   Capacity-Building Framework" [background] argues that both staff and families need capacity
   for partnership. Use family voice in the improvement system (Module 9 empathy interviews),
   not only in satisfaction surveys. [H]
6. **The community is your enrollment funnel.** Recruit through trusted local messengers (preschools, faith and
   community groups, pediatricians) and be candid about what the school is not; overpromising
   produces October attrition. [H]

**Common founder mistakes**
- Treating family engagement as events and newsletters.
- First contact with a family being a complaint.
- Communicating only in English, or only by email.
- Recruiting with a vision families cannot picture and then losing them to the reality.
- Letting a small group of highly vocal founding parents set policy by default.

**Read next**
1. EEF, *Working with Parents to Support Children's Learning* guidance report. Verified.
2. Bergman and Chan (2021), "Leveraging Parents through Low-Cost Technology," *Journal of Human
   Resources* 56(1). Verified.
3. Rogers and Feller (2018), "Reducing student absences at scale by targeting parents'
   misbeliefs," *Nature Human Behaviour*. Verified.

## 11. Founder leadership and the first 18 months

**Why it matters.** Principals matter nearly as much as teachers, across the whole school. At
first the founder is also fundraiser, operations chief and chief recruiter, and year one sets
norms that are very hard to change later.

**Things to know**

1. **The principal effect is large and school-wide.** Grissom, Egalite and Lindsay's Wallace
   Foundation synthesis (*How Principals Affect Students and Schools*, 2021, verified) reviewed
   219 studies. Moving from an average principal to one at the 75th percentile is associated
   with about three months of additional learning a year in reading and math, across every
   student in the school. Four behavior domains mattered: engaging in instructionally focused
   interactions with teachers, building a productive climate, facilitating collaboration and
   professional learning, and managing personnel and resources strategically. [E]
2. **Instructional leadership is concrete.** Bambrick-Santoyo's *Leverage Leadership 2.0* (2018,
   verified) frames it as a few high-leverage practices: data-driven instruction,
   planning, observation and feedback, professional development, student culture, staff culture
   and managing school leadership teams. Its method is to see it, name it and do it, with
   frequent short observations and practice-based feedback. Practitioner wisdom (Uncommon
   Schools), not trial evidence, but consistent with the coaching literature.
   [H grounded in E]
3. **Guard the founder's calendar as a system.** This is Bryk's "see the system" applied to
   yourself. In year one, operations will eat instruction: lunch, buses, enrollment paperwork,
   compliance reports, a broken boiler. Hire a strong operations lead early and track, weekly, the
   protected share of your time spent in classrooms and with teachers. [H]
4. **A realistic first-18-month arc.**
   - **Months -18 to -6 (planning):** legal form and application or registration; board; budget
     and cash flow; facility; curriculum adoption (reading, math, knowledge-rich content);
     assessment plan; special-education plan; safeguarding policy; community recruiting;
     founding hires.
   - **Months -6 to 0:** hire the full team; run two or more weeks of summer training covering
     routines, curriculum internalization and the behavior system, rehearsed with practice not
     slides; finish enrollment; get SIS, data and compliance systems live; walk through the
     first day minute by minute.
   - **Months 0 to 2:** culture is set in the first six weeks. Teach routines relentlessly,
     screen everyone, run the first interim assessment, and make positive contact with every
     family.
   - **Months 2 to 12:** weekly observation and feedback cycles; data meetings after each
     interim assessment; the first PDSA cycles on one or two practical measures; monthly board
     reporting on academics, finance and enrollment; recruiting for next year starting in
     autumn.
   - **Months 12 to 18:** an honest year-one review against the design brief; revise the brief's
     hypotheses register; handle retention conversations early; prepare for the authorizer's
     first formal review or inspection.
   [H as a template]
5. **Relationships are the job.** With the board, the founder needs transparent reporting,
   including bad news early. With staff, apply Yeager's mentor mindset: high standards, high
   support, reasons given. With families, apply section 10. Bryk et al.'s *Organizing Schools
   for Improvement* (2010) found relational trust to be one of the essential supports that
   distinguished improving Chicago schools [background; this course covers Bryk's improvement
   work]. [E]
6. **Plan for your own replaceability.** A school that depends on one person is fragile. Develop a
   second-in-command, document systems, and have the board hold a succession plan by year three. [V and H]
7. **Scale second.** Do not open a second campus or add grades faster than planned until the
   first school's results, culture and finances are stable across at least two cohorts. Many
   networks that failed grew before they had a proof point [background]. [H]

**Common founder mistakes**
- Founder as hero: doing everything, delegating nothing, burning out by March.
- Spending year one on fundraising and operations while instruction drifts.
- Hiding problems from the board or authorizer.
- Changing the model every term in response to anecdotes, instead of running disciplined PDSA
  cycles.
- Expanding before the first school works.

**Read next**
1. Grissom, Egalite and Lindsay, *How Principals Affect Students and Schools: A Systematic
   Synthesis of Two Decades of Research* (Wallace Foundation, 2021). Verified. Free.
2. Paul Bambrick-Santoyo, *Leverage Leadership 2.0: A Practical Guide to Building Exceptional
   Schools* (Jossey-Bass, 2018). Verified.
3. Bryk, Sebring, Allensworth, Luppescu and Easton, *Organizing Schools for Improvement: Lessons
   from Chicago* (University of Chicago Press, 2010). Background; not re-verified in this pass.
   It is the empirical foundation for the "five essential supports."

## Cross-cutting: the ten questions a founder should be able to answer cold

Answer these with numbers and named sources, or reread the relevant section.

1. What is my per-pupil revenue, all in, and at what enrollment do I break even on operations?
2. What is my cash position in month four of year one at 70% enrollment?
3. Who can close my school, on what criteria, and when is my first formal review?
4. Who is my designated safeguarding lead, and what checks has every adult passed?
5. How will I identify, serve and fund a student with an IEP or EHCP on day one?
6. Which phonics program and screener, and what happens to a K-1 child below benchmark?
7. Which math curriculum, and how does it handle worked examples, interleaving and fluency?
8. What does my hiring process ask a candidate to do, and how will I keep the good ones?
9. Which numbers will I watch weekly, and how have I protected them from Campbell's law?
10. What is my rule for adopting any new ed-tech or AI tool, and what would make me stop?
