# School-design brief: template and guide

The versioned brief the learner writes from Module 0 (v0) to the capstone (v5). It follows the
template in `synthesis/learning-design.md` section 4c. For each of the 11 sections this guide gives:
what the section is for, the tags a reader should expect, prompts to draft from, an example entry
for a hypothetical school, and common failures. The example school is **Riverbend**, a 400-student
K-8 public charter (two classes of about 22 per grade, 30 staff, 55% of families low-income, 18%
English learners, 14% with IEPs). The examples are illustrations of form and tagging, not a
recommended design; your brief should argue its own choices.

## Rules that apply to the whole brief

- **Tag every declarative sentence in sections 3-9** with [E], [H] or [V]. An untagged sentence
  fails capstone rubric criterion 8. Sections 1-2 are tagged inline wherever a design choice is
  made.
- **[E] needs a source slug** (for example `07-embedding-formative-assessment`) and, where the
  finding is contested or its effect size is disputed, a caveat in the same sentence.
- **Split compound claims.** "Retrieval practice daily, 8 minutes" is [E] for retrieval and spacing
  and [H] for the 8 minutes. Tag both.
- **Values are not weaker than evidence; they are different.** Never upgrade a [V] to [E] because a
  book agrees with you. Never downgrade an [E] to [H] because you dislike it.
- **Refer to the learner model.** Sections 4-9 cite the commitments in section 3 by number (L1-L5).
- **Every [H] appears in the open hypotheses register (s10); every [V] in the value commitments
  register (s11).**
- **Length:** v0 is one page; v5 is 3,000-4,000 words.

## Header and changelog format

```
# <School name> - Design Brief v<N>
Date: <yyyy-mm-dd>   Previous version: v<N-1>
Changelog since v<N-1>:
- [s5] Reversed: "projects every afternoon" -> explicit default with PF for 4 concepts.
  Motivated by M4 (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark; Kapur). Tag changed [V] -> [E]/[H].
- [s6] Added hinge-question standard. Motivated by M6 (Wiliam & Leahy). New H-05.
```

Each changelog line names the section, what changed (added / reversed / re-tagged / removed), the
module and author that motivated it, and any register entry it created. Capstone criterion 10 reads
the cumulative changelog from v0 to v5.

## 1. Purpose and community (mostly [V])

**Guidance.** Say who the school serves, what "world-class" means for these children, and how the community takes part in design. This section is mostly value commitments, and that is fine, as long as they are named as values and not dressed up as findings. Define "world-class" through observable outcomes and experiences, not adjectives. State which design layers are open to community codesign (purpose, culture, the periphery, and which knowledge to prioritize where the evidence is silent) and which are mechanisms that go through an evidence review first. This split is the governance rule for tension t04. Leaving it unstated is the most common founding mistake (`08-extraordinary-learning-for-all`).

**Expected tags.** Mostly [V]. Use [E] only for claims about how community ownership affects sustainment. The split between open and closed layers is itself [V], with an [H] that it produces better designs.

**Prompts.**

- Who exactly does the school serve (ages, neighborhood, needs, languages, share of students with disabilities)?
- Write three to five graduate outcomes a stranger could check, spanning intellectual prowess, identity and well-being.
- Which design layers are open to families, students and staff, and which are fixed by evidence? Who decides disputes?
- Who was in the founding design team, and whose voices are missing?
- What would make you say, in year five, that the school had failed its purpose even if test scores were high?

**Example entry (Riverbend, 400-student K-8).**

Riverbend serves 400 children in K-8 on the city's east side. 55% of families qualify for free lunch, 18% of students are learning English, and 14% have an IEP. "World-class" here means four checkable things. Every child leaves grade 8 reading fluently and knowing a broad, coherent body of history, science, literature and the arts [V]. Every child produces work for a real audience at least once a term [V]. At least 85% of students agree "adults here know me and push me" [V]. Every subgroup's results are within five points of the school average, so variation is treated as a design problem [V].

The founding design team (six families, three students, four teachers, two neighbors) owns purpose, culture, the periphery and content choices where the evidence is silent [V]. Mechanisms (phonics, retrieval practice, formative assessment, TLCs) go through an evidence review, and the reasoning is explained to the team in writing rather than put to a vote [V]. Community ownership helps new designs survive (`08-extraordinary-learning-for-all`) [E]. That this split yields better designs is [H], logged as H-09.

**Common failures.**

- Adjectives instead of checkable outcomes ("innovative", "whole child").
- Promising the community it will "design the school" without saying which layers are open.
- Tagging values as [E] because a book agrees with them.

## 2. The three Tuesdays (narrative; tagged inline)

**Guidance.** Describe a February Tuesday for three children at different ages (the default is 7, 12 and 16; a K-8 school uses its youngest, middle and oldest bands, for example 7, 10 and 13). For each child, give one lesson minute by minute, plus a sentence on the rest of the day. This section forces the design down to the median classroom, where Mehta & Fine found depth usually is not (`09-in-search-of-deeper-learning`). Tag inline: each design choice visible in the minute-by-minute carries [E], [H] or [V]. The narrative as a whole is a hypothesis about what your system will produce. Test it in year one by shadowing a student for a whole day.

**Expected tags.** Inline tags on each design choice. Mostly [E] for lesson mechanics, [H] for timings and dosages, [V] for what the day prioritizes.

**Prompts.**

- Pick a real subject and a real unit for each child. What exactly is being learned in that lesson?
- Minute by minute: where is retrieval, where is new content explained, where does the teacher check every student's understanding, and what happens to students who are behind?
- Where in each child's day do they ask their own question, and what happens to it?
- Where in the 12- or 13-year-old's day do they have status, choice or a real audience?
- If a visitor shadowed each child all day, what would they see that contradicts your brochure?

**Example entry (Riverbend, 400-student K-8).**

**Amara, 7 (grade 2), 9:10-9:55, literacy.**
- 9:10: Five minutes of phonics review of the "ai/ay" spelling, chorally then with individual turns, then reading a decodable passage [E].
- 9:15: Read-aloud from the unit "The Ancient Nile." Four words are pre-taught with pictures. The teacher stops twice to ask a why-question, and every student answers on a whiteboard [E for the mechanisms; H for the frequency].
- 9:40: Amara writes one because/but/so sentence about the flood season [E for content-embedded sentence work; H for the specific sequence].
- 9:50: Amara asks "Why did they wrap the bodies?" It goes on the question board and is taken up in Friday's question slot [H].

Rest of her day: math with a concrete-to-abstract number line, 40 minutes of science, and recess.

**Leo, 10 (grade 5), math.** A five-question retrieval starter mixes last month's fractions with this week's. A worked example is paired with a similar problem for students to try. A hinge question goes up on whiteboards: at 80% or better the class moves on, and below that the teacher re-teaches using the most common distractor [E for mechanisms; H for the threshold].

**Noor, 13 (grade 8), science.** She predicts what happens to the particles when a sealed syringe of air is compressed, commits in writing, then watches a simulation. The explanation that follows targets the "particles shrink" misconception (t11) [E]. In the afternoon she runs a design review for grade-6 students in the studio [H].

**Common failures.**

- Describing the showcase elective instead of the median core lesson.
- No point at which the teacher learns what every student understands.
- Leaving the students who are behind out of the narrative.

## 3. Model of the learner (mostly [E])

**Guidance.** Write five commitments about memory, prior knowledge and motivation that the school designs around. Each one must be accurate to the evidence, cite a source slug, and be load-bearing: later sections should point back to it by number ("per L2"). Then list the folk theories the school explicitly rejects. This is the section the capstone rubric checks first. Learning styles, "motivation must come before competence" and "knowledge is obsolete because you can search for it" must not appear, except in the rejected list.

**Expected tags.** Mostly [E], with the source slug for each. Add [H] where a mechanism is robust but the claim that it matters at school scale is untested (for example curiosity spillover, mindset messages).

**Prompts.**

- What is learning, in one sentence a teacher could use to judge a lesson?
- What does the school believe about working memory, long-term memory and prior knowledge, and which source says so?
- What does the school believe about intuitive theories: do they get erased, replaced or outcompeted?
- What does the school believe about motivation (curiosity, competence, status and respect), and which parts are [E] and which [H]?
- Which folk theories does the school reject, and where will staff meet this list (hiring, PD, procurement)?

**Example entry (Riverbend, 400-student K-8).**

- **L1. Learning is a durable change in long-term memory, and working memory is the bottleneck.** New material is taught in small steps, with extraneous load cut (`00-foundational-papers`; `01-how-learning-happens`) [E].
- **L2. What children already know determines what they can learn next,** including intuitive theories. Those theories are outcompeted over years, not erased in a lesson (`02-scienceblind`; `13-learning-scientific-concepts`) [E].
- **L3. Retrieval, spacing and correction build memory. Performance during a lesson is not learning** (`01`; `12-why-we-remember`) [E].
- **L4. Novices need explicit guidance, and guidance fades as expertise grows.** Brief generation before instruction helps for some concepts, under stated conditions (`00`; `04-productive-failure`) [E].
- **L5. Motivation follows competence and respect.** Curiosity needs enough knowledge to notice a gap. Adolescents invest effort where they are treated as capable (`00`, Gruber et al.; `05-10-to-25`) [E for the mechanisms; H that school-wide routines built on them move outcomes].

*Rejected folk theories:* learning styles, the "learning pyramid," "digital natives," motivation-first, knowledge-is-obsolete, and "struggle is always good" (`01`) [E that these are false]. The list is used in interviews and vendor reviews [V].

**Common failures.**

- Listing ten commitments nobody can remember.
- Tagging growth-mindset messaging or curiosity spillover as [E] without a caveat.
- Commitments that no later section depends on.

## 4. Curriculum ([E] for mechanisms, [V] for content choices)

**Guidance.** Sketch the knowledge sequence across grades, the concept progressions (spirals) in science and math, how literacy time is allocated, and what is deliberately excluded. The mechanisms (a coherent, cumulative, prerequisite-mapped sequence, phonics as a separate track, content-embedded writing) are [E]. Which periods, texts and cultures are studied is [V], so log those choices in the value register with who might disagree. Show at least one concept whose progression is designed across three or more grades.

**Expected tags.** [E] for sequencing, knowledge-building, phonics and conceptual prerequisites. [H] for specific sequences and minutes. [V] for content selection and exclusions.

**Prompts.**

- Sketch the sequence for history and science by grade. What does each unit depend on, and what depends on it?
- Choose one hard concept (particle model, natural selection, proportional reasoning) and show its progression across grades, with the intuitive theory it must outcompete.
- How many daily minutes go to phonics, knowledge-building ELA, writing, science and history in K-2 and in 3-5?
- Which representations and vocabulary are standardized across grades?
- What is deliberately excluded, and why?
- Who owns the curriculum: the school, the department or the individual teacher?

**Example entry (Riverbend, 400-student K-8).**

Riverbend adopts one knowledge-building ELA curriculum for K-5. It runs alongside a separate systematic phonics track in K-2 with decodable texts. Leveled texts are not used to ration content (`06-beyond-the-science-of-reading`) [E]. History is taught chronologically twice: an introductory pass in grades 1-4 and a deeper pass in grades 5-8, with each unit naming the earlier units it relies on [E for cumulative sequencing; H for this particular spine]. Which civilizations, texts and figures appear is decided by the teaching team with family input and logged as V-03 [V].

Science is built as four long progressions: matter, energy, forces and interactions, and living systems (`13-learning-scientific-concepts`) [E for learning progressions; H for the specific sequence]. The particle model is introduced in grade 3, formalized in grade 5 before photosynthesis, and revisited in grades 7 and 8 [H]. Its target misconceptions include "particles expand" and "gas has no mass" (`02-scienceblind`) [E].

K-2 daily minutes are 30 of phonics, 45 of knowledge-building ELA, 30 of science or history, and 15 of writing [H]. One fraction model (the number line) is used from grades 3-6 [H].

Excluded: skill-of-the-week comprehension units (`06`) [E], a stand-alone "21st-century skills" course [V/E], and teacher-by-teacher unit selection in the core [V].

**Common failures.**

- "Knowledge-rich" asserted with no actual sequence.
- Presenting content choices as if the science dictated them.
- No conceptual-prerequisite check (photosynthesis before particles).

## 5. Instruction ([E] defaults, [H] parameters)

**Guidance.** Specify the default lesson, the named exceptions and the conditions under which each applies, and the policy on homework and practice. The default is explicit, with guidance fading as expertise grows [E]. Exceptions such as productive failure, the studio, or seminar discussion must state their conditions, and they must be switchable: say what observation or data would send a unit back to the default (tension t01). Unguided discovery is excluded for novices. Show how scaffolding changes between the youngest and oldest students, which makes the expertise-reversal effect visible.

**Expected tags.** [E] for the components of the default lesson and for the exclusion of unguided discovery. [H] for timings, thresholds, how many PF concepts, and homework amounts. [V] for how much open inquiry the school wants at all.

**Prompts.**

- Write the default lesson as five to seven phases, each with its mechanism and the learner-model commitment (L1-L5) it serves.
- Which exceptions exist? For each: the conditions, who approves it, and the switch signal that sends it back to the default.
- How does scaffolding change between grade 1 and grade 8?
- What is the homework and practice policy: purpose, amount, success rate, spacing?
- What does the observation rubric reward, and what does it refuse to reward (visible activity, student preference)?

**Example entry (Riverbend, 400-student K-8).**

**Default lesson** [E for components (`01-how-learning-happens`, Rosenshine); H that one shared model beats teacher-by-teacher variation]:
1. Retrieval starter mixing old and recent material, 5-8 minutes (L3).
2. The learning intention, separated from the task.
3. Explicit explanation with worked examples, and redundant text removed from slides (L1, L4).
4. An all-student check: whiteboards or a hinge question (L2).
5. Guided practice aiming for about 80% success (L5) [H for the threshold].
6. Independent practice.
7. An exit ticket used to plan tomorrow's lesson.

**Exception: productive failure.** Used for up to four designated concepts a year in grades 6-8 math and science. All four conditions must hold: the goal is conceptual, students have relevant prior knowledge, the problem admits several approaches, and a scripted consolidation is protected in the next period (`04-productive-failure`) [E for the conditions; H for the number]. Switch signal: if observed consolidation runs under 20 minutes or delayed transfer falls below the explicit-instruction baseline, the unit reverts (t01).

**Exception: the Friday studio.** It is periphery, with explicit skill clinics and open ideas (s8).

**Scaffolding across ages.** In K-2 nearly everything is modeled first. By grade 8, students attempt problems first in about a fifth of lessons [H].

**Homework.** Retrieval of material already taught, about 10 minutes times the grade number, cumulative, done or not done rather than graded [E for retrieval and spacing; H for the amounts].

**Common failures.**

- "Balanced approach" with no conditions stated.
- Exceptions with no switch signal.
- Rewarding engagement in observations.

## 6. Assessment and feedback ([E] mechanisms, [H] cadence)

**Guidance.** Specify the formative toolkit, the grading policy, the standard every hinge question must meet, how mastery is defined, and how results are reported to families. Grading policy must reflect Butler: no grades on formative work (`07-embedding-formative-assessment`) [E]. Reporting must be honest enough that families know where their child stands. Otherwise families will treat every check as a judgment (t12), as in casebook k03. Define mastery for core concepts as success on transfer and delayed items, not on recall.

**Expected tags.** [E] for comment-only formative feedback, retrieval-as-learning, all-student response and generative items. [H] for cadence, thresholds and the hinge-question bank. [V] for the definition of mastery and reporting choices.

**Prompts.**

- Which formative routines are school-wide in every classroom, and which are teacher choice?
- State the hinge-question standard. What must every hinge question have before it goes in the bank?
- What is graded, when, and how is it reported? Where does effort or work habit appear, if anywhere?
- How is mastery of a core concept defined?
- How often do families hear how their child is doing, in what format, and who codesigned that format?
- Which assessment data never leave the classroom or team (for example, never enter evaluation)?

**Example entry (Riverbend, 400-student K-8).**

**School-wide routines:** no hands up for answering, mini-whiteboards in every room, planned wait time, and a daily exit ticket (`07-embedding-formative-assessment`) [H for the package; the component techniques have partial evidence].

**Hinge-question standard** [H]. Every hinge question must:
- be answerable by every student in under a minute
- have each wrong option map to a named misconception from the unit's register (`02-scienceblind`)
- come with a decision rule written in advance, for example "80% or more correct: move on; otherwise re-teach using the most common distractor"
- be banked with its results each year

**Grading.** Formative work gets comments only, and each comment requires a student action (Butler; Kluger & DeNisi) [E]. Unit tests and end-of-term assessments are graded against standards [V]. A work-habit line is reported separately from achievement [H].

**Mastery.** A core concept counts as mastered only with success on a novel prediction or transfer item and on a re-test at least four weeks later [E that recall and conceptual items diverge; V for the definition].

**Families** get a monthly one-page note per core subject naming the standard, the distance from it, and the next step. The format was codesigned with the family council (`05-10-to-25`) [H/V].

**Common failures.**

- Grades plus comments on formative work.
- Hinge questions with random distractors.
- Reporting so sparse that families revolt (see k03).

## 7. Adults ([E] for TLCs, [H] for hiring criteria)

**Guidance.** Cover hiring, the development plan for each teacher's first three years, the TLC compact, and how adults talk to students. Tension t06 sets the default: develop, don't rely on selection. Pre-hire signals predict effectiveness weakly (`10-creating-the-schools-our-children-need`) [E]. Hiring should look for disciplinary depth and coachability, and the development system has to be designed to fill the rest. Apply the mentor mindset to adults as well as to students.

**Expected tags.** [E] for sustained, collaborative, practice-based PD and the TLC package, and for the weakness of credentials as predictors. [H] for hiring criteria, TLC parameters, and keeping TLCs separate from evaluation. [V] for staff culture commitments.

**Prompts.**

- What does the hiring process observe directly (work samples, a feedback cycle, a disciplinary knowledge task)?
- What does years one to three look like for a new teacher: coaching, curriculum-specific training, observation?
- Write the TLC compact: group size, frequency, duration, agenda, action plans, peer observation, and what never happens (evaluation).
- How do adults speak to students (wise feedback, sincere questions, no public status-lowering)?
- What does the school stop doing to create time for TLCs?
- What would show, after three years, that the development system is not working?

**Example entry (Riverbend, 400-student K-8).**

**Hiring.** A demo lesson followed by one feedback cycle: the candidate re-teaches a segment the next day. Plus a subject-knowledge task and a structured discussion of student work [H; logged as H-01].

**Years one to three.** Curriculum-specific onboarding before August. Fortnightly coaching in year one, monthly in year two. A TLC from week six. No new teacher carries an extra-curricular role in year one [H].

**TLC compact** [E for the package (`07-embedding-formative-assessment`, EEF trial); H for the parameters]:
- groups of nine, meeting monthly for 90 minutes, for two years
- a fixed agenda: an opening retrieval activity, a "how's it going" round in which each teacher reports specifically on their action plan, new learning, and a personal plan for next month
- two peer observations per teacher between meetings, focused on the observed teacher's chosen technique, with cover time provided
- trained facilitators
- nothing said in a TLC enters appraisal [H]

Time for TLCs comes from cutting the weekly staff meeting to twice a month and ending the practice of grading every piece of written work [V].

**Talking to students.** Major feedback carries a sincere standard-and-belief statement plus a revision chance (`05-10-to-25`) [E, modest replication base]. No public behavior charts [V/H].

**Switch signal.** If within-school variation in effectiveness hasn't narrowed after two full years, redesign the TLCs (t06).

**Common failures.**

- "Hire the best" as the whole strategy.
- TLCs with no accountability round, or with evaluators in the room.
- No stated source of time.

## 8. Structure ([H])

**Guidance.** Cover the schedule, grouping, the periphery, and what is kept conventional, with reasons. Tension t13 says spend change capacity on instruction, assessment and adult learning first, and change structure only where a specific instructional need requires it. Name that need each time you depart from convention. The periphery (studio, clubs, arts, debate) must be protected in the timetable and designed with apprenticeship features. Everything here is a hypothesis unless a source directly supports it.

**Expected tags.** Mostly [H]. Use [E] only where evidence speaks directly (for example daily math supporting spacing, or targeted small-group tutoring). Use [V] for protecting the periphery and for detracking commitments.

**Prompts.**

- What does the weekly timetable look like for K-2, 3-5 and 6-8? Where is common planning time?
- How are students grouped, and when and how does grouping change (flexibly, by prior knowledge, temporarily)?
- What is the periphery, who takes part (everyone?), and what apprenticeship features does it have?
- What do you keep conventional on purpose?
- For each structural departure, which instructional need requires it?

**Example entry (Riverbend, 400-student K-8).**

- **Classes.** Two classes of about 22 per grade. K-5 classes are self-contained, with a specialist science teacher from grade 3. Grades 6-8 are departmentalized, with 55-minute periods and math every day, because daily contact supports spaced retrieval [H for the structure; E for spacing].
- **Planning time.** Grade teams share three common planning periods a week, needed for PDSA cycles and hinge-bank work [H].
- **Grouping.** Mixed-attainment classes, with flexible, temporary small groups formed by prior knowledge from exit-ticket data. Groups are re-formed every unit [E against fixed labels; H on specifics]. Tutoring for K-2 students below the decoding benchmark takes place in groups of three, four days a week [E].
- **The periphery.** A Friday studio for grades 3-8 (90 minutes, compulsory for all) with skill clinics, critique, mixed-age mentors and a term exhibition. Protected from test-season cuts [V/H]. Advisory in grades 6-8: 12 students per adult, 20 minutes a day [H].
- **Kept conventional.** Age-graded classes, the standard calendar, and a 7-hour day. None of these blocks our instructional model, so we are not spending change capacity on them [H].

**Common failures.**

- Structural novelty (4-day weeks, all-playlist models) with no instructional need behind it.
- The periphery "when there's time".
- No common planning time for the improvement system.

## 9. Improvement system ([E] method, [V] aim)

**Guidance.** Give the year-one aim, the driver diagram, the PDSA cadence, the practical measures, and how improvement data is kept separate from accountability. The aim is a value choice: which problem matters most this year, and for whom. The method (predicted small tests, practical measures, attention to variation) has a strong track record in other sectors, while the school evidence is case-based (`11-improvement-in-action`). Write the rule for tension t05 into the charter: improvement measures never enter evaluation.

**Expected tags.** [V] for the aim and for keeping improvement data out of evaluation. [E] that high-stakes use corrupts indicators and that aggregates hide gaps. [H] for the specific drivers, change ideas and target numbers.

**Prompts.**

- Write one year-one aim: what, how much, by when, for whom (with subgroups).
- Draw the driver diagram: aim, primary drivers, secondary drivers, change ideas, and a measure at each level.
- Specify the first PDSA cycle: change, prediction, practical measure, balancing measure, duration, and who runs it.
- What is the cadence (weekly huddles, fortnightly cycles, termly driver review)?
- Which measures go to the board, at what aggregation, and which never leave the teams?
- What is the switch signal if practical measures and accountability measures diverge?

**Example entry (Riverbend, 400-student K-8).**

**Aim.** By May of year one, 80% of K-2 students reach the decoding benchmark (fall baseline: 52%). The gap between English learners and other students is halved, from 18 points to 9 [V for the aim; H for the size].

**Primary drivers:**
- the quality of phonics instruction
- the dosage and quality of tutoring
- attendance
- practice at home

**Secondary drivers and change ideas, for example:** a daily five-minute review of sounds previously taught; tutoring groups of three with a fixed routine; a text to families after the second absence.

**Practical measures,** returned to teachers within a week: weekly decoding checks, the share of tutoring sessions delivered, and daily attendance. Balancing measures are teacher prep time and the student-experience "I like reading" item [H].

**First PDSA.** Two K classes try a fixed 10-minute sound-review routine for three weeks. Prediction: accuracy on reviewed sounds rises from 60% to 80%.

**Cadence.** Weekly 20-minute team huddles, a fortnightly cycle review, and a termly driver-diagram review.

**Separation.** The board sees aggregate practical measures monthly and accountability measures twice a year. Class-level practical data stays with the teams and never enters appraisal [V]. High-stakes use corrupts indicators (`11-improvement-in-action`) [E].

**Common failures.**

- Ten aims.
- PDSA templates filled in after the fact, with no written prediction.
- Improvement dashboards shown to evaluators (see k18).

## 10. Open hypotheses register ([H])

**Guidance.** List every [H] in sections 3-9, each with the evidence or PDSA cycle that would resolve it. The register is where the brief admits what it doesn't know, and it drives the improvement agenda. A strong register is specific: each entry has a written prediction, a practical measure, a decision rule, and a review date. Make sure your riskiest bets are there, not just your safest.

**Expected tags.** Every entry is [H]. When an entry is resolved, it moves to [E-local] ("supported in our context") or is retired, with the date and evidence recorded. Local results never become general [E].

**Prompts.**

- Have you found every [H] tag in sections 3-9 and entered it here?
- For each entry, what is the smallest test that could change your mind?
- What would you have to see to drop the idea?
- Which three hypotheses carry the most risk if they are wrong? Are they scheduled first?

**Example entry (Riverbend, 400-student K-8).**

- **H-01.** Response to one feedback cycle at interview predicts year-one growth. *Test:* score every hire at interview, then correlate with year-one observation growth; n is small, so pool data over three hiring years. *Decision:* drop the feedback cycle from interviews if there is no relationship after three years. *Review:* June, annually.
- **H-04.** A weekly question slot doubles student question rates in grades 3-5 within a term. *Test:* PDSA in two classes, with the audit protocol run before and after. *Balancing measure:* scores on curriculum-embedded knowledge checks. *Decision:* spread to all classes if rates at least double and knowledge checks hold.
- **H-06.** PF for four concepts in grades 6-8 improves delayed transfer compared with explicit teaching. *Test:* compare delayed quiz results with the prior year's explicit cohort; check fidelity by observation. *Switch:* revert any unit where consolidation runs under 20 minutes.

**Register format.** One row per hypothesis:

| Field | What goes in it |
|-------|-----------------|
| Id | H-01, H-02, ... (stable; never reused) |
| Hypothesis | One sentence, specific enough to be wrong ("X raises Y by Z for whom") |
| Brief section | Where the [H] tag appears (s3-s9) |
| Why [H] not [E] | Mechanism unproven, parameter untested, evidence from other contexts, or case-only evidence |
| Test | PDSA cycle, comparison, or evidence review; who runs it; duration |
| Prediction | Written before the test, with a number |
| Practical measure | What is counted, how often, returned to whom within how many days |
| Balancing measure | What could get worse (teacher time, well-being, another subject) |
| Decision rule / switch signal | What result leads to adopt, adapt, or abandon |
| Review date | When it is next looked at |
| Status | open / testing / supported locally (E-local) / abandoned, with date and one-line evidence |

**Common failures.**

- An empty or vague register ("we'll see how it goes").
- Hypotheses with no disconfirming result specified.
- Only safe hypotheses listed.

## 11. Value commitments register ([V])

**Guidance.** List every [V] in the brief, with one sentence on who might reasonably disagree and why. The point is not to defend values with evidence. It is to hold them knowingly, to see what they cost, and to prevent values from being laundered as findings. Evidence can inform a value commitment (by revealing its costs) but cannot settle it. At least two of your capstone defense challenges will attack entries here.

**Expected tags.** Every entry is [V]. An entry that cites evidence should say what the evidence informs, not claim that it decides the commitment.

**Prompts.**

- Have you found every [V] in the brief and entered it here?
- For each value: who would reasonably disagree (a real group, not a straw man), and what is their best argument?
- What does the commitment cost (money, time, other goals, some families)?
- Which values conflict with each other, and which wins when they do?

**Example entry (Riverbend, 400-student K-8).**

- **V-01. One common knowledge curriculum for all, with no rationing of content by level.** *Who disagrees:* teachers who see curriculum choice as the core of professional judgment; some families who want the curriculum to center their own heritage more strongly. *Cost:* some teacher attrition and less local responsiveness. *Evidence informs:* knowledge gaps widen inequality (`06-beyond-the-science-of-reading`), but that doesn't settle whose knowledge.
- **V-05. Understanding is assessed; belief is never required.** *Who disagrees:* some science advocates, who want acceptance of evolution and climate science treated as a learning outcome; some religious families, who want alternatives presented. *Cost:* tension on both flanks (see k21).
- **V-07. The Friday studio is protected even in test season.** *Who disagrees:* board members focused on accountability, and teachers of students far behind, who would rather have the time for intervention. *Cost:* 90 minutes a week of core time.

**Register format.** One row per commitment:

| Field | What goes in it |
|-------|-----------------|
| Id | V-01, V-02, ... |
| Commitment | One sentence, stated as a choice ("We choose X over Y") |
| Brief section | Where the [V] tag appears |
| Who might reasonably disagree | A real group, and their best argument in one sentence |
| Cost | What the commitment gives up (money, time, another goal, some families' preferences) |
| Evidence that informs it | What evidence says about its costs or consequences, without claiming to decide it |
| Conflicts with | Other V entries it can collide with, and which wins |
| Owner and review | Who can reopen it, and when |

**Common failures.**

- Values listed with no dissenters ("everyone agrees children matter").
- Values defended with evidence as if evidence settled them.
- A register shorter than the number of [V] tags in the brief.
