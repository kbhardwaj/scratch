## The book in one sentence
Learning is a change in long-term memory that is constrained by a tiny working memory, so instruction for novices should be explicit, well-sequenced, prior-knowledge-aware, heavily practiced, frequently checked, and stripped of popular myths.

## The book in one paragraph
Kirschner (an Open Universiteit Netherlands educational psychologist) and Hendrick (a teacher-researcher) pick roughly 34 "seminal" papers in educational and cognitive psychology and give each one a short, standard treatment: why you should read it, the abstract, what the study did and found, what it means for the classroom, and takeaways (with illustrations by Oliver Caviglioli). The 2nd edition (2024) adds a new opening part on memory and cognition (five chapters) and a closing "lethal mutations" chapter. The arc runs from **how memory works** (working memory, the multi-store model, reconstructive memory, knowledge, metacognition) through **how the brain works for learning** (novice vs. expert, cognitive load, evolutionary primary/secondary knowledge, dual coding), **prerequisites** (prior knowledge, self-regulation, mindset, self-efficacy, attribution), **supports** (scaffolding, tutoring, problem solving, active learning), **the teacher** (sequencing, against pure discovery, direct instruction, formative assessment, feedback, study techniques), **context** (situated learning, cognitive apprenticeship, communities of practice) and **cautionary tales** (learning styles, media, "teaching that kills learning", urban legends, lethal mutations). The stance is unapologetically cognitivist and pro-explicit-instruction for novices; it is the canonical "science of learning" primer used in UK/Dutch/Australian teacher education.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
**"What must be true about any school I build, given how human memory and attention actually work — regardless of my values about what school is for?"**
Use it as the constraint layer. Your institutional choices (schedule, curriculum, assessment, teacher development, technology) can be creative, but they must not violate the mechanisms in Parts 1-5. Read it also with a second, skeptical question: *where does the book's "novices need guidance" frame stop applying* — for experts, for motivation, for curiosity, for adolescence? Other books in the curriculum (Kapur, Engel, Mehta & Fine, Yeager) press on exactly those edges.

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

Standard chapter structure in the book (verified via reviews): why read this, abstract of the paper, the study, what it means in practice, how to use it in the classroom, takeaways, further reading. Below: the study, the finding, why it matters, and the design implication.

### Part 1 — Memory and cognition (new in the 2nd edition)

#### Ch 1 — Working memory
- **Core claims.** Working memory (WM) is the mental workspace where conscious thinking happens. It is small (a handful of meaningful elements), short-lived (seconds without rehearsal), and has partially separate channels for verbal/auditory and visual/spatial information coordinated by an attention-controlling "central executive". Everything a pupil learns has to pass through this bottleneck.
- **Key study.** Baddeley & Hitch (1974) replaced the single "short-term store" with a multi-component model (phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, central executive; Baddeley added the episodic buffer in 2000). Background lineage: Miller (1956) "magical number seven"; Cowan (2001) revised the practical limit down to about four chunks.
- **Why it matters.** WM limits apply to *novel* information only. Information already organized in long-term memory (LTM) can be pulled into WM as a single chunk, which is why experts appear to have large working memories.
- **So-what for a school designer.** Treat attention as the scarcest resource in the building. Instruction, slides, worksheets, classroom routines and even building noise all spend WM. Chunk content, sequence it, remove distractions, and automate basics (letter-sound knowledge, number facts) so WM is freed for higher-order work.

#### Ch 2 — Opening the black box
- **Core claims.** Learning can be modeled as information flowing through stores: sensory register (large, very brief) → attention selects → working/short-term memory → encoding into long-term memory; retrieval brings it back. Behaviorism treated the mind as a black box; the cognitive revolution opened it.
- **Key study.** Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968) "modal model" of memory (bk mapping). It is simplified and superseded in details, but it gives teachers a usable causal model.
- **Why it matters.** It yields the book's working definition of learning: **a change in long-term memory**. If nothing has changed in LTM, nothing has been learned, however busy the lesson looked.
- **So-what.** Your school's quality system should look for evidence of durable change (delayed checks, cumulative assessment), not just in-lesson performance. Attention is the gate: what students don't attend to cannot be learned ("memory is the residue of thought", Willingham).

#### Ch 3 — Ah, yes, I remember it well
- **Core claims.** Memory is reconstructive, not a recording. People fill gaps with what fits their existing schemas, and they distort material that doesn't fit their culture or expectations. Confidence is a poor guide to accuracy.
- **Key study.** Bartlett (1932), "The War of the Ghosts": English participants retelling a Native American folk tale shortened it, dropped unfamiliar elements and rationalized it toward their own cultural norms (bk mapping; the chapter title is taken from a song about two people misremembering the same evening).
- **Why it matters.** Schemas are both the engine of learning (new knowledge attaches to what's there) and the source of misconceptions (new knowledge gets bent to fit what's there).
- **So-what.** Build in retrieval with correction; expect students to "remember" a distorted version; surface prior conceptions before teaching (links to 02-scienceblind and 13-learning-scientific-concepts). Assessment should include items that detect plausible-but-wrong reconstructions.

#### Ch 4 — "What you know, you know"
- **Core claims.** Long-term memory is effectively unlimited and holds different kinds of knowledge: facts and concepts (declarative/semantic), events (episodic) and skills/procedures (procedural). Knowledge that is richly connected and well-organized is easier to retrieve and use. Knowing how is built from knowing that, through practice to automaticity.
- **Key study.** The specific anchor paper could not be verified; the chapter sits in the Tulving (1972) episodic/semantic and Anderson (ACT) declarative/procedural tradition. Treat the mapping as unverified.
- **Why it matters.** "Knowledge" is not the opposite of "skills"; skills such as critical thinking, reading comprehension and problem solving are domain-specific and made of knowledge.
- **So-what.** A knowledge-rich, deliberately sequenced curriculum is not a traditionalist preference but a cognitive requirement (links to 06-beyond-the-science-of-reading). Distinguish in your curriculum between knowledge to be understood and procedures to be automated; they need different practice regimes.

#### Ch 5 — Do you know what you know? Metacognition
- **Core claims.** Metacognition is knowledge about, and regulation of, one's own cognition: knowing what you know, planning, monitoring and evaluating. Learners, especially novices, are often poorly calibrated: they confuse fluency and familiarity with mastery.
- **Key study.** Flavell (1979) coined and framed metacognition (metacognitive knowledge, experiences, goals, strategies) (bk mapping). Related: Bjork's work on illusions of competence; Kruger-Dunning style miscalibration.
- **Why it matters.** Metacognition is not a generic skill that can be taught in isolation; it is largely domain-bound, because you need to know a domain to judge your knowledge of it.
- **So-what.** Teach metacognitive strategies inside subjects, model them aloud, and give students objective calibration evidence (low-stakes quizzes) rather than asking "do you understand?". Don't buy a stand-alone "learning to learn" course and expect transfer.

### Part 2 — How does our brain work?

#### Ch 6 — A novice is not a little expert
- **Core claims.** Experts and novices differ not in general intelligence or reasoning skill but in the amount and organization of domain knowledge. Experts see the deep structure of a problem; novices see surface features. So you cannot turn a novice into an expert by having them *act like* an expert (e.g., "be a scientist", "be a historian").
- **Key study (v).** Chi, Feltovich & Glaser (1981): physics experts sorted problems by underlying principle (conservation of energy, Newton's second law); novices sorted by surface features (inclined planes, springs). Related: de Groot and Chase & Simon (1973) on chess masters' recall of meaningful vs. random positions.
- **Why it matters.** This is the book's master distinction. The epistemology of a discipline (how experts create knowledge) is not the pedagogy for learning it (how novices acquire knowledge). Kirschner's summary phrase: novices need to be taught; experts can inquire.
- **So-what.** Programs that make children "do what experts do" (open inquiry, authentic research, unstructured projects) must be staged: first build the knowledge that makes expert-like work possible. The balance of guidance should shift with expertise (see expertise reversal, below).

#### Ch 7 — Take a load off me (cognitive load theory)
- **Core claims.** Because WM is limited, instruction should minimize load that doesn't contribute to learning. Problem-solving by means-ends analysis (working backward from the goal) consumes WM on searching and leaves little for building schemas, so novices can solve problems yet learn little from doing so.
- **Key study (bk, high confidence).** Sweller (1988), "Cognitive load during problem solving: effects on learning". Consequences developed over the following decades: the **worked example effect** (studying worked solutions beats solving equivalent problems for novices), goal-free problems, the **split-attention** and **redundancy** effects, and the **expertise reversal effect** (what helps novices hurts experts; Kalyuga et al. 2003). Load types: **intrinsic** (complexity of the material relative to the learner's knowledge), **extraneous** (caused by poor design), and, in older formulations, **germane** (effort devoted to schema building; now usually folded into intrinsic).
- **Why it matters.** CLT is the single most consequential design theory in the book. It explains why explicit instruction works for novices and why guidance should fade with expertise.
- **So-what.** Adopt worked examples → faded examples (completion problems) → independent practice as a default progression. Audit materials for split attention (labels away from diagrams), redundancy (reading slide text aloud), and decorative clutter. Build teacher-training around load.

#### Ch 8 — Dancing in the dark
- **Mapping caution.** The anchor study for this chapter could not be verified. Best candidate (bk): Craik & Lockhart (1972) levels of processing. The content below describes that study; if the book anchors this chapter elsewhere, the ideas still belong in this course, since the task brief names Craik & Lockhart as covered.
- **Core claims.** How well something is remembered depends on how deeply it was processed at encoding. Processing for meaning (semantic) produces more durable memory than processing for surface features (appearance, sound). Rote repetition without meaning ("maintenance rehearsal") adds little.
- **Key study.** Craik & Lockhart (1972) framework; Craik & Tulving (1975) experiments: words judged for meaning ("does it fit this sentence?") were recalled far better than words judged for case or rhyme.
- **Why it matters.** Students remember what they think about. Activities should make students think about the meaning of the target content, not about the activity's mechanics (Willingham's "memory is the residue of thought").
- **So-what.** Evaluate every task by asking "what will students actually be thinking about?" A poster about photosynthesis may make them think about fonts. Known critique: "depth" is hard to define independently of the memory it predicts (circularity), and transfer-appropriate processing (Morris, Bransford & Franks 1977) shows the best encoding depends on how memory will be tested.

#### Ch 9 — An evolutionary view of learning
- **Core claims.** Humans evolved to acquire some knowledge without instruction — **biologically primary** knowledge such as spoken language, face recognition, basic social reasoning, basic navigation. Most school knowledge (reading, writing, algebra, chemistry, history) is **biologically secondary**: culturally recent, not acquired by immersion alone, effortful, and usually requiring explicit instruction and motivated practice.
- **Key study (bk, high confidence).** David Geary (2007, 2008) on evolutionary educational psychology; later fused with CLT by Sweller.
- **Why it matters.** It explains why "natural learning" analogies (children learn to talk without lessons, so they can learn to read the same way) fail. It also explains why secondary learning is not intrinsically motivating for many children.
- **So-what.** Don't design school around the assumption that curiosity and immersion will produce literacy and numeracy. Do harness primary abilities (social learning, storytelling, play, imitation) as vehicles for secondary content. Critique to carry: the primary/secondary boundary is a theoretical claim with limited direct evidence; see 03-intellectual-lives-of-children (Engel) for a curiosity-first counterweight.

#### Ch 10 — One picture and one thousand words (dual coding)
- **Core claims.** The mind has partially separate systems for verbal and non-verbal (imagistic) information. Information encoded both ways has two routes to retrieval and is remembered better. Concrete words that evoke images are remembered better than abstract ones.
- **Key study (bk, high confidence).** Allan Paivio's dual coding theory (1971; 1986). The applied descendant is Richard Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning: people learn better from words plus pictures than words alone (multimedia principle), with pictures placed near relevant text (spatial contiguity), with spoken rather than written narration for animations (modality), and without extraneous decoration (coherence).
- **Why it matters.** Dual coding is not learning styles: *all* learners benefit from well-designed combinations of words and visuals; no learner benefits from a mode matched to their "style".
- **So-what.** Standardize visual formats (timelines, diagrams, concept maps, graphic organizers) across the school so students aren't re-learning the representation every lesson. Train teachers to draw while explaining, not to read dense slides aloud.

### Part 3 — Prerequisites for learning

#### Ch 11 — What you know determines what you learn
- **Core claims.** The most important single factor influencing learning is what the learner already knows. Meaningful learning happens when new material is anchored to relevant existing ideas; rote learning happens when it isn't. Teachers should find out what students know and teach accordingly.
- **Key study (v).** David Ausubel (1960; 1968). His 1960 experiment showed an **advance organizer** (a brief, higher-level introductory framework) improved learning of an unfamiliar text (on the metallurgy of steel). Two organizer types: expository (for new material) and comparative (relating new to familiar).
- **Why it matters.** This is the prior-knowledge principle underpinning every "knowledge-rich" argument. It also counters a misreading of Ausubel: he supported **meaningful reception learning** (being told, well) over discovery, but he was firmly against rote learning.
- **So-what.** Pre-assess prior knowledge; open units with organizers; sequence curriculum so each unit builds anchoring ideas for the next; treat gaps in foundational knowledge as the first thing to fix, not a sideshow.

#### Ch 12 — Why independent learning is not a good way to become an independent learner
- **Mapping caution.** Title verified; anchor paper not verified (bk candidates: Kirschner & van Merriënboer 2013, "Do learners really know best?", and the Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006 line of argument).
- **Core claims.** Self-regulated, self-directed learning is a legitimate *goal* of schooling but a poor *method* for novices. Learners left to choose tasks, pace and resources tend to choose badly: they pick what feels fluent, avoid effortful practice, and misjudge their learning. Self-regulation itself has to be taught, modeled and scaffolded, and it depends on domain knowledge.
- **Key evidence.** Research on learner control and self-regulated learning (e.g., Zimmerman's phases of forethought, performance, reflection) shows effective self-regulation is built gradually through guided practice; also the "digital native" and "learner knows best" myths.
- **So-what.** A school that promises independent learners should plan a gradient: high teacher control early, explicit teaching of planning/monitoring strategies, gradually increasing choice. "Independent study time" for young or novice learners is a design risk, not a virtue signal.

#### Ch 13 — Beliefs about intelligence can affect intelligence
- **Core claims.** Students hold implicit theories about intelligence: fixed ("entity") or malleable ("incremental"). These theories shape goals (performance vs. learning), responses to failure (helpless vs. mastery-oriented) and effort.
- **Key study (v).** Dweck & Leggett (1988), "A social-cognitive approach to motivation and personality". Follow-ons: Mueller & Dweck (1998) praise for intelligence vs. effort; Blackwell, Trzesniewski & Dweck (2007).
- **Why it matters and limits.** Growth mindset became an education fad; large replications and meta-analyses (e.g., Sisk et al. 2018) show small average effects, concentrated among lower-achieving or at-risk students; the large US National Study of Learning Mindsets (Yeager et al. 2019, Nature) found a small but real effect from a brief online intervention, bigger in schools whose peer norms supported challenge. The authors present the ideas while cautioning against poster-and-assembly implementations.
- **So-what.** Don't run mindset as a slogan. Embed it in structures: praise process and strategy, not trait; design assessment that makes improvement visible; normalize error in feedback culture. See 05-10-to-25 (Yeager) for the adolescent version and the "wise intervention" logic.

#### Ch 14 — ...thinking makes it so (self-efficacy)
- **Mapping caution.** Self-efficacy is confirmed as a topic of the book; the exact chapter title and anchor are background knowledge.
- **Core claims.** Self-efficacy is a person's belief that they can succeed at a specific task. It predicts choice of activities, persistence and effort. It is task-specific, not global self-esteem.
- **Key study (bk).** Bandura (1977), "Self-efficacy: toward a unifying theory of behavioral change". Four sources: **mastery experiences** (strongest), **vicarious experience** (seeing similar others succeed), **verbal persuasion**, and **physiological/emotional states**.
- **Why it matters.** Motivation follows success more reliably than success follows motivation. The best route to confident students is making them actually competent, with carefully sequenced success.
- **So-what.** Design for high success rates in practice (Rosenshine suggests around 80%); use peer models; avoid empty praise, which students see through. Connect with Weiner (next) on how students explain outcomes.

#### Ch 15 — How you think about achievement is more important than the achievement itself
- **Core claims.** How students explain success and failure (their attributions) shapes future motivation. Attributions vary on three dimensions: **locus** (internal/external), **stability** (stable/unstable), **controllability**. Attributing failure to stable, uncontrollable causes ("I'm not a maths person") produces helplessness; attributing it to controllable, unstable causes (effort, strategy) sustains persistence.
- **Key study (v).** Bernard Weiner (1985; 1986) attribution theory of achievement motivation and emotion.
- **Why it matters.** Teacher behavior sends attributional signals: unsolicited help, excessive sympathy for failure, or easy praise can signal "you're low ability".
- **So-what.** Train teachers in attributional feedback ("you haven't got the strategy yet; try X"). Beware of ability grouping signals that fix attributions. Report progress in ways that attribute change to controllable causes.

### Part 4 — Which learning activities support learning?

#### Ch 17 — Why scaffolding is not as easy as it looks
- **Core claims.** Scaffolding is temporary, adjustable support from a more knowledgeable other that lets a learner complete a task they couldn't manage alone, and which is **faded** as competence grows. It is contingent: it depends on continuous diagnosis of what the learner can do. Much of what gets labeled scaffolding (a worksheet with hints, permanent sentence starters) is really just support that never fades.
- **Key study (bk, high confidence).** Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976), "The role of tutoring in problem solving": tutors helping 3-5-year-olds build a pyramid of blocks. They named six functions: recruiting interest, reducing degrees of freedom, maintaining direction, marking critical features, controlling frustration, demonstrating. Conceptually linked to Vygotsky's **zone of proximal development** (what a learner can do with help but not yet alone), although Vygotsky himself didn't use the term "scaffolding". Van de Pol, Volman & Beishuizen (2010) reviewed classroom scaffolding: contingency, fading and transfer of responsibility are the essential features. Palincsar & Brown (1984) reciprocal teaching (teacher models four comprehension strategies — questioning, clarifying, summarizing, predicting — then hands roles to students) is the classic classroom example of scaffolding with fading.
- **Why it matters.** Scaffolding is hard to do at whole-class scale because it's contingent on the individual.
- **So-what.** Build explicit **fading plans** into curriculum materials (I do / we do / you do; worked → completion → independent). Train teachers in diagnosis (checking for understanding) rather than in scaffolding "tools". Use reciprocal teaching in literacy.

#### Ch 18 — The holy grail: whole class teaching and one-to-one tutoring
- **Core claims.** One-to-one tutoring combined with mastery learning produced dramatically better outcomes than conventional whole-class teaching. The challenge is finding group-teaching methods that approach tutoring's effectiveness at scale.
- **Key study (v).** Bloom (1984), "The 2 sigma problem": students tutored one-to-one with mastery learning performed about two standard deviations above conventionally taught students (average tutored student above ~98% of the control group); mastery learning in groups gave roughly one sigma. Bloom listed candidate variables (feedback-correctives, cues, time on task, prior knowledge enhancement, higher-order questions).
- **Limits.** The 2-sigma figure came from small studies by Bloom's doctoral students (Anania; Burke) with short durations and closely aligned tests; modern meta-analyses of tutoring find substantial but smaller effects (often 0.3-0.4 SD for high-dosage tutoring, e.g., Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan 2020).
- **So-what.** Treat Bloom as a design target not a promise. The ingredients to scale are: mastery criteria before moving on, frequent formative checks, corrective re-teaching, and targeted small-group or high-dosage tutoring for students who fall behind. This is also the charter for AI tutoring claims; hold vendors to real effect sizes.

#### Ch 19 — Problem-solving: how to find a needle in a haystack
- **Mapping caution.** Title verified; anchor study not verified (bk candidates: the worked-example and means-ends literature, e.g., Sweller & Cooper 1985; Newell & Simon 1972).
- **Core claims.** Problem-solving is not a general, trainable skill; it is domain-specific and depends on schemas stored in long-term memory. General heuristics (e.g., "understand the problem, devise a plan") add little without domain knowledge. Novices searching a problem space are like searching a haystack; knowledge gives you a magnet.
- **Key study.** Sweller & Cooper (1985) showed algebra students who studied worked examples outperformed those who solved equivalent problems, and took less time. Newell & Simon's (1972) problem-space theory describes means-ends search.
- **So-what.** Teach problem-solving through worked examples and comparison of problem types within subjects; interleave problem types once basics are secure so students learn to choose a strategy. Be skeptical of generic "problem-solving skills" courses. Tension: 04-productive-failure (Kapur) argues that, for conceptual understanding, well-designed problem-solving *before* instruction can outperform instruction-first. Hold both.

#### Ch 20 — Activities that give birth to learning
- **Core claims.** "Active learning" is ambiguous. Chi distinguishes observable engagement modes: **Passive** (receiving), **Active** (manipulating: underlining, copying, repeating), **Constructive** (generating beyond the given: explaining, self-explaining, drawing concept maps, asking questions), **Interactive** (co-constructing with a partner who contributes substantively). Learning tends to increase Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive (ICAP).
- **Key study (bk, high confidence).** Chi (2009), "Active-constructive-interactive: a conceptual framework for differentiating learning activities"; Chi & Wylie (2014) ICAP. Earlier Chi work on **self-explanation** (Chi et al. 1989; 1994): students who explained worked examples to themselves learned more.
- **Why it matters.** Physical activity isn't cognitive activity. A lecture where students must self-explain can be more "active" than a hands-on activity where they follow instructions.
- **So-what.** Ask of every task: what will students *generate*? Build self-explanation prompts, "explain it to your partner" routines, and structured discussion into lesson templates. Note the constructive mode can be done within explicit instruction; this reconciles "active learning" and "direct instruction".

### Part 5 — The teacher

#### Ch 21 — Zooming out to zoom in
- **Mapping caution.** Anchor (bk, medium confidence): Reigeluth & Stein (1983) elaboration theory; the theory's own metaphor is a zoom lens.
- **Core claims.** Sequence instruction from simple, general overviews ("epitome") to more detailed and complex elaborations, returning periodically to the big picture to show how parts fit (zoom out, zoom in, zoom out). Organize by a single orientation (concepts, principles, or procedures), use prerequisite sequencing, summarizers and synthesizers.
- **Why it matters.** It's a practical answer to Ausubel's prior-knowledge principle and a counter to fragmented curricula.
- **So-what.** Curriculum maps should show the "big picture" spine per subject and how each unit elaborates it; units should open with a zoomed-out overview and close with synthesis. Related: van Merriënboer's 4C/ID (whole-task, simple-to-complex task classes) is the modern successor.

#### Ch 22 — Why discovery learning is a bad way to discover things
- **Core claims.** Pure (unguided) discovery repeatedly fails to produce learning compared with guided discovery or explicit instruction. Constructivism as a theory of learning (learners build their own knowledge) doesn't imply constructivism as a teaching method (learners should be left to discover it). Cognitive activity, not behavioral activity, drives learning.
- **Key study (bk, high confidence).** Mayer (2004), "Should there be a three-strikes rule against pure discovery learning?": reviews three waves of research (problem-solving rules in the 1960s, conservation strategies in the 1970s, programming concepts in the 1980s) where pure discovery lost to guided methods. Companion: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006), "Why minimal guidance during instruction does not work", which critiques discovery, problem-based, experiential and inquiry learning on CLT grounds; and Alfieri et al. (2011) meta-analysis (unassisted discovery < explicit instruction; enhanced/guided discovery > other methods).
- **Why it matters.** It is the book's most contested claim in the wider field. Critics (Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn 2007) argued that PBL and inquiry as actually practiced are heavily scaffolded, so KSC attacked a straw man. Kirschner & Hendrick's position: guidance must be substantial for novices, whatever the label.
- **So-what.** Inquiry and projects are allowed in your school, but as **guided** inquiry with explicit instruction of prerequisite knowledge, worked examples, and structured checkpoints; open inquiry reserved for students with enough domain knowledge. See 09-in-search-of-deeper-learning and 04-productive-failure for the counter-arguments you'll need to reconcile.

#### Ch 23 — Direct instruction
- **Core claims.** Effective teachers share a set of observable practices that research from three strands (cognitive science, observations of master teachers, and experimental studies of cognitive supports) converged on. Direct instruction (lower-case "d i") is not lecturing; it's highly interactive, explicit, sequenced, heavily practiced and continuously checked.
- **Key study (v).** Rosenshine (2012), "Principles of Instruction: research-based strategies that all teachers should know" (American Educator), the 10-principle version: (1) begin with a short review of previous learning; (2) present new material in small steps with student practice after each step; (3) ask a large number of questions and check responses of all students; (4) provide models (worked examples); (5) guide student practice; (6) check for student understanding; (7) obtain a high success rate; (8) provide scaffolds for difficult tasks; (9) require and monitor independent practice; (10) engage students in weekly and monthly review. Distinguish from Engelmann's scripted Direct Instruction (capital DI; Project Follow Through).
- **Why it matters.** Rosenshine is the most implementable single document in the book; it's become a de facto instructional framework in many UK/Australian schools.
- **So-what.** Use Rosenshine as the backbone of a common instructional model and teacher observation language — but not as a checklist for grading lessons (lethal mutation risk; see ch. 35). Build daily review and cumulative weekly/monthly review into the timetable and curriculum materials.

#### Ch 24 — Assessment for, not of learning
- **Core claims.** Formative assessment — using evidence of student learning to adapt teaching and to guide learners — produces substantial learning gains, especially for lower attainers. The key is that the information is **used** to change what happens next.
- **Key study (v).** Black & Wiliam (1998), "Assessment and classroom learning" (Assessment in Education) — a review of ~250 studies — and its practitioner version "Inside the black box". They reported effect sizes typically in the 0.4-0.7 range. Later, Wiliam defined five key strategies: clarifying learning intentions and success criteria; engineering effective discussions and tasks that elicit evidence; feedback that moves learning forward; activating students as resources for one another; activating students as owners of their learning.
- **Limits.** The 0.4-0.7 figures have been criticized (Bennett 2011; Kingston & Nash 2011 found smaller average effects, around 0.2) because "formative assessment" is defined too loosely and implementation quality varies hugely.
- **So-what.** Make formative assessment the operating system of classrooms (hinge questions, mini-whiteboards, exit tickets, no-hands-up questioning) and of teacher development. Separate formative data from grading/accountability, or teachers will teach to the dashboard. Deep dive: 07-embedding-formative-assessment and 10-creating-the-schools-our-children-need.

#### Ch 25 — Feed up, feedback, feed forward
- **Core claims.** Feedback is powerful but variable: a sizable share of feedback interventions make performance worse. Good feedback closes the gap between current and desired performance by answering three questions: **Where am I going?** (feed up — goals), **How am I going?** (feedback — progress), **Where to next?** (feed forward). It operates at four levels: **task** (correct/incorrect), **process** (strategies), **self-regulation** (monitoring), and **self** (praise about the person) — the last is least effective.
- **Key study (v).** Hattie & Timperley (2007), "The power of feedback" (Review of Educational Research). Background: Kluger & DeNisi (1996) meta-analysis — about a third of feedback interventions decreased performance, especially when feedback drew attention to the self. The book (per a verified summary) frames feedback on a continuum from corrective (right/wrong) to directive (how to do it) to epistemic (prompting the student to think about doing it better).
- **So-what.** Feedback should be more work for the recipient than the giver (Wiliam). Replace heavy written marking with whole-class feedback, re-do tasks and process-level comments; ban grade-plus-comment combinations for formative work (grades crowd out comments, per Butler 1988). Tie feedback to clear success criteria.

#### Ch 26 — Learning techniques that really work
- **Core claims.** Of ten common study techniques, two had high utility (**practice testing / retrieval practice** and **distributed / spaced practice**), three moderate (**elaborative interrogation**, **self-explanation**, **interleaved practice**), and five low (**summarization**, **highlighting/underlining**, **keyword mnemonic**, **imagery for text**, **rereading**). Students mostly use the low-utility ones because they feel fluent.
- **Key study (v).** Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), "Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques" (Psychological Science in the Public Interest). Related: Roediger & Karpicke (2006) testing effect; Cepeda et al. (2006) spacing; Rohrer & Taylor (2007) interleaving; Bjork & Bjork on **desirable difficulties** — conditions that slow apparent progress but improve long-term retention and transfer (spacing, interleaving, retrieval, variation).
- **Why it matters.** **Learning vs. performance** is the core insight: what makes practice look good now (massed, blocked, rereading) often makes retention worse; what makes practice feel hard (retrieval, spacing) makes learning last. Teachers and students systematically mistake one for the other (Soderstrom & Bjork 2015).
- **So-what.** Build spacing and retrieval into the **institution**, not just lessons: cumulative curricula, low-stakes quizzing, interleaved homework, spaced revision calendars, and assessments that test previously-taught material. Teach students about these techniques explicitly — they won't adopt them on their own.

### Part 6 — Learning in context

#### Ch 27 — Why context is everything
- **Mapping caution.** Title verified; anchor unverified (bk candidates: situated cognition, Brown, Collins & Duguid 1989; or research on transfer and context-dependent memory).
- **Core claims.** Knowledge is partly bound to the context in which it was learned; transfer to new contexts is difficult and not automatic. Learning in varied contexts and explicitly abstracting the principle (comparing cases) supports transfer. Classic evidence: Godden & Baddeley (1975) divers recalled word lists better in the environment where they learned them; Gick & Holyoak (1980) showed people rarely transfer an analogous solution unless prompted or given multiple analogs.
- **So-what.** Don't assume a skill learned in one subject will show up in another. Plan transfer: vary examples, compare cases, name the underlying principle, revisit ideas in new contexts across the curriculum.

#### Ch 28 — The culture of learning
- **Core claims.** Knowledge is situated in the activity, context and culture in which it is used; school often teaches abstract, decontextualized knowledge that students can't use. Learning is enculturation into the practices of a community.
- **Key study (bk, medium confidence).** Brown, Collins & Duguid (1989), "Situated cognition and the culture of learning" (Educational Researcher). Critiqued by Anderson, Reder & Simon (1996, "Situated learning and education"), who argued many situated claims were overstated and that abstract instruction does transfer when well designed.
- **So-what.** Authentic tasks and disciplinary practices matter for meaning and motivation, but as the *destination* of a knowledge-building sequence rather than a replacement for it. The book's framing reconciles situated learning with explicit instruction through apprenticeship (next chapter).

#### Ch 29 — Making things visible
- **Core claims.** Traditional apprenticeship worked because the craft was visible; in academic domains the expert's thinking is invisible. Cognitive apprenticeship makes thinking visible through: **modeling** (thinking aloud), **coaching**, **scaffolding** and **fading**, then **articulation** (students explain their reasoning), **reflection** (compare their process with experts'), and **exploration** (pose and solve new problems). Sequencing: increasing complexity, increasing diversity, global before local skills.
- **Key study (bk, high confidence).** Collins, Brown & Newman (1989) and the teacher-facing Collins, Brown & Holum (1991), "Cognitive apprenticeship: making thinking visible" (American Educator). Exemplars: Palincsar & Brown's reciprocal teaching (reading), Scardamalia & Bereiter's procedural facilitation (writing), Schoenfeld's heuristic modeling (mathematics).
- **So-what.** This is the bridge between explicit instruction and authentic practice. Make think-aloud modeling, articulation and gradual transfer of responsibility required parts of the school's instructional model, in every subject.

#### Ch 30 — It takes a community to save $100 million
- **Core claims.** Much professional knowledge is tacit and lives in **communities of practice** — groups who share a domain, a practice and relationships — rather than in manuals. Learning is increasing participation in such communities ("legitimate peripheral participation").
- **Key study (bk, medium confidence).** Lave & Wenger (1991) situated learning; Wenger (1998) communities of practice. The title refers to the widely told Xerox story: ethnographer Julian Orr found copier technicians learned repairs by sharing stories; Xerox's Eureka knowledge-sharing system built on this is credited with roughly $100 million in savings.
- **So-what for a school designer (this one is institutional).** Teacher expertise will grow mainly in structured professional communities: co-planning, lesson study, instructional coaching, shared curriculum and joint review of student work. Budget time for this (links to 11-improvement-in-action on networked improvement communities and 10-creating-the-schools-our-children-need on teacher learning communities).

### Part 7 — Cautionary tales

#### Ch 31 — Did you hear the one about the kinaesthetic learner...?
- **Core claims.** The "meshing hypothesis" (matching instruction to a learner's preferred style, e.g., visual/auditory/kinesthetic, improves learning) has no adequate support. People have preferences, but teaching to them doesn't improve outcomes; content, not learner, should determine the mode.
- **Key study (bk, high confidence).** Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008), "Learning styles: concepts and evidence" (Psychological Science in the Public Interest): specified the crossover design needed to prove meshing and found virtually no studies met it; those that did contradicted it. Coffield et al. (2004) reviewed 71 style models.
- **So-what.** Ban learning-styles labeling in planning, reports and training. Offer instead: dual coding for all, and differentiation by prior knowledge. Use this as a hiring/training litmus test.

#### Ch 32 — When teaching kills learning
- **Core claims.** Some instruction actively harms learning ("mathemathantic" — Clark's coinage meaning roughly "learning-killing"). Common patterns: giving high-ability or knowledgeable learners heavy, structured guidance they don't need (interfering with their working methods), giving novices unstructured discovery they can't handle, and letting students choose methods they enjoy but learn less from. Students' liking of a method often inversely predicts its effectiveness.
- **Key study (bk, high confidence).** Clark (1989), "When teaching kills learning: research on mathemathantics". It is an early statement of what CLT later called the expertise reversal effect.
- **So-what.** Differentiate guidance by prior knowledge, not by ability labels or preference. Monitor effects, not satisfaction: student enjoyment surveys are not learning evidence.

#### Ch 33 — The medium is NOT the message
- **Core claims.** Media (TV, computer, video, tablet) are delivery vehicles; they don't cause learning, any more than a grocery truck causes nutrition. Gains attributed to media usually come from the instructional method, novelty or better-designed materials that came with them. Instructional method is what matters.
- **Key study (bk, high confidence).** Clark (1983), "Reconsidering research on learning from media" (Review of Educational Research), and the Clark (1994) vs. Kozma (1994) debate; Kozma argued some media afford unique methods (simulations), Clark replied that the method could be delivered other ways.
- **So-what.** Buy technology to reduce cost, increase access or enable a specific method (spaced retrieval, simulation, adaptive practice), never because a medium is "engaging". Require any EdTech/AI procurement to name the instructional mechanism and a plausible effect size. Highly relevant to AI tutors.

#### Ch 34 — The ten deadly sins of education
- **Content caution.** Title verified; the list's exact contents are background knowledge from the authors' other writing and should be checked before quoting.
- **Core claims.** A collection of persistent myths and bad practices, drawing on Kirschner & van Merriënboer (2013) "Do learners really know best? Urban legends in education" and De Bruyckere, Kirschner & Hulshof (2015) *Urban Myths about Learning and Education*. Typical members: learning styles; "digital natives" who can multitask and learn differently; learners know best / learner control; the learning pyramid (the fabricated "we remember 10% of what we read..." figures); discovery learning for novices; teaching generic 21st-century skills without knowledge; multitasking; left/right brain; "you can just look it up"; neuromyths generally.
- **So-what.** Build a "myth firewall" into the institution: a shared glossary of debunked ideas used in hiring, professional development, procurement and parent communication.

#### Ch 35 — Lethal mutations: the dirty dozen (new in the 2nd edition)
- **Content caution.** Title verified; the twelve items below are an informed reconstruction of the argument, not a verified list.
- **Core claims.** A "lethal mutation" (term from Ann Brown & Joseph Campione) is when a research-based practice is adapted in implementation in a way that removes its active ingredient, so it no longer works or backfires. Examples the authors' argument covers (bk): retrieval practice turned into high-stakes testing or copying from notes; Rosenshine's principles used as a lesson-observation checklist; cognitive load misread as "make it easy"; worked examples without fading; formative assessment turned into data-tracking spreadsheets; growth mindset as posters; dual coding as decorative icons; feedback as marking policy; scaffolds that never fade; "direct instruction" as long lecture; interleaving before basics; desirable difficulties misread as "difficulty is good" (difficulties are only desirable if the learner can respond successfully).
- **Why it matters.** This is the institutional chapter of the book: most harm comes not from bad ideas but from good ideas implemented without understanding of mechanism.
- **So-what.** For every adopted practice, write down its **active ingredient** and the most likely mutation; train and observe for mechanism, not surface compliance. This is exactly the problem addressed by 11-improvement-in-action (disciplined improvement) and 07-embedding-formative-assessment (tight but loose).

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. **Learning is a change in long-term memory, and the gateway is a tiny working memory.** Every design decision — from lesson structure to building noise to slide design — either spends or conserves working memory for the thing to be learned.
2. **What you already know determines what you can learn next (Ausubel; Chi; Bartlett).** Knowledge is the engine of comprehension, problem-solving and critical thinking; curriculum must be sequenced and cumulative, and prior knowledge must be diagnosed.
3. **Novices are not little experts.** Instruct novices explicitly (worked examples, modeling, guided practice) and fade guidance as expertise grows; open inquiry is for those who know enough to use it (Sweller; Mayer; Kirschner, Sweller & Clark; expertise reversal).
4. **Learning is not performance.** Techniques that feel productive now (rereading, massing, blocking) are weak; techniques that feel hard (retrieval, spacing, interleaving) are strong (Dunlosky; Bjork). Make retrieval and spacing structural.
5. **Evidence of learning must be elicited and used continuously.** Formative assessment and feedback that moves the learner forward (Black & Wiliam; Hattie & Timperley) are among the most powerful and most frequently mutated practices.
6. **Rosenshine's principles are a usable common instructional model**, provided they're treated as mechanisms, not a checklist: review, small steps, many questions to all, models, guided practice, checks, high success, scaffolds, independent practice, spaced review.
7. **Motivation is largely downstream of competence and interpretation.** Self-efficacy grows from mastery experiences; attributions and mindsets shape persistence; engineer success and signal controllable causes.
8. **Being cognitively active matters more than being physically active (ICAP).** Aim for generative (constructive) and interactive thinking, which can happen inside explicit teaching.
9. **Mode, media and style are not mechanisms.** Learning styles are a myth; technology does not teach by itself; method does.
10. **Good practices die by mutation.** The implementation question — how do we preserve the active ingredient at scale? — is the central problem for a school, not the choice of practices.

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **Working memory** — limited, short-lived conscious workspace — bites in explanation design, slide design, multitasking tasks.
- **Long-term memory / schema** — organized, effectively unlimited knowledge store; schemas are chunks that act as single units in WM — bites in curriculum sequencing and why knowledge makes thinking easier.
- **Chunking** — grouping elements into meaningful units — bites in fluency drills and automating basics.
- **Cognitive load (intrinsic / extraneous / germane)** — load from the material's complexity vs. from poor design vs. from schema building — bites in material audits and teacher training.
- **Worked example effect** — studying solutions beats solving for novices — bites in maths and science lesson design.
- **Expertise reversal effect** — supports that help novices hurt experts — bites in differentiation and in advancing students.
- **Split-attention / redundancy / modality** — Mayer/Sweller design effects — bite in slides, worksheets and video.
- **Biologically primary vs. secondary knowledge** — evolved and naturally acquired vs. culturally invented and taught — bites in literacy debates.
- **Dual coding** — combining words and visuals for two retrieval routes — bites in visual conventions across the school.
- **Levels of processing** — deep (meaning) vs. shallow (surface) encoding — bites in task design ("what will they think about?").
- **Advance organizer** — brief higher-order framework presented before new material — bites in unit launches.
- **Meaningful vs. rote learning** — anchored to prior ideas vs. isolated — bites in assessment design.
- **Metacognition / calibration** — knowledge and regulation of one's cognition; how well confidence matches accuracy — bites in study-skills teaching.
- **Implicit theories (fixed/growth)** — beliefs about malleability of ability — bites in praise and feedback culture.
- **Self-efficacy** — task-specific belief in one's ability to succeed; built by mastery experiences — bites in success-rate targets.
- **Attribution (locus / stability / controllability)** — how students explain outcomes — bites in teacher language.
- **Scaffolding / fading / ZPD** — contingent, temporary support within what a learner can do with help — bites in materials and lesson sequences.
- **2 sigma problem** — tutoring plus mastery far outperforms class teaching; the scaling challenge — bites in intervention design and AI tutoring claims.
- **ICAP** — interactive > constructive > active > passive — bites in "active learning" policy.
- **Elaboration theory / zoom lens** — simple-to-complex sequencing with recurring big picture — bites in curriculum mapping.
- **Pure vs. guided discovery** — unassisted discovery underperforms; guided discovery can work — bites in project and inquiry policy.
- **Direct instruction (di vs. DI)** — explicit, interactive, checked teaching vs. Engelmann's scripted program — bites in debates about "traditional" teaching.
- **Formative assessment** — eliciting evidence and using it to adapt instruction — bites in classroom routines and data systems.
- **Feed up / back / forward; task/process/self-regulation/self levels** — feedback anatomy — bites in marking policy.
- **Desirable difficulties** — challenges that slow performance but improve retention and transfer — bites in homework and revision design.
- **Learning vs. performance** — durable change vs. momentary success — bites in lesson observation and grading.
- **Retrieval / spacing / interleaving** — the high-utility study techniques — bite in schedules, homework and assessment.
- **Transfer (near/far)** — applying learning in new contexts; rare without deliberate design — bites in "21st-century skills" claims.
- **Situated cognition / cognitive apprenticeship** — knowledge bound to practice; make expert thinking visible via modeling, coaching, scaffolding, articulation, reflection, exploration — bites in project work and vocational pathways.
- **Community of practice / legitimate peripheral participation** — learning as growing participation in a practitioner community — bites in teacher development.
- **Meshing hypothesis** — the (unsupported) claim that matching style improves learning — bites in PD and parent expectations.
- **Mathemathantic** — instruction that kills learning — bites in over-guiding experts and under-guiding novices.
- **Media vs. method** — delivery vehicle vs. instructional mechanism — bites in EdTech procurement.
- **Lethal mutation** — implementation that removes a practice's active ingredient — bites in every school-wide initiative.

## Evidence strength & limits
- **Robust (replicated across labs, classrooms and meta-analyses):** retrieval practice and spacing; worked examples for novices; expertise reversal; the limits of working memory; prior knowledge effects; dual coding/multimedia principles (for well-specified conditions); the failure of learning styles meshing; the superiority of guided over unguided discovery; the variable but often large effects of feedback (with a real risk of negative effects).
- **Solid but with inflated headline numbers:** Bloom's 2 sigma (real tutoring effects are smaller but still large with high dosage); Black & Wiliam's 0.4-0.7 effect sizes (later estimates lower; heavily implementation-dependent); Hattie's broader effect-size rankings (methodologically criticized — the book uses the 2007 feedback review, which is a more conventional synthesis).
- **Contested:** growth mindset (small average effects; heterogeneous); the germane-load construct in CLT (revised); strong situated-cognition claims (Anderson, Reder & Simon critique); the primary/secondary knowledge distinction (theoretically appealing, limited direct tests); levels of processing (circularity critique; transfer-appropriate processing).
- **Arguing vs. showing.** The book selects "seminal" papers, which are old by design (many from 1960-2013). Seminal ≠ current; the reader should check each against later meta-analyses. The authors take a clear side in the "explicit instruction vs. inquiry" debate; chapters 12 and 22 are advocacy grounded in evidence, but the strongest evidence is for novices on well-structured domains (maths, science, reading) with near-transfer outcomes. Evidence is thinner for ill-structured domains, long-horizon projects, creativity, motivation over years, and adolescent identity.
- **Known critiques of the book.** Reviewers (e.g., the 2021 Frontiers in Psychology review; Learning Scientists and Schools Week reviews) praised clarity and practicality but noted: selection bias toward cognitive and instructivist work; practical applications sometimes more assertive than the underlying paper warrants; little on equity, culture, emotion or neuroscience beyond the cognitive level; and sparse treatment of productive-failure style findings (Kapur) that complicate the instruction-first message.
- **Mapping caveat for this file.** Several chapter anchors above (chs. 4, 8, 12, 19, 21, 27, 28, 30, 34, 35) are background-knowledge reconstructions; the underlying studies are real, but their placement in this book is not verified.

## Institutional design implications
- Adopt a **common instructional model** based on Rosenshine + cognitive apprenticeship, framed as mechanisms (review, small steps, checks, models, guided → independent practice, spaced review, think-aloud) rather than an observation checklist. **[E]** for the components; **[H]** that a common model improves school-wide outcomes more than teacher autonomy.
- Build a **knowledge-rich, cumulative, sequenced curriculum** in every subject, with explicit prerequisite maps and "zoom-out" spines; buy or build shared materials rather than leaving every teacher to write their own. **[E]** (prior knowledge, CLT) for the need; **[H]** that shared materials raise quality at scale.
- Make **retrieval and spacing structural**: daily review, weekly/monthly cumulative quizzes, cumulative end-of-year assessments, interleaved homework, school-wide revision calendars. **[E]**
- Default to **explicit instruction for novices, fading to guided and then open inquiry** as expertise grows; projects occur after knowledge-building, with checkpoints. **[E]** for novices; **[H]** for the right balance in secondary/upper years; **[V]** in how much open inquiry you want at all.
- Make **formative assessment the operating system** (all-student response routines, hinge questions, exit tickets) and keep formative data out of teacher evaluation. **[E]** for the practice; **[H]** for the separation rule.
- **Feedback policy:** favor whole-class feedback, re-do tasks, process-level comments; no grades on formative work; minimize written marking workload. **[E]** (Kluger & DeNisi; Butler; Hattie & Timperley) plus **[H]** for workload effects.
- **Tiered support modeled on Bloom:** mastery thresholds, rapid re-teaching, high-dosage small-group tutoring for students behind. **[E]** for tutoring; **[H]** for AI tutors approximating it.
- **Motivation through competence:** target high success rates in practice (~80%), process-focused praise, attributional language training; no mindset posters. **[E]** for self-efficacy/attribution mechanisms; **[H]** for school-wide culture effects.
- **Myth firewall:** a school glossary of debunked ideas (learning styles, learning pyramid, digital natives, left/right brain) used in hiring, PD and procurement. **[E]** that the myths are false; **[V]** in making this a hiring criterion.
- **EdTech/AI procurement rule:** every tool must name the instructional method it enables and evidence of effect; no purchases for "engagement" alone. **[E]** (Clark) + **[V]**.
- **Teacher development as a community of practice:** protected co-planning time, lesson study, coaching, joint review of student work; PD content grounded in the mechanisms of this book. **[H]** (strong theory, mixed PD evidence) + **[V]**.
- **Lethal-mutation audit:** for every initiative, document the active ingredient, the likely mutation, and how you'll check fidelity to mechanism. **[H]**
- **Metacognition and study skills taught explicitly inside subjects**, with calibration quizzes, not as a generic course. **[E]** for domain-embedded metacognition; **[H]** for scheduling.
- **Materials design standards** (no split attention, no redundancy, no decorative clutter; standard visual organizers school-wide). **[E]**
- **Ability grouping and differentiation by prior knowledge, flexibly and temporarily**, not by style or fixed labels. **[E]** against styles; **[H]** on grouping specifics.

## Retrieval practice

1. **Recall.** What does the book take as its working definition of learning, and which memory structure is the bottleneck?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Learning is a (durable) change in long-term memory. Working memory — small, short-lived, handling only a few new elements at once — is the bottleneck through which new information must pass.</details>

2. **Explain why.** Why do worked examples beat problem-solving practice for novices, and why does the advantage disappear for experts?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Novices solving problems use means-ends search, which consumes working memory and leaves little capacity for building schemas (Sweller 1988). Worked examples remove the search so capacity goes to understanding the solution structure. Experts already have schemas, so worked examples become redundant load and independent problem-solving is better (expertise reversal effect).</details>

3. **Recall.** Name Dunlosky et al.'s two high-utility techniques and two of the low-utility ones students favor.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>High: practice testing (retrieval practice) and distributed (spaced) practice. Low: rereading, highlighting/underlining, summarization, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text (any two of these).</details>

4. **Apply.** A middle-school science department proposes that Year 7 students spend a term "being scientists", designing their own investigations on questions they choose. Using this book, what do you advise?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Novices are not little experts (Chi et al.); unguided discovery underperforms guided methods (Mayer 2004; KSC 2006). Keep the goal but restructure: explicit teaching of the needed concepts and of experimental design (control of variables) with worked examples; then guided investigations with scaffolds that fade; open student-designed inquiry later, once students have the knowledge to use it. Build in checks for understanding throughout.</details>

5. **Spot the misconception.** "We should use dual coding for our visual learners and podcasts for our auditory learners."
<details><summary>Answer</summary>This confuses dual coding with learning styles. Dual coding (Paivio; Mayer) says everyone benefits from combining words and visuals appropriately. The meshing hypothesis (matching mode to "style") has no adequate evidence (Pashler et al. 2008). Choose mode by content, not learner.</details>

6. **Explain why.** Why might a teacher who praises every attempt with "you're so clever" harm motivation, according to chapters 13-15?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Trait praise signals a fixed, ability-based explanation of success (Dweck: fixed theory; Weiner: stable, uncontrollable attribution). When failure comes, the same logic implies low ability, producing helplessness. Process/strategy feedback attributes outcomes to controllable causes and supports persistence; real self-efficacy comes from mastery experiences, not praise.</details>

7. **Recall.** State Hattie & Timperley's three feedback questions and the four levels at which feedback operates. Which level is least effective?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Where am I going? (feed up), How am I going? (feedback), Where to next? (feed forward). Levels: task, process, self-regulation, self. Feedback about the self (personal praise) is least effective.</details>

8. **Apply.** Your school's new "retrieval practice" initiative has become weekly graded tests that pupils revise for by cramming. What went wrong and how do you fix it?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>A lethal mutation: the active ingredient (low-stakes, effortful recall, spaced over time, with feedback) was replaced by high-stakes, massed performance. Fix: make quizzes frequent, low- or no-stakes, cumulative (including older material), followed by quick correction; separate them from grading; teach students why retrieval works.</details>

9. **Explain why.** Why does Bloom's 2 sigma finding matter for school design, and what caution applies to the number?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>It shows how much better learning can be when instruction is individualized with mastery and correctives, setting a target for group methods (formative assessment, mastery learning, tutoring). Caution: the effect came from small, short studies with aligned tests; modern tutoring meta-analyses find large but smaller effects (roughly 0.3-0.4 SD for high-dosage programs). Use it as a design target, not a forecast, especially when evaluating AI tutoring claims.</details>

10. **Spot the misconception.** "Kirschner and Hendrick say students should be passive; active learning doesn't work."
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Wrong. The book distinguishes behavioral from cognitive activity. Chi's ICAP shows constructive and interactive engagement (self-explaining, generating, discussing) produce the most learning, and these can happen inside explicit instruction. What the book opposes is minimal guidance for novices, not student thinking; Rosenshine's model is highly interactive (many questions, all students responding, guided practice).</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- **"It's pro-lecture."** It's pro-*explicit* and pro-*interactive*: small steps, constant questioning, checks for understanding, guided practice. Long uninterrupted talk violates its own working-memory principles.
- **"It says inquiry and projects are bad."** It says *unguided* discovery is bad for *novices*. Guided inquiry, cognitive apprenticeship and projects for students with sufficient knowledge are compatible with the book.
- **"Cognitive load theory means make it easy."** CLT says reduce *extraneous* load so capacity goes to the intrinsic challenge; desirable difficulties (retrieval, spacing) deliberately add productive effort.
- **"Desirable difficulties means harder is better."** A difficulty is only desirable if the learner can successfully respond to it; impossible tasks produce failure, not learning.
- **"Knowledge-rich means rote memorization."** Ausubel is explicitly against rote learning; the aim is meaningful, organized, connected knowledge that supports thinking.
- **"Metacognition, problem solving and critical thinking are generic skills we can teach separately."** The book argues these are largely domain-specific and knowledge-dependent.
- **"Formative assessment means more tests and more data."** It means eliciting evidence *and using it* to change teaching and learning now; data collection without responsive action is a mutation.
- **"Growth mindset is the answer to motivation."** The book presents mindset alongside self-efficacy and attribution; the evidence says small, conditional effects, and competence-building is the more reliable engine.
- **"Seminal means current."** The papers are chosen for influence, often decades old; each should be read alongside later replications and critiques.
- **"This is a complete theory of schooling."** It's a theory of how individual minds acquire knowledge and skills in instructional settings. It says little about purpose, identity, belonging, adolescence, equity or organizational change — which is why the rest of this curriculum exists.
