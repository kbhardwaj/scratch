## The book in one sentence

The biggest lever a school has for raising achievement is getting every teacher to routinely collect evidence of what students actually understand, minute by minute, and use it to adjust teaching, and the only reliable way to get teachers to change those habits is sustained, school-based, small-step practice in monthly teacher learning communities.

## The book in one paragraph

Wiliam and Leahy argue that teacher quality varies enormously and matters more than almost any structural reform, so a school's strategy should be to improve the teachers it already has. The best-evidenced way to do that is formative assessment — not a type of test but a *use* of evidence: teachers, peers, and learners gather information about where learning is, interpret it, and use it to make better next-step decisions than they would have made without it. The authors organize this into one big idea (use evidence to adapt teaching to meet learning needs) and five key strategies (clarify intentions and success criteria; elicit evidence; give feedback that moves learning forward; activate students as resources for one another; activate students as owners of their learning). Each strategy chapter offers a menu of concrete classroom techniques — hinge-point questions, no-hands-up questioning, mini whiteboards, exit tickets, comment-only marking, "find it and fix it", peer and self assessment routines — with the pitfalls of each. The distinctive second half of the argument is institutional: knowing about these techniques changes nothing, because teaching runs on habits; teachers need to pick one or two techniques, practise them for months, and report back to a small group of colleagues who meet monthly for about two years. That "teacher learning community" (TLC) structure — choice, flexibility, small steps, accountability, support — is the implementation engine.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)

This is the curriculum's bridge from Design to Institutions. Other books tell you *what* good instruction looks like; this one gives (a) a compact, well-evidenced classroom operating system for adapting instruction to evidence, and (b) a concrete, trialled mechanism for spreading it across a staff. Read it asking: **"What routines would make it impossible for a teacher in my school to go a whole lesson without knowing what most students understand — and what weekly/monthly structure makes teachers actually adopt those routines and keep them?"**

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

### Background you need first: Black & Wiliam (1998) and where "formative assessment" comes from

- **Origins.** Michael Scriven (1967) distinguished *formative* from *summative* evaluation of curricula; Benjamin Bloom (late 1960s, mastery learning) applied "formative" to classroom tests used to correct learning before the unit ends. For decades "formative assessment" usually meant "more frequent tests."
- **The 1998 review.** Paul Black and Dylan Wiliam, at King's College London, reviewed roughly 250 studies (selected from several hundred publications, 1988-1997) in "Assessment and Classroom Learning" (*Assessment in Education*, 1998). Their accessible summary, "Inside the Black Box" (*Phi Delta Kappan*, 1998), claimed that strengthening classroom formative practice produced gains typically in the range of about 0.4-0.7 standard deviations, with the largest benefits for lower achievers. "Black box" = policy treats the classroom as inputs/outputs and ignores what happens inside.
- **From review to practice.** The KMOFAP project (King's-Medway-Oxfordshire Formative Assessment Project, 1999-2001; Black, Harrison, Lee, Marshall & Wiliam, *Assessment for Learning: Putting It into Practice*, 2003) worked with ~24 secondary maths/science teachers; Wiliam et al. (2004) reported effects of roughly 0.3 SD on externally scored measures. The big lesson: teachers could not simply be "told" the research; they had to reinvent practices in their own classrooms over time.
- **Assessment for Learning (AfL).** The UK Assessment Reform Group popularized "assessment for learning" (vs. "of learning"). Wiliam later preferred to define formative by *function*: any assessment is formative to the extent that evidence is elicited, interpreted, and used to make better instructional decisions than would otherwise have been made (Black & Wiliam 2009 definition; paraphrased).
- **The five-strategy framework.** Leahy, Lyon, Thompson & Wiliam, "Classroom Assessment: Minute by Minute, Day by Day" (*Educational Leadership*, 2005), and Wiliam & Thompson (2008) crossed three processes (where the learner is going / where the learner is now / how to get there — echoing Ramaprasad 1983 and Sadler 1989 on feedback as closing a gap) with three agents (teacher / peer / learner), yielding the five strategies. That grid is the backbone of this book.
- **So-what:** Formative assessment is not a product you buy, a test platform, or a data dashboard. It is a set of in-lesson decision routines. A school that buys "formative assessment software" but does not change moment-to-moment teaching has bought the wrong thing.

### Ch 1 — Why Formative Assessment Should Be a Priority for Every Teacher

- **Core claims**
  - Educational achievement matters enormously for individuals (earnings, health, civic life) and nations (economic growth; Hanushek & Woessmann's work linking test scores to GDP growth is the standard reference Wiliam draws on).
  - Within-school variation in teacher effectiveness dwarfs between-school variation. In the classrooms of the most effective teachers, students learn at something like twice the rate of those with average teachers (Wiliam's recurring summary of value-added research, e.g., Hanushek, Rivkin; exact multipliers vary by study).
  - Therefore "teacher quality" is the lever — but replacing teachers (deselection, better hiring) is slow and small-effect at the margin. The high-yield route is improving *existing* teachers ("love the one you're with" is Wiliam's phrase for this stance).
  - Many popular reforms (structural changes, smaller schools, new governance, technology, learning styles, generic PD) have weak or no effects. Formative assessment has among the strongest evidence-to-cost ratios.
  - Formative assessment is defined by use: evidence elicited, interpreted, and acted on by teachers, learners, or peers. It can operate on long cycles (across units/terms), medium cycles (within/between units, days-weeks), and short cycles (minute-by-minute and day-by-day). The biggest payoff is in the **short cycle**, because that is where decisions actually change teaching for the students in front of you.
  - One big idea + five key strategies (see grid under "Mental models").
- **Key evidence:** Black & Wiliam 1998; KMOFAP (Wiliam, Lee, Harrison & Black 2004); value-added teacher effectiveness literature.
- **So-what for a school designer:** Your default investment should be in teacher practice, not structures. Hire for coachability as well as current skill, and budget for continuing improvement of *everyone*, because the argument is that every teacher can get better — not because they are deficient, but because they can always get better (paraphrase of a Wiliam refrain).

### Ch 2 — Your Professional Learning (the institutional mechanism: Teacher Learning Communities)

This is the chapter to read twice. It explains why most professional development fails and what replaces it.

- **Core claims**
  - Teachers do not lack knowledge; they lack a way of changing *habits*. Teaching is a real-time performance with hundreds of decisions per hour; practices are automatized. Changing them is more like changing an athlete's technique than learning a fact. Knowing about hinge questions does not make you use them at minute 23 of a messy lesson.
  - One-off workshops, conferences, and "inspirational" training produce knowledge but little sustained change. Teacher change needs **time, practice in one's own classroom, and follow-up over an extended period** (roughly two years).
  - The design principles for teacher change (Wiliam's five): **choice** (teachers pick techniques that fit their context and style), **flexibility** (adapt the technique, within limits), **small steps** (one or two changes at a time), **accountability** (commit publicly to colleagues to try something and report back), and **support** (colleagues, structured materials, peer observation).
  - **"Tight but loose"** (Thompson & Wiliam 2008): be *tight* on the core theory (the five strategies and the big idea) and on the process (regular TLC meetings, action plans, peer observation), but *loose* on which techniques and how teachers adapt them. Too tight kills ownership and local fit; too loose produces "lethal mutations" where a technique keeps its name but loses the feature that made it work (e.g., traffic lights used only as a feel-good display; "no hands up" with the same three students always picked).
- **The TLC model — structure** (standard Wiliam/Leahy specification; numbers are typical recommendations, from the book and the associated KLT/EFA materials)
  - **Group:** about 8-12 teachers (smaller becomes fragile when people are absent; larger leaves no time for everyone to report). Can be mixed subject/grade; the common content is the pedagogy. Led by a teacher leader rather than an outside expert — the leader facilitates, does not lecture.
  - **Cadence:** monthly, about 75-120 minutes, for about two years. Monthly because teachers need ~3-4 weeks to actually try a technique repeatedly; more frequent meetings mean "nothing to report", less frequent means momentum dies.
  - **Standard agenda** (roughly):
    1. *Introduction* (~5 min) — goals of the session.
    2. *Starter activity* (~5 min) — something that gets everyone thinking about formative practice.
    3. *How's it going?* (~25-50 min, the heart) — each teacher reports on what they committed to try last time, what happened, what they'd change; colleagues respond. This is where accountability and support live.
    4. *New learning about formative assessment* (~20-40 min) — a short input on an idea or technique, often with an activity.
    5. *Personal action planning* (~15 min) — each teacher writes down which technique(s) they will try before next meeting, and plans a peer observation.
    6. *Review of learning* (~5 min) — did we meet the session's goals?
  - **Between meetings:** teachers practise their chosen technique(s) and do **structured peer observations** — the observer looks at what the observed teacher asked to be watched for (their chosen technique), not a general evaluative lesson judgement. Observations are developmental, not evaluative.
  - **Personal action plans:** a written plan listing a small number of techniques the teacher will add (and sometimes things they will *stop* doing to create time).
- **Why it works (the causal story)**
  - It targets habit change directly: repeated trial in real classrooms, with reflection at spaced intervals.
  - It creates *supportive accountability*: you said in front of colleagues you would try X, so you do.
  - It makes improvement collective and normal rather than remedial.
  - It is cheap and scalable: leaders can run it from packaged materials, so it does not depend on scarce experts.
  - It respects professional judgement (choice), which drives buy-in.
- **Leadership requirements:** Leaders must give teachers *time* (protected meeting time — the book emphasizes finding time by stopping lower-value activities, e.g., some routine meetings or low-value marking), must not use TLCs as a vehicle for evaluation, and must keep the focus narrowly on formative assessment rather than letting the agenda drift to logistics.
- **Key evidence:** The ETS "Keeping Learning on Track" program (Thompson, Wiliam, Leahy and colleagues, mid-2000s) developed the TLC approach in US districts. The strongest test is the UK **EEF efficacy trial of "Embedding Formative Assessment"** (delivered by SSAT, based on Wiliam & Leahy's materials; 140 English secondary schools, ~25,000 Year 10-11 pupils, 2015-2017; evaluation published 2018): pupils in EFA schools made about **two months' additional progress** on GCSE Attainment 8, effect size ~0.09 (0.11 excluding schools already in a similar programme), with a very high security rating (verified on EEF project page). Schools ran monthly TLC workshops from resource packs and did structured peer observations — i.e., almost exactly the model in this chapter.
- **So-what for a school designer:** Build the TLC into the calendar from day one: monthly, protected, 90 minutes, groups of ~10, trained teacher leaders, two-year arc, peer observation slots in the timetable. It is the professional-learning backbone, not an add-on.

### Ch 3 — Strategy 1: Clarifying, Sharing, and Understanding Learning Intentions and Success Criteria

- **Core claims**
  - Students cannot hit a target they cannot see. Many students (disproportionately disadvantaged ones) fail not from lack of ability but from not knowing what quality looks like in a domain.
  - But the ritual of writing an objective on the board and having students copy it is mostly empty. The goal is *understanding*, not *sharing*.
  - Distinguish **learning intentions** (what is to be learned) from **the task** (what students will do). Teachers often write activities ("we will make a poster about volcanoes") in place of learning.
  - **Context-specific vs. context-free** intentions: "write a persuasive letter to the council about the skate park" (specific) vs. "use evidence to support a claim" (transferable). Wiliam and Leahy favor making the transferable, context-free intention visible so learning generalizes.
  - **Product vs. process** success criteria: what the finished work looks like vs. what you do to get there. Rubrics that describe products at vague levels ("uses some detail" / "uses a lot of detail") are close to useless for learners.
  - Sometimes you should *not* announce the intention at the start (e.g., inquiry where announcing the conclusion spoils it). The requirement is that by the end students understand what they were learning.
  - Quality in complex domains is often tacit; exemplars communicate it better than descriptors. Students learn quality by comparing and judging real work (Sadler's work on "guild knowledge").
- **Techniques (selection)**
  - Student-friendly language for criteria; co-constructing criteria with students.
  - Analyzing samples of anonymous work of varying quality; ranking samples and articulating why.
  - Using exemplars and "worked" examples of both strong and weak work.
  - Students designing test items or rubrics.
  - Separating learning intention from task on the board/in planning.
  - (bk) "Wall of excellence"-type displays and "choose-swap-choose" style comparative judgement activities.
- **So-what:** Curriculum documents should specify learning intentions and include annotated exemplars at multiple quality levels. Planning templates should force a separation between "what students will learn" and "what they will do."

### Ch 4 — Strategy 2: Engineering Effective Discussions, Tasks, and Activities That Elicit Evidence of Learning

The most technique-dense chapter, and the one with the highest classroom payoff.

- **Core claims**
  - Teaching is contingent: you cannot know what to do next without evidence of what students understand. Most classroom questioning collects evidence from the few confident volunteers, which gives a dangerously biased sample.
  - The purpose of questioning is to (a) *cause thinking* and (b) *provide the teacher with information to adapt instruction*. Questions that do neither are wasted.
  - **Plan questions in advance.** Good diagnostic questions are hard to invent in real time; they should be shared resources.
  - **Decision-driven data collection** beats data-driven decision making: first identify the decision you need to make (move on or reteach?), then design the question that informs it.
  - **Evaluative vs. interpretive listening** (after Brent Davis 1996/97): evaluative listening checks whether the student said the "right answer"; interpretive listening tries to understand what the student is thinking. Formative assessment needs the latter.
- **Named techniques**
  - **No hands up / random selection.** Students do not raise hands to answer (they may raise them to ask a question). The teacher selects, often randomly (names on popsicle sticks, a randomizer). Effects: everyone has to think because anyone might be asked; the teacher gets a representative sample. Pitfall: fake randomness (putting the stick back in your pocket), or students shutting down — pair with "phone a friend"/"I don't know yet, come back to me" norms.
  - **Wait time.** Mary Budd Rowe (1974/1986): typical teacher wait after asking is about one second; extending to ~3+ seconds improves length and quality of answers and participation. Also wait time after the answer.
  - **All-student response systems:** mini whiteboards, ABCD cards (lettered cards held up for multiple-choice), finger voting, clickers/digital polls. Everyone answers simultaneously so the teacher sees the whole class's distribution at once.
  - **Hinge-point questions.** A diagnostic question placed at the point in the lesson where the teacher must decide whether to move on. Design rules: every student answers (all-student response); the teacher can read the responses in about 30 seconds; students answer in about a minute or two; and **every wrong option is diagnostic** — distractors are written to correspond to specific, known misconceptions, so a wrong answer tells you *what* to fix. Also, it should be hard to get right for the wrong reason. Set a threshold in advance (e.g., if most are right, move on and follow up with the few; if not, reteach differently).
  - **Exit tickets / exit passes.** A short question at the end of the lesson that students answer before leaving; the teacher sorts responses to plan tomorrow's start (e.g., regroup, reteach, extend). The key is that the teacher *uses* them the next day.
  - **"Pose-pause-pounce-bounce"** (bk; associated with Pam Fisher/UK practice): ask, wait, pick a student, then bounce the answer to another student to evaluate or build on it.
  - **Basketball, not ping-pong** discussion: rather than teacher-student-teacher-student, the question moves student-to-student with the teacher guiding.
  - **Question shells** (bk): generic stems that produce thinking ("Why is X an example of Y?", "What is the same and different about...?", "Is it always, sometimes, or never true that...?").
  - **Formative use of summative tests:** use test results to identify which items many students missed and reteach those; students analyze their own errors.
  - Also: asking students to generate questions; "hot-seating"; using discussion tasks and ranking activities that surface reasoning.
- **Evidence:** Rowe on wait time; the diagnostic-item tradition in science and maths misconceptions research; Black & Wiliam 1998. Much of the chapter is craft knowledge grounded in the broader review rather than individual RCTs of each technique.
- **So-what:** Build item banks of hinge questions per unit with misconception-keyed distractors as a *school asset*. Equip every room with mini whiteboards. Make no-hands-up and all-student response default norms across the school so students experience consistency.

### Ch 5 — Strategy 3: Providing Feedback That Moves Learning Forward

- **Core claims**
  - Feedback is not automatically good. **Kluger & DeNisi (1996)** meta-analysis of ~600 effect sizes: feedback interventions improved performance on average, but in roughly a third (about 38%) of cases they *lowered* performance. Feedback that directs attention to the self (praise, ego, ranking) tends to hurt; feedback directed at the task and how to improve tends to help.
  - **Ruth Butler (1987/1988)**: students given comments only improved; students given grades only or grades plus comments did not — the grade swamped the comment. Hence **comment-only marking** (or delaying grades until after students respond to comments).
  - The measure of feedback is what the student does with it. Feedback should be **more work for the recipient than the donor**; if the student doesn't have to do anything, it's not functioning as feedback.
  - Feedback should be *forward-looking* ("what to do next") rather than retrospective description of what went wrong. Good feedback functions like a coach's correction, i.e., a recipe for improvement, not a verdict.
  - A formal definition used in the book lineage: feedback is information about the gap between actual and reference levels that is **used to alter the gap** (Ramaprasad 1983). Information that is not used is not feedback — it's just "data."
  - Feedback depends on relationship and trust: students must believe the teacher thinks they can improve. The same comment can motivate or deflate depending on the relationship.
  - Grading itself: the book is skeptical of frequent grades because they invite ego-involvement and comparison.
- **Named techniques**
  - **Comment-only marking** (or "comments first, grades later").
  - **"Find it and fix it"**: instead of marking each error, tell the student there are, say, five errors in a section (or place a mark in the margin by lines with errors) and have them find and correct them.
  - **"Three questions"** (bk): the teacher writes three questions/tasks in the student's book that the student must answer in class time, making feedback into work.
  - **Scaffolded feedback / re-do time**: dedicated lesson time to act on feedback ("DIRT"-like time in UK practice).
  - **Group feedback** (bk): the teacher reads all the work, writes the four or five most common comments on the board, and each student decides which apply to them.
  - **Focused marking**: comment on one or two criteria rather than everything.
  - **Feedback on task and process** rather than on the person; avoid generic praise.
- **Evidence strength:** Kluger & DeNisi and Butler are solid and replicated in spirit; Hattie & Timperley (2007) provide a complementary levels model (task, process, self-regulation, self). Specific techniques like "find it and fix it" are practitioner wisdom.
- **So-what:** Marking policy is a design decision. Mandate *responses to feedback* rather than volume of written marking; ban (or delay) grades on formative work; build in lesson time for acting on feedback. This also reduces teacher workload — a real win.

### Ch 6 — Strategy 4: Activating Students as Learning Resources for One Another

- **Core claims**
  - With 25-30 students and one teacher, the teacher cannot be the only source of feedback. Peers can be powerful — if structured.
  - Cooperative learning works when two conditions hold (**Robert Slavin**'s synthesis, 1990s-2000s): **group goals** (the group succeeds together) and **individual accountability** (each member's learning counts, so no free-riding). Group work without both is often worse than individual work.
  - Peer assessment benefits the *assessor* at least as much as the assessed: judging another's work against criteria builds understanding of quality, which feeds self-assessment.
  - Students often accept criticism from a peer more readily and in more accessible language than from a teacher; and students are often more honest about their confusion with peers.
- **Named techniques**
  - **"Two stars and a wish"**: two things done well, one thing to improve.
  - **C3B4ME** ("see three before me"): ask three peers before asking the teacher.
  - **Peer evaluation of homework**, **homework help board** (students post problems they couldn't solve; others who solved them help).
  - **Error classification**: peers categorize errors in work (e.g., careless slip vs. conceptual).
  - **Pre-flight checklist**: before handing in, a peer checks the work against a checklist of required features.
  - **"I-You-We" checklist** (bk): self, partner and group contribution checks.
  - **Reporter at random** (bk): any group member may be asked to report for the group, enforcing individual accountability.
  - **Group-based test preparation**: groups prepare members for an individual test; reward based on members' improvement.
- **So-what:** Teach peer assessment explicitly as a skill with scripted protocols; never use unstructured "group work." Norms (kind, specific, helpful) must be school-wide. Design group tasks with both group goals and individual accountability.

### Ch 7 — Strategy 5: Activating Students as Owners of Their Own Learning

- **Core claims**
  - The long-run goal is **self-regulated learning**: students who monitor their own understanding, choose strategies, and persist. This draws on metacognition research and models of self-regulation (Monique Boekaerts's dual-processing model: students continually decide whether to pursue *growth* — engaging with the task — or *well-being* — protecting themselves from threat).
  - Motivation is an outcome of successful learning as much as a cause: students who experience success become more motivated. Classroom climate should push students toward the growth pathway: task-involving (focus on improvement) rather than ego-involving (focus on ranking).
  - Attributions matter (Bernard Weiner; Carol Dweck's mindset work): students who attribute success/failure to effort and strategy (internal, unstable, controllable) persist more than those who attribute to fixed ability.
  - Self-assessment is hard and must be taught; students' self-reports are unreliable until they understand the criteria (links back to Strategy 1).
- **Named techniques**
  - **Traffic lights** (red/amber/green): students signal their confidence on work or on lesson learning intentions; the teacher acts on the signals (e.g., greens help ambers, teacher works with reds). Known lethal mutation: using it as display with no follow-up; students signal green to avoid attention.
  - **Colored cups** (red/yellow/green cups on desks) (bk) during independent work to signal status in real time without hand-raising.
  - **Learning logs / journals**: end-of-lesson reflection on what was learned and what's unclear.
  - **Learning portfolios**: students curate work showing progress.
  - **Self-assessment against rubrics/checklists** before submission.
  - **Students writing their own questions** or test items.
  - (bk) "Plus-minus-interesting" and similar reflection prompts.
- **So-what:** Self-regulation is a learned capability, not a personality trait. Build explicit routines from early grades; tie self-assessment to exemplars and criteria; avoid school cultures (public rankings, frequent grades) that push students into well-being-protecting mode.

### Conclusion

- **Core claims:** Improvement must be continuous and teacher-owned; start small; keep the focus on the five strategies; expect it to take years; and create structures (TLCs, peer observation, protected time) so that improvement does not depend on heroic individuals. The book closes with the challenge that every teacher can improve and that the school's job is to make that improvement routine.
- **So-what:** A world-class school is one where continuous, evidence-led improvement of classroom practice is *structural* rather than optional.

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book

1. **Formative assessment is a use of evidence, not a type of test.** Any activity is formative to the extent that evidence of learning is elicited, interpreted, and used to make better next-step decisions. The short cycle (within the lesson, lesson-to-lesson) is where most of the payoff is.
2. **The TLC model is the institutional engine.** Teachers change by practicing a few techniques over months, reporting to 8-12 colleagues in monthly ~90-minute meetings for ~2 years, with peer observation between meetings. Principles: choice, flexibility, small steps, accountability, support. The EEF RCT (140 schools) found about +2 months' progress from this model at scale.
3. **Improve the teachers you have.** Within-school differences in teacher effectiveness are large; developing all teachers beats structural reform or trying to hire your way to quality.
4. **Get evidence from every student, not volunteers.** No hands up, all-student response systems, and hinge-point questions with misconception-keyed distractors make the whole class's thinking visible in seconds, enabling real-time adaptation.
5. **Feedback can hurt.** About a third of feedback interventions lower performance (Kluger & DeNisi 1996); grades swamp comments (Butler). Feedback must be task-focused, forward-looking, and require the student to do work.
6. **Tight but loose.** Hold the core theory and process tight; let teachers choose and adapt techniques. Watch for lethal mutations that keep a technique's name but lose its mechanism.
7. **Students are an under-used resource.** Structured peer assessment (with group goals + individual accountability) and taught self-assessment extend feedback beyond what one teacher can provide, and build self-regulation.
8. **Clarity of target precedes everything.** Separate learning intention from task, prefer transferable (context-free) criteria, and use exemplars because quality is often tacit.

## Mental models & vocabulary

- **The big idea** — use evidence about learning to adapt teaching to meet learning needs. Bites: every lesson plan should contain at least one planned point where evidence changes what happens next.
- **The 3x3 grid (five strategies)** —

  | | Where the learner is going | Where the learner is now | How to get there |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Teacher | 1. Clarify/share learning intentions & success criteria | 2. Engineer discussions/tasks that elicit evidence | 3. Feedback that moves learning forward |
  | Peer | 1. Understand and share intentions & criteria | 4. Activate students as learning resources for one another | (4) |
  | Learner | 1. Understand intentions & criteria | 5. Activate students as owners of their own learning | (5) |

  Bites: a common planning/observation lens across all subjects.
- **Short / medium / long cycle** — timescale of the evidence-to-decision loop (minutes-days / weeks / terms). Bites: most "data" systems operate on long cycles and don't change daily teaching.
- **Hinge-point question** — a planned diagnostic question at a decision point in a lesson; all students answer within a couple of minutes; teacher reads responses within ~30 seconds; each wrong answer maps to a misconception. Bites: a unit's hinge questions are reusable curriculum assets.
- **All-student response system** — any method (whiteboards, ABCD cards, fingers, digital polls) producing simultaneous answers from everyone. Bites: equip every room; make it routine.
- **No hands up** — teacher selects who answers (often randomly); hands only for asking questions. Bites: equity of participation; must pair with psychological safety norms.
- **Exit ticket** — a short end-of-lesson check sorted to plan the next lesson. Bites: only works if the next lesson actually changes.
- **Wait time** — pause after questions (and after answers); ~1 second typical, 3+ better (Rowe). Bites: cheap, universally applicable.
- **Evaluative vs. interpretive listening** — listening for the right answer vs. listening to understand thinking. Bites: coaching observations can track this.
- **Comment-only marking** — feedback without grades on formative work. Bites: grading/reporting policy.
- **Feedback (Ramaprasad sense)** — information about a gap that is *used* to close it. Bites: unused comments are not feedback; judge marking by student response.
- **Ego-involving vs. task-involving** — classroom climate focused on comparison vs. improvement. Bites: rankings, public leaderboards, frequent grades push to ego.
- **Growth vs. well-being pathway (Boekaerts)** — student's moment-to-moment choice between engaging and self-protecting. Bites: explains disengagement as rational self-protection.
- **Group goals + individual accountability (Slavin)** — the two conditions for cooperative learning to work. Bites: design of any group task.
- **Teacher learning community (TLC)** — 8-12 teachers, monthly, ~75-120 min, ~2 years, fixed agenda built around "How's it going?" reports and personal action plans. Bites: professional development calendar, budget, leadership roles.
- **Personal action plan** — teacher's written commitment to a few techniques (and things to stop). Bites: the unit of accountability in a TLC.
- **Tight but loose** — rigid on core ideas and process, flexible on local adaptation. Bites: any school-wide initiative.
- **Lethal mutation** — adaptation that preserves a technique's surface but destroys its active ingredient. Bites: fidelity checks during peer observation.
- **Decision-driven data collection** — decide what decision you need to make, then collect only the data that informs it. Bites: data strategy; avoid dashboards nobody acts on.

## Evidence strength & limits

- **Robust:**
  - Feedback can have large effects in either direction (Kluger & DeNisi 1996; widely replicated pattern that ego-focused feedback harms).
  - Grades undermining comments (Butler 1987/88) — well-known, conceptually replicated, though original samples were small and specific.
  - Wait time effects (Rowe) — older but consistent.
  - Cooperative learning conditions (Slavin) — strong meta-analytic base.
  - The EEF RCT of the Embedding Formative Assessment programme: large, independent, very high security rating, positive (~0.09-0.11 SD; about two months). This is the strongest direct evidence for *the TLC delivery model at scale*.
- **Contested / weaker:**
  - The headline 0.4-0.7 SD effect from Black & Wiliam 1998 was a narrative synthesis, not a formal meta-analysis. **Bennett (2011)**, "Formative Assessment: A Critical Review," argued the concept was ill-defined and the effect sizes over-stated; **Kingston & Nash (2011)** meta-analysis found a much smaller weighted mean effect (~0.2) and flagged few high-quality studies; Dunn & Mulvenon (2009) raised similar methodological concerns. Wiliam has conceded the 1998 numbers were not a precise estimate, while arguing the effects are real and cost-effective.
  - The effect at scale (EEF ~0.1) is much smaller than the 1998 figures — typical of scale-up decay. Treat 0.1 SD for the whole school as a realistic, still valuable, estimate.
  - Individual techniques (hinge questions, traffic lights, find-it-and-fix-it) are mostly practitioner-derived; few have been isolated in RCTs. The book is arguing from mechanism and teacher experience rather than showing technique-level causal effects.
  - Self-assessment and mindset claims: mindset interventions have mixed replication (small average effects, larger for some subgroups); students' self-assessments are often poorly calibrated.
  - Teacher-effectiveness multipliers ("twice as fast") depend on value-added models with known measurement noise.
- **What's arguing vs. showing:** The chapter-2 claims about habit change and the five principles are plausible theory informed by experience; the TLC model's effectiveness has been shown (EEF), but which ingredients are necessary has not been isolated.

## Institutional design implications

- Make the TLC the core professional-learning structure: monthly, protected 75-120 minute meetings, groups of 8-12, two-year cycles, trained teacher leaders. [E] (EEF RCT; KLT experience)
- Timetable structured, non-evaluative peer observation between TLC meetings, focused on each teacher's chosen technique. [E] (part of trialled package; individual contribution untested) / [H] for its specific effect
- Keep TLCs strictly separate from performance evaluation. [H] (theory: psychological safety; not directly tested)
- Adopt "tight but loose" governance: school-wide commitment to the big idea and five strategies; teacher choice of techniques. [H]
- Create time for TLCs by stopping lower-value activities (e.g., routine administrative meetings, volume marking). [V]/[H]
- Build and maintain a school-owned bank of hinge-point questions with misconception-keyed distractors for every unit. [H] (mechanism well-grounded; bank effect untested)
- School-wide classroom norms: no hands up for answering, all-student response systems in every room, planned wait time, daily exit tickets used to plan the next lesson. [H] (component techniques have partial evidence)
- Marking/feedback policy: comment-only or delayed grades on formative work; require student action on feedback; lesson time for re-drafting; judge feedback by student response, not volume of teacher writing. [E] (Butler; Kluger & DeNisi) for the grade/comment principle; [H] for specific routines
- Minimize ego-involving practices (public rankings, frequent grading, ability-labeling displays). [E]/[V]
- Curriculum documents specify learning intentions separately from tasks and include annotated exemplars at multiple quality levels. [H]
- All group work designed with group goals plus individual accountability. [E] (Slavin)
- Teach peer and self assessment explicitly as skills, starting early grades, with common school-wide protocols. [H]
- Budget professional learning for all teachers continuously, not only for struggling ones; improvement as a professional norm. [V] (plus [E] for teacher-effect size)
- Treat vendor "formative assessment platforms" and data dashboards skeptically unless they change within-lesson decisions. [H]
- Set expectations realistically: a whole-school formative programme might yield about 0.1 SD over two years — worth it, but not magic. [E]

## Retrieval practice

1. State the definition of formative assessment used by Wiliam and Leahy in your own words. What makes an assessment "formative"?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Not the type of test but its use: evidence of student achievement is elicited, interpreted, and used by teachers, learners, or peers to make better (or better-founded) next-step decisions than they would have made without that evidence. The same quiz can be formative or not depending on whether it changes decisions.</details>

2. Name the five key strategies and the big idea.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Big idea: use evidence of learning to adapt teaching to meet learning needs. Strategies: (1) clarify, share, understand learning intentions and success criteria; (2) engineer discussions, tasks, activities that elicit evidence of learning; (3) provide feedback that moves learning forward; (4) activate students as learning resources for one another; (5) activate students as owners of their own learning.</details>

3. Why do Wiliam and Leahy think one-off workshops fail to change teaching, and what five principles do TLCs embody instead?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Teaching is habitual, real-time performance; knowing about a technique does not change automatized practice. Change needs repeated trial in one's own classroom with follow-up over a long period. Principles: choice, flexibility, small steps, accountability, support.</details>

4. Describe the standard TLC meeting: size, frequency, duration, and agenda.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>About 8-12 teachers, monthly, roughly 75-120 minutes, over about two years. Agenda: introduction; starter activity; "How's it going?" (each teacher reports on what they tried — the longest part); new learning; personal action planning; review of learning. Between meetings, teachers practise and do structured peer observations.</details>

5. Scenario: A science teacher asks "What happens to a ball thrown upward at the top of its path?" and takes answers from three raised hands, all correct, and moves on. What went wrong and how would you redesign this moment?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Evidence came only from confident volunteers — a biased sample; the teacher has no idea what most students think. Redesign as a hinge-point question: multiple choice with distractors mapping to misconceptions (e.g., "velocity and acceleration both zero", "force upward still acting"), all students answer on ABCD cards or whiteboards after wait time, teacher reads the distribution in ~30 seconds and decides to move on or reteach based on a pre-set threshold.</details>

6. Spot the misconception: "Formative assessment means giving more frequent short tests and recording the scores in our tracking system."
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Frequency and recording are not the point; it's formative only if evidence changes instruction or learning decisions, ideally on the short cycle. Recording scores into a tracker that nobody acts on in the next lesson is summative-style monitoring. Frequent grading can even harm learning by inducing ego-involvement.</details>

7. What did Ruth Butler find about grades and comments, and what policy follows?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Students receiving comments only improved; those receiving grades, or grades plus comments, did not — grades crowd out attention to comments. Policy: comment-only marking on formative work, or delay grades until students have acted on comments.</details>

8. Explain why Kluger & DeNisi's finding matters for a school's feedback policy.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>In about 38% of feedback interventions, feedback made performance worse. So "more feedback" is not automatically better; feedback that focuses on the self (praise, ranking, ego) can backfire. Policy should require task-focused, forward-looking feedback that students must act on, and judge feedback by the student response it provokes.</details>

9. Scenario: A new principal wants every teacher to use "traffic lights" daily and will check during walk-throughs. Using "tight but loose" and "lethal mutation," critique the plan.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>It is tight on the technique rather than the idea — teachers lose choice and adapt to compliance. Likely lethal mutation: students flash cards for inspectors, the teacher doesn't act on the signals, students show green to avoid attention. Better: be tight on the big idea and the TLC process, let teachers choose techniques in personal action plans, and use non-evaluative peer observation to check whether the active ingredient (acting on the signal) is present.</details>

10. The EEF trial found about two months' progress (~0.09 SD) while Black & Wiliam 1998 suggested 0.4-0.7 SD. How do you reconcile these for planning?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The 1998 figures came from a narrative review of often small, varied studies (critiqued by Bennett 2011 and Kingston & Nash 2011); scale-up via packaged materials across 140 schools typically dilutes effects. For planning, expect roughly 0.1 SD whole-school over two years from a cheap intervention — good value, but not transformative on its own; combine with strong curriculum and other levers.</details>

## Common misreadings of this book

- **"It's about assessment/testing."** It's about teaching: in-lesson decision-making. Tests are one possible evidence source among many.
- **"Formative = low stakes quizzes + data dashboards."** Data that doesn't change the next lesson isn't formative. The short cycle matters most.
- **"Just adopt the techniques."** The book's central institutional claim is that techniques without a habit-change structure (TLCs) don't stick.
- **"Mandate the techniques for consistency."** Violates "tight but loose"; produces compliance and lethal mutations. Be tight on principles and process, loose on technique choice.
- **"Feedback should be frequent and detailed."** Volume isn't the goal; feedback that requires student action and is task-focused is. Detailed written marking can be low-value and exhausting.
- **"No hands up means putting students on the spot."** It's about sampling fairly and making everyone think; paired with supportive norms ("come back to me", "phone a friend"), it improves participation.
- **"Peer assessment is just students marking each other's work."** Its main value is learning criteria and judgment; it requires structure, group goals and individual accountability.
- **"The 0.4-0.7 effect sizes are the expected result."** Realistic at-scale effects are about 0.1 SD. Still worthwhile.
- **"This is a teacher book, not a leadership book."** Chapter 2 is a leadership blueprint: calendar, time, groups, roles, accountability culture.
