## The papers in one sentence
Learning is limited by a small working memory and shaped by what the learner already believes, so good schooling guides novices hard, uses curiosity on purpose, and treats misconceptions as structured systems to rebuild rather than as isolated errors to correct.

## The papers in one paragraph
Two of the six papers are about **capacity**. Sweller's cognitive load theory says working memory is tiny and short-lived when it handles new information, while long-term memory is effectively unlimited. Expertise is a large store of organized schemas, and instruction exists to build them. Kirschner, Sweller & Clark turn that into a polemic: letting novices discover things wastes their scarce working memory on searching, so fully guided instruction (worked examples, explicit explanation) beats minimal guidance. The expertise reversal effect adds that the guidance should fade as learners gain expertise. One paper is about **motivation as a memory amplifier**. Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath show that a state of curiosity engages the brain's dopamine reward circuit and the hippocampus, and improves memory for the answer and even for unrelated material seen during the curious state. Later work narrows this: the incidental benefit is short-lived and small, and a 2024 study found that curiosity *hurt* memory for unrelated school facts. Three papers are about **prior knowledge as structure**. Posner et al. say that replacing a deep conception requires the learner to be dissatisfied with the old one and to find the new one intelligible, plausible and fruitful. Chi says the hardest misconceptions come from placing a concept in the wrong ontological category (treating heat or electric current as a substance when it is an emergent process), and that change requires shifting the category. Her ICAP framework ranks engagement as interactive > constructive > active > passive. Vosniadou shows that children fuse new instruction with an underlying framework theory, producing "synthetic models" such as a flat disc or a hollow sphere Earth, so teaching must target the framework's presuppositions and not only the surface facts.

## Why these are in the curriculum (the question to read them with)
**What does a school's instructional model have to respect about human cognition, whatever its pedagogy or philosophy?** These papers are the "physics" under the curriculum's books. Every design decision later (how much discovery, when to use problems before instruction, how to sequence science, what counts as engagement) either works with these constraints or quietly violates them. Read them asking two questions: *which constraint is this?* and *how strong is the evidence that it binds in a real K-12 classroom?*

## Paper-by-paper: the salient knowledge

### 1. Sweller — Cognitive Load Theory (1988; 1998; 2019)

**The claim**
- Human cognition has two memory systems that matter for instruction. **Working memory** is where conscious processing happens. For *novel* information it can hold only a few elements at once (roughly 2-4 interacting items) and loses them within seconds unless they are rehearsed. **Long-term memory** is effectively unlimited and stores knowledge as **schemas**: organized chunks that working memory can treat as a single element.
- Expertise is mainly a large library of domain-specific schemas, many of them **automated** so they run without using working memory. A chess master "sees" a position in a few chunks; a novice sees 25 separate pieces.
- So the purpose of instruction is to build and automate schemas in long-term memory. Anything that uses up working memory without contributing to schema building is waste.
- The 1998 paper sets out the three types of load, which have become the theory's standard vocabulary:
  - **Intrinsic load**: the complexity of the material itself, set by how many elements must be processed at the same time because they interact (*element interactivity*). Solving an equation has high element interactivity; learning vocabulary pairs has low.
  - **Extraneous load**: load created by poor design, such as split attention between a diagram and distant text, redundant information, or unguided search.
  - **Germane load**: working memory devoted to building schemas. The 2019 update folds it into intrinsic load. It is now described as the working memory resources allocated to handling intrinsic load, not as a separate additive type. This is an important technical change: CLT no longer says "add germane load."
- The 2019 update also grounds the theory in **evolutionary psychology** (David Geary's distinction). *Biologically primary* knowledge, such as speaking a native language, recognizing faces or basic social reasoning, is acquired without instruction, and working memory limits bind it less. *Biologically secondary* knowledge, such as reading, algebra or chemistry, is culturally recent, must be taught explicitly, and is where CLT applies. The update also adds newer effects, including a working memory depletion effect (working memory capacity drops temporarily after sustained effort).

**The method and key experiment (1988)**
- Sweller had learners solve problems such as transformation problems and simple geometry or trigonometry, and compared conventional goal-specific problems ("find angle X") with **goal-free** versions ("find as many angles as you can"). He also ran a computational model of the problem solving.
- The key argument: when novices solve conventional problems, they use **means-ends analysis**. They keep track of the current state, the goal state, the differences between them, the operators that reduce those differences, and a stack of subgoals. This is an effective way to *solve* a problem but a heavy working memory load, and it leaves little capacity for noticing the problem's structure, so solvers can succeed repeatedly without learning the schema.
- Findings: goal-free problem solvers acquired schemas better. Across the 1980s and 1990s, the same logic produced the **worked example effect** (studying worked solutions beats solving equivalent problems, for novices), the **split-attention effect**, the **redundancy effect**, the **modality effect** (a diagram with spoken text beats a diagram with written text) and the **completion problem effect**. These came from Sweller, Cooper, Chandler, Tarmizi, Mousavi, Paas and others; the details are background knowledge.

**Limits and critiques**
- **Measuring load is the weak point.** Much of CLT measures load with a single self-report item (Paas's 1992 mental effort scale). Critics, notably de Jong (2010) in *Instructional Science*, argue this makes the theory hard to falsify: if an intervention works, "extraneous load went down"; if it fails, "intrinsic load was too high." The 2019 paper acknowledges this measurement problem.
- **Germane load was conceptually unstable**, which is why it was demoted in the 2019 update.
- **Ecological validity.** Many classic studies are short, lab-style or single-lesson experiments with older students or adults, in well-structured domains (mathematics, physics, technical training). Evidence for ill-structured domains (writing, history interpretation, design) is thinner.
- The **primary/secondary distinction** is plausible but hard to test directly. Some developmental researchers dispute where the line falls.
- **Motivation** is largely outside the model. Working memory in CLT is a capacity; what learners *want* to attend to is not formally included, which is where Gruber's work (paper 3) adds something.

**So what for a school designer**
- Treat working memory as the scarcest resource in the building. Every slide, worksheet, bell schedule interruption, "open the laptop and log in," and poorly integrated diagram costs it.
- Schemas are the product. A curriculum that does not build **knowledge in long-term memory**, sequenced and cumulative, is not building expertise, whatever the pedagogy.
- Design review standard: materials should be checked for split attention, redundancy and uncontrolled search, just as software is checked for unnecessary complexity.

### 2. Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) + the expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga et al. 2003)

**The claim**
- Constructivist, discovery, problem-based, experiential and inquiry-based teaching share one feature: learners are given **minimal guidance** and are expected to discover or construct key information themselves.
- Given human cognitive architecture (paper 1), this fails for **novices**: searching for solutions uses up working memory and does not add to long-term memory. The authors say learners can be "busy" without learning.
- **Fully guided instruction**, meaning explicit explanation of the concepts and procedures, worked examples and process worksheets, is more effective and more efficient for novices. This is argued to hold for novices and intermediates; advanced learners may do fine with less guidance.
- A second argument: constructivist teaching confuses **the epistemology of a discipline with its pedagogy**. Scientists discover things, but novices learning science are not in a scientist's position. Experts investigate *using* schemas that novices do not yet have. Teaching students to "act like scientists" does not give them the knowledge that makes scientists effective.
- They also cite Richard Clark's findings that weaker students taught with less guidance can show *lower* scores after instruction than before, and that learners often prefer the method from which they learn least.

**The method**
- This is a **review and argument paper**, not a new experiment. It combines (a) CLT architecture, (b) the worked example literature, (c) Mayer's 2004 review "Should there be a three-strikes rule against pure discovery learning?" in *American Psychologist* (background knowledge), which surveyed decades of pure-discovery studies, and (d) medical school problem-based learning evaluations.

**The expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003)**
- Instructional methods that help novices can become **neutral or harmful for more expert learners**. For an expert, a worked example or integrated explanatory text is *redundant*: the learner must reconcile it with schemas they already have, which adds extraneous load.
- Evidence: a set of experiments in which the relative effectiveness of formats (for example worked examples versus problem solving, or diagram with text versus diagram alone) **reversed** as learners gained expertise across sessions.
- Design consequence: **guidance fading**. Move from full worked examples, to completion problems, to independent problems as competence grows. Ideally adapt this to each learner, using quick "rapid assessment" probes of the kind Kalyuga later developed.
- This is the key reconciliation clause: the 2006 paper's case is strongest for **novices**. Its authors do not claim that direct instruction is always best.

**Findings and the response**
- The 2007 replies in *Educational Psychologist* 42(2) matter as much as the original:
  - **Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn (2007)**: problem-based learning and inquiry learning *as actually practiced* are **heavily scaffolded**, not minimally guided. KSC attacked a straw man, and the evidence from well-scaffolded inquiry is favorable.
  - **Schmidt et al. (2007)**: problem-based learning in medical education is not minimal guidance, and its outcomes are competitive.
  - **Kuhn (2007)**: the goals differ. Inquiry aims partly at learning *how to inquire*, which direct instruction on content does not measure.
  - **Sweller et al. (2007) rejoinder**: if inquiry is heavily scaffolded with explicit guidance, it becomes more like direct instruction, which supports their point. The critics also have not shown controlled comparisons favoring less guidance.
- Background knowledge: the debate later moved to "which guidance, when?" Examples are de Jong et al.'s 2023 *Educational Research Review* paper arguing for combining inquiry and direct instruction, and Kapur's productive failure (book 04), which argues that a *designed* struggle phase **before** instruction can beat instruction-first for conceptual understanding and transfer.

**Limits and critiques**
- The central category, "minimal guidance," is **broad**. It groups pure discovery, which really is weak, with structured inquiry, which is often fine. Much of the fight is about labels.
- It is a synthesis, not a meta-analysis. The strongest controlled evidence concerns worked examples in well-structured domains.
- Outcomes are mostly **near-term content knowledge**. Transfer, motivation, identity and long-run inquiry skill are measured less, and some critics say those are exactly the goals inquiry serves.
- The "epistemology ≠ pedagogy" point is powerful, but it does not settle whether *some* practice of inquiry is needed to learn inquiry. Mehta & Fine (book 09) press this question.

**So what for a school designer**
- Default for new content with novices: **explicit, guided instruction with worked examples**, then faded guidance. This is the lowest-risk default.
- Any discovery, project or inquiry block must specify its **guidance design**: what knowledge is taught before or during it, what scaffolds exist, and when the teacher explains. "Let them figure it out" is not a design.
- Build **expertise-sensitive differentiation**. The same lesson format cannot be optimal for a novice and an advanced student (expertise reversal). This argues for frequent short diagnostics and flexible grouping.
- Do not measure success by visible busyness or student preference. Both can move in the opposite direction from learning.

### 3. Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath (2014) — curiosity and the dopaminergic circuit (+ follow-ups)

**The claim**
- A **state of curiosity** (wanting to know an answer) puts the brain into a mode that improves hippocampus-dependent memory. This mode is driven by the same **dopaminergic reward circuit** involved in anticipating extrinsic rewards.
- Surprisingly, the benefit is not limited to the thing one is curious about. **Unrelated information** encountered during the curious state is also remembered better.

**The method and key experiment**
- Participants first rated many trivia questions for how curious they were about the answer and how confident they were that they knew it. Questions they already knew were removed, and each person's high- and low-curiosity questions were selected.
- In the fMRI scanner: a trivia question appeared, then an **anticipation period** before the answer. During that period a **neutral, unrelated face** was shown, and participants made a simple judgment about it. Then the answer appeared.
- A surprise memory test followed, for the faces and for the trivia answers, both **immediately** and after a **one-day delay**.

**Findings**
- Answers to high-curiosity questions were remembered better. **Faces seen during high-curiosity anticipation were also remembered better**, although the effect was modest and in the delayed test was concentrated in high-confidence recognition.
- During anticipation in high-curiosity states, activity rose in the **midbrain (substantia nigra/ventral tegmental area)** and **nucleus accumbens**, which are reward-related dopaminergic regions. Individual differences in the incidental face benefit were predicted by anticipatory activity in the midbrain and hippocampus and by **functional connectivity** between them.
- Interpretation: curiosity sets up a **prediction and reward state** that improves hippocampal encoding in general, a "rising tide" for a short window.

**The follow-ups (verified)**
- **Stare et al. (2018)** replicated the curiosity benefit for answers and for incidental faces, both immediately and after 12 hours. The benefit persisted, but post-learning **sleep did not increase it**.
- **Murphy et al. (2021)** varied *when* the face appeared. Incidental memory was enhanced **only for faces shown shortly after the question**, not later in the anticipation period or around the answer. Their reading: the *elicitation* of curiosity, meaning the moment the gap opens, drives the incidental benefit, not sustained anticipation or satisfaction. The benefit is a brief window.
- **Keller, Salvi, Leiker, Gruber & Dunsmoor (2024)**: this is the important caution. When the incidental material was **complex, school-like facts** (science, English, history facts) instead of faces, memory for those facts was **worse** after high-curiosity questions than after low-curiosity ones. The authors suggest the open question **pulls attention and processing** toward itself and crowds out unrelated complex material nearby. Curiosity is therefore not a general-purpose memory booster; for demanding material it can act as a **distractor**.
- Background knowledge, from the broader literature: effects on incidental material are generally **small and variable** across studies, while the benefit for the *target* information (the answer to what you are curious about) is more robust. Kang et al. (2009, *Psychological Science*) is an earlier behavioral and fMRI precursor. Gruber & Ranganath's 2019 *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* "PACE" framework (prediction, appraisal, curiosity, exploration) is their later theoretical model.

**Limits and critiques**
- Participants were adults in small fMRI samples, with trivia questions (low-stakes, one-shot facts) and faces. That is far from a K-12 unit on photosynthesis.
- The measures are **correlational in the brain**. Curiosity was selected, not manipulated, and the dopamine role is inferred from BOLD activity in dopaminergic regions, not measured directly.
- The incidental effect is **fragile**: its timing matters (Murphy 2021), its size is small, and it **reverses** for complex academic content (Keller 2024).
- Trivia curiosity is a brief *state*. Classroom interest over weeks involves *trait* curiosity, developing interest, identity and social factors, which these studies do not address. Fandakova & Gruber (2021, *Developmental Science*) report that curiosity and interest affect memory differently in children and adolescents (background knowledge; the paper appeared in search results but was not read in detail).

**So what for a school designer**
- **Open the gap before giving the answer.** Posing a question, prediction or puzzle *before* instruction reliably improves memory for the answer. This is cheap and robust, and it fits pretest and productive failure effects.
- **Do not rely on curiosity to carry unrelated content.** A great hook followed by an unrelated, dense content block may hurt that block. Make the hook *the entry to* the target content, so the answer the student wants is the thing you need them to learn.
- **Timing matters.** Put the most important material close to the moment curiosity is raised and resolved, not at the end of a long wait.
- Curiosity is a design variable for memory, not only a value. That gives the child-centered tradition (book 03) a mechanism, and also gives it limits.

### 4. Posner, Strike, Hewson & Gertzog (1982) — accommodation of a scientific conception

**The claim**
- Learning science often requires more than adding facts. It needs **accommodation**, a Piagetian term: replacing or reorganizing central concepts, as opposed to **assimilation**, which fits new information into the existing structure. The model is explicitly **analogous to Kuhn's paradigm shifts** and Lakatos's research programmes in the history of science.
- Accommodation happens only under four conditions:
  1. **Dissatisfaction** with the existing conception. It must fail to explain something the learner cares about, which is usually an *anomaly*.
  2. The new conception must be **intelligible**: the learner can understand what it says and build a representation of it.
  3. It must be **plausible**: it looks as if it could be true and fits the learner's other beliefs.
  4. It must be **fruitful**: it solves the anomaly *and* opens new possibilities, doing more than the old view.
- A learner's **conceptual ecology** (existing concepts, epistemological commitments such as what counts as a good explanation, metaphysical beliefs, analogies and exemplars) determines whether each condition is met.

**The method**
- A **theoretical paper** grounded in philosophy of science, illustrated with interview data from students learning special relativity. It is a normative account of *what must be true* for rational conceptual change, not a controlled experiment.

**Findings / uptake**
- It became the dominant framework in science education in the 1980s and 1990s and produced the **cognitive conflict** teaching strategy: elicit the student's idea, confront it with a discrepant event, then introduce the scientific idea as the resolution.
- The "intelligible, plausible, fruitful" test is still widely used in teacher education and science-curriculum design.

**Limits and critiques**
- **The authors revised it themselves.** Strike & Posner (1992), "A revisionist theory of conceptual change," concluded that the original was **too rational and too cold**. It assumed learners have well-articulated misconceptions about most topics, which is unrealistic; many are fragmentary or formed on the spot. It also underplayed motivation, affect and social context. They also clarified that the theory is normative and epistemological, not an empirical description of learning.
- **Pintrich, Marx & Boyle (1993)** "Beyond cold conceptual change" (background knowledge) argued that motivation, goals and classroom context decide whether conflict leads to change.
- **Cognitive conflict often fails in practice.** Students explain away anomalies, ignore them, or treat school science as a separate compartment. Limón's 2001 review in *Learning and Instruction* (background knowledge) found mixed results.
- **Knowledge-in-pieces critics** (diSessa) argue that intuitive knowledge is not a coherent theory to be replaced but many small pieces ("p-prims") to be reorganized. On that view, "replacement" is the wrong metaphor.
- Radical, paradigm-shift change is **slow**. A single lesson with a discrepant event rarely does it.

**So what for a school designer**
- For the handful of **deep, known-to-be-hard concepts** in each science and math sequence (force and motion, heat and temperature, evolution, fractions as numbers, density), plan explicitly for accommodation: surface prior ideas, create real dissatisfaction, and make the new idea intelligible (models, analogies), plausible (evidence) and fruitful (show what it predicts).
- Do not expect one "demo" to be enough. Build recurring cycles over weeks and years.
- Formative assessment should target *conceptions*, not just answers. This links directly to Wiliam's hinge questions (book 07).

### 5. Chi — Three types of conceptual change (2008) and ICAP (Chi & Wylie 2014)

**The claim (2008)**
Chi separates three kinds of change, depending on what the learner's prior knowledge is like:
1. **Belief revision.** A single false belief ("the heart oxygenates blood") is contradicted by explicit information and replaced. This is fairly easy. Refutation texts and self-explanation work.
2. **Mental model transformation.** A set of interrelated beliefs forms a flawed but coherent model, such as a single-loop model of circulation. Change needs a series of belief revisions, or a whole-model shift, often supported by **self-explanation** as students repair the model.
3. **Categorical shift.** The hardest case. The concept is placed in the **wrong ontological category**. Chi's central example: students treat heat, electric current, light and diffusion as **substances or entities** ("heat flows into the spoon") when they are **emergent processes**, produced by many agents interacting under no central control. Because category membership decides which properties the mind can attribute, no quantity of facts fixes it. The learner has to (a) *learn the new category* (what emergent processes are like), and (b) *realize the concept belongs there*.
- Earlier roots: Chi, Slotta & de Leeuw (1994) "From things to processes" in *Learning and Instruction*, and Chi (2005) on emergent processes in the *Journal of the Learning Sciences* (background knowledge).

**The claim (ICAP 2014)**
- Engagement modes can be defined by **overt behavior**, and they predict learning in the order **Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive**:
  - **Passive**: receiving, such as listening or reading, with no overt action.
  - **Active**: manipulating, such as highlighting, copying, pausing or rewinding. This activates prior knowledge and stores the material.
  - **Constructive**: generating output beyond the given information, such as self-explaining, drawing a concept map, predicting or comparing. This produces new inferences.
  - **Interactive**: two or more learners co-constructing, with each partner's contributions building on the other's. It needs *both* partners to be constructive; turn-taking that is merely active does not count.
- The paper assigns a cognitive process to each mode and supports the ordering with a re-analysis of many prior studies.

**The method**
- 2008: a theoretical synthesis built on decades of Chi's own studies (protocol analyses of physics experts and novices, self-explanation experiments, emergent-process instruction).
- 2014: a framework paper that classifies existing studies by mode and shows that comparisons mostly follow the ICAP ordering.

**Limits and critiques**
- **Ontological categories are contested.** Gupta, Hammer & Redish (2010) argue that experts often and *productively* reason about processes such as current and energy *as if* they were substances, and that learners' ontologies shift with context. On this view, the "wrong category" is sometimes a useful resource, and instruction should build flexibility, not ban substance talk.
- The evidence for categorical shift instruction (teaching emergence first) is promising but comes from **small studies**. Broad classroom effects are not established.
- **ICAP critiques (Thurn et al. 2023)**: (a) overt behavior is a poor indicator of cognitive engagement, since students can be highly constructive while silently listening and passive while "discussing"; (b) the strict I > C > A > P ordering does not follow from the broader literature, because effects depend on prior knowledge, task quality and what is being learned. The ICAP hypothesis also sits uneasily with worked example and expertise reversal findings: for novices, *studying* a good example (formally "passive or active") can beat generating (constructive).
- ICAP is easy to misuse as "group work > lecture." Chi's own framework places **constructive self-explanation of a clear explanation** above unproductive group chat.

**So what for a school designer**
- Classify your key concepts. **Facts to revise** can be handled quickly with refutation and feedback. **Models to transform** need model-building tasks and self-explanation. **Category errors** (heat, current, evolution by natural selection as an emergent population process, equilibrium, market prices) need dedicated instruction on the category itself, such as emergent systems, often across subjects.
- Use ICAP as a **lesson-design prompt** ("where in this lesson does every student *generate* something?"), not as a ranking of teaching formats. Include constructive moments, such as self-explanation prompts, predictions and explaining to a partner, *inside* explicit instruction.
- Teacher observation rubrics should not score engagement by visible activity alone (Thurn et al.).

**Connects to:** 02-scienceblind (Shtulman's intuitive theories overlap heavily with Chi's categories), 13-learning-scientific-concepts (Amin covers Chi, the ontology debate and newer syntheses), 01-how-learning-happens (includes Chi's self-explanation work, background knowledge), 04-productive-failure (generation before instruction as "constructive"), 09-in-search-of-deeper-learning (engagement quality versus activity).

### 6. Vosniadou & Brewer (1992) — mental models of the earth / framework theory

**The claim**
- Children's knowledge is organized by a **framework theory** of physics: a small set of deep, largely implicit **presuppositions** built from everyday experience, such as "the ground is flat," "unsupported things fall down," and "space is organized in up and down."
- When instruction conflicts with these presuppositions, children do not simply swap old for new. They **combine** the two into **synthetic models**, which are internally consistent compromises.
- Conceptual change is therefore **gradual** and happens as the presuppositions themselves are revised, not through one-shot replacement.

**The method and key experiment**
- Elementary school children in the US (grades 1, 3 and 5) were interviewed with a set of **generative questions**, which cannot be answered from memorized facts. For example: "If you walked for many days in a straight line, where would you end up?" "Is there an edge to the earth?" "Where do people live on the earth?" Children also made **drawings** and **clay models**. Consistent response patterns across questions were used to infer each child's underlying mental model. (Grade levels from background knowledge.)

**Findings**
- Most children held a **consistent** model. The **initial models** were a flat rectangular or disc earth. The **synthetic models** included the **dual earth** (a flat earth where we live plus a round "earth" in the sky), the **hollow sphere** (people live on flat ground *inside* a sphere), and the **flattened sphere**. The scientific **sphere** model became more common with age. Around five distinct model types are commonly reported.
- The synthetic models show children taking the adult statement "the earth is round" and **fitting it into** the unrevised presuppositions of flat ground and absolute "down."
- Later, Vosniadou (1994) and colleagues generalized this into the **framework theory approach**, which is one side of the long debate with diSessa's knowledge-in-pieces.

**Limits and critiques**
- **Method artifact debate.** Nobes, Siegal, Panagiotaki and colleagues (2003 onward) found that with **forced-choice** tasks and ready-made 3D models, instead of open generative questions and drawings, many young children chose the scientific sphere, and their knowledge looked **fragmented** rather than a coherent wrong model. Adults also gave "naive" answers to some of the original questions. Their conclusion: open questions can *create* the appearance of coherent synthetic models.
- Vosniadou's reply (background knowledge): forced-choice tasks overestimate understanding by allowing recognition of taught facts. The debate is partly about **what counts as knowing**.
- Cultural samples differ. Cross-cultural replications (Greece, India, Samoa and others; background knowledge) found both similar and differing patterns.
- **What survives**: children are not blank slates, and instruction can produce **hybrids** that look correct on surface tests but rest on an unchanged framework. Both sides accept this.

**So what for a school designer**
- **Assessment must be generative.** Recall and multiple-choice items can show "the earth is round" while the child believes we live inside a bowl. Use prediction and explanation questions, drawings, and "what would happen if" items to detect synthetic models.
- **Target the presupposition, not the surface fact.** Teach what "down" means (toward the center of the earth) along with "the earth is a sphere." Map the presuppositions behind each hard concept in the curriculum.
- Plan for **years-long progressions**. Framework change in K-5 science is slow and should be sequenced deliberately across grades, not left to each teacher.

## How these six fit together
Think of them as one stack.

**Layer 1: the processor (CLT).** Every new idea passes through a working memory with room for only a few interacting elements. Long-term memory schemas are the only way around that limit. *Constraint: control load and build schemas.*

**Layer 2: the guidance policy (KSC 2006 + expertise reversal).** Because of Layer 1, novices need strong guidance and experts need less. Guidance is a **dial set by expertise**, not an ideological choice. *Constraint: match guidance to what the learner already knows, and fade it.*

**Layer 3: the gate (Gruber et al.).** What reaches long-term memory also depends on the learner's motivational state. Curiosity, meaning an open information gap the learner wants closed, improves encoding of the answer. It is a brief window that can even compete with nearby unrelated material. *Constraint: open the gap on the target content, right before you teach it.*

**Layer 4: the existing structure (Posner, Chi, Vosniadou).** New information is not stored on a blank slate. It meets prior conceptions that are organized to different degrees: single beliefs, flawed models, wrong categories, deep framework presuppositions. The three papers form a sequence. Posner gives the **conditions** for change (dissatisfaction, then intelligible, plausible, fruitful). Chi gives the **types** of change and how hard each is (revise, transform, recategorize). Vosniadou gives the **mechanism of failure**: new knowledge is fused with old presuppositions into synthetic models. *Constraint: diagnose the structure, then target its deepest layer.*

**Where the layers interact (the important part for a designer)**
- **Conceptual change is expensive in working memory.** Holding an old model and a new one, spotting the conflict and rebuilding is high element interactivity. That is a CLT argument for **explicit, well-scaffolded** conceptual change instruction, not pure discovery. It also explains why cognitive conflict alone often fails: the learner lacks the capacity or schemas to resolve it and settles on a synthetic model.
- **Dissatisfaction resembles curiosity.** Posner's first condition and Gruber's information gap are close relatives. A well-built anomaly creates a *wanting to know* state that makes the explanation stick. Keller 2024 is the warning: resolve the gap with the target content, not something else.
- **The productive struggle debate lives here.** KSC say novices should not search. Kapur (04) says a *designed* failure phase before instruction activates prior knowledge (Layer 4) and creates dissatisfaction and curiosity (Layer 3), and pays off *if followed by explicit instruction* (Layer 2). The layered view turns a culture war into an engineering question: for this concept and these learners, does a short generate-first phase add enough activation and dissatisfaction to justify its load?
- **ICAP versus worked examples.** "Constructive > passive" and "worked examples beat problem solving" seem to conflict until you add expertise. Novices need clear models to study, with *constructive* self-explanation of those models; that combination is the reconciliation.
- **Assessment is the common instrument.** Layer 1 needs to know what schemas exist (expertise reversal needs diagnostics). Layer 4 needs generative probes to find synthetic models and category errors. A school that cannot see students' current knowledge cannot apply any of these papers.

## The 8 ideas you must carry out of these papers
1. **Working memory for new material is tiny; long-term memory is huge.** Instruction exists to build organized knowledge in long-term memory. Design that wastes working memory is the most common and most avoidable failure.
2. **Guidance should be set by expertise.** Novices learn best from explicit instruction and worked examples. The same support becomes a burden for experts, so fade it (expertise reversal).
3. **"Minimal guidance" and "inquiry" are not the same thing.** Well-scaffolded inquiry and designed struggle followed by instruction have support. Unguided discovery for novices does not.
4. **Prior conceptions are structured, not blank.** Students fuse instruction with deep presuppositions into synthetic models that can pass surface tests. Hard concepts need targeted, long-run conceptual change design.
5. **Not all misconceptions are equal.** Revise false beliefs quickly, transform flawed models with self-explanation, and treat category errors (process mistaken for substance) with dedicated instruction on the category.
6. **Curiosity improves memory for what you are curious about.** Open the question before giving the answer. The spillover to unrelated material is small, short-lived, and can reverse for complex academic content.
7. **Change requires dissatisfaction plus an intelligible, plausible, fruitful alternative.** A discrepant demo without a clear explanation, or an explanation without dissatisfaction, usually fails.
8. **Visible activity is not cognitive engagement.** Busyness, preference and "interactive" formats are poor proxies. Look for students generating inferences about the target content.

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **Working memory** — the limited, short-duration processor for new information — every added element in a lesson competes for it.
- **Schema** — an organized knowledge chunk in long-term memory, treated as one element — the real product of schooling.
- **Element interactivity** — how many elements must be processed together — decides intrinsic load; hard for novices, easy once chunked.
- **Intrinsic / extraneous / (germane) load** — load from the content, from poor design, and (formerly) from schema building — audit materials for extraneous load.
- **Worked example effect** — studying solutions beats solving, for novices — default for introducing procedures.
- **Expertise reversal** — novice-helpful supports hurt experts — argues for diagnostics and fading.
- **Guidance fading** — worked example, then completion problem, then independent problem — the standard progression.
- **Biologically primary / secondary knowledge** — evolved-and-easy versus cultural-and-taught — why reading and algebra need explicit teaching.
- **Information gap / curiosity state** — wanting to know an answer you lack — open it right before teaching the target.
- **Accommodation vs. assimilation** — restructuring versus adding — which kind a unit needs changes the design.
- **Conceptual ecology** — the learner's beliefs, epistemic standards and exemplars that decide what is plausible — a new idea must fit it or change it.
- **Intelligible / plausible / fruitful** — Posner's tests for a new conception — a checklist for any explanation of a hard concept.
- **Ontological category / categorical shift** — the kind of thing a concept is (entity versus process); moving it — the hardest type of change.
- **Emergent process** — an outcome of many interacting agents with no central control — heat, diffusion, evolution, traffic, markets.
- **ICAP** — Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive — a prompt for generation, not a format ranking.
- **Framework theory** — deep implicit presuppositions (flat ground, absolute down) — the real target of instruction.
- **Synthetic model** — a hybrid of new instruction and old presuppositions — passes recall tests, fails prediction tests.
- **Knowledge-in-pieces** — diSessa's rival view: intuitive knowledge as fragments — changes the metaphor from "replace" to "reorganize."

## Evidence strength & limits
- **Robust**: limited working memory for new information; the worked example effect for novices; expertise reversal as a general pattern; curiosity improving memory for the *target* answer; persistence and resistance of intuitive conceptions; hybrid or synthetic answers after instruction.
- **Moderate**: split attention, redundancy and modality effects in real classrooms (effects shrink outside the lab); categorical shift instruction; the ICAP ordering as a *tendency*.
- **Contested**: whether minimal guidance critiques apply to structured inquiry and PBL (it depends on definitions); coherent framework theories versus knowledge in pieces (partly a methods artifact debate); strict ICAP ordering and its behavioral indicators; the extent to which curiosity helps *incidental* learning (small, timing-dependent, reversed for complex facts).
- **Argued more than shown**: KSC's claim about the epistemology/pedagogy confusion; Posner's four conditions (normative, not tested as a causal model); the primary/secondary knowledge distinction in CLT 2019.
- **General limits**: many samples are adults or university students, lab tasks and short timescales. K-12, multi-year, whole-school evidence is thin for all six. Treat them as **constraints on design**, not as tested school models.

## Institutional design implications
- Adopt a **school-wide instructional default** of explicit instruction with worked examples for new content, with planned guidance fading. **[E]**
- Require every project, inquiry or PBL unit to document its **guidance plan** (what is taught explicitly, when, and with which scaffolds) before approval. **[H]** (The principle is evidence-based; the review process is an institutional hypothesis.)
- Run a **materials design review** (split attention, redundancy, extraneous load) on core curriculum, as in code review. **[E]** for the effects; **[H]** that the review process improves outcomes at scale.
- Use **frequent, short diagnostic probes** to set guidance levels and grouping (expertise reversal). **[E]** for the principle; **[H]** for the specific cadence.
- Build a **"hard concepts register"** for each subject: known misconceptions, their type (belief, model, category) and presuppositions, and a multi-year progression for each. **[H]**
- Make **generative, prediction-based assessment items** standard for science and math concepts, so synthetic models are caught. **[E]** that surface items miss them; **[H]** for school-level impact.
- Teach **emergent processes** as a cross-curricular idea (science, social studies, economics) before or alongside heat, diffusion and evolution. **[H]**
- Train teachers to **open the question before the answer** (predict, pose, pretest) and to link hooks to the target content. **[E]** for target memory; the caution against unrelated hooks is **[E]**, but from a single 2024 study.
- **Do not** score teacher effectiveness on visible student activity or student preference. **[E]** that these are poor learning proxies.
- Treat **curiosity and children's intellectual lives** as ends worth protecting in themselves, not only as memory tools. **[V]**
- Decide explicitly how much curriculum time goes to learning *how to inquire* (a goal KSC's evidence does not measure) versus acquiring content efficiently. **[V]**, informed by **[E]** that inquiry skill itself needs knowledge and guidance.

## Retrieval practice
1. Name the three types of cognitive load and explain how the 2019 update changed one of them.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Intrinsic (element interactivity of the material), extraneous (from poor design) and germane (schema building). In 2019, germane load was redefined as the working memory resources devoted to intrinsic load, not a separate additive load.</details>

2. Why does solving conventional problems with means-ends analysis sometimes fail to produce learning, even when students get the right answers?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Means-ends analysis requires holding the current state, the goal, the differences and the subgoals in working memory. That uses the capacity needed to notice and store the problem's structure, so the solution is found but no schema is built.</details>

3. A school's middle school math department reports that its strongest students find worked-example lessons "boring and slow," and their gains have flattened. Which effect is this, and what should change?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The expertise reversal effect: for more expert learners, worked examples are redundant and add load. Fade to completion problems and independent problem solving for those students, using quick diagnostics to decide who moves on.</details>

4. Spot the misconception: "Kirschner, Sweller & Clark showed that project-based learning doesn't work."
<details><summary>Answer</summary>They argued that minimal guidance fails for novices. Critics showed that well-run PBL and inquiry are heavily scaffolded, and KSC's rejoinder mainly says heavily guided versions work *because* of the guidance. The claim concerns guidance levels, not the project format itself.</details>

5. In Gruber et al. (2014), what was the evidence that curiosity affects memory beyond the answer itself, and which brain circuit was involved?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Unrelated faces shown during high-curiosity anticipation were remembered better than faces shown during low-curiosity anticipation. Anticipatory activity in the SN/VTA (midbrain) and nucleus accumbens, and midbrain-hippocampus connectivity, predicted the benefit.</details>

6. A teacher opens with a gripping mystery question about volcanoes, then spends ten minutes on unrelated grammar facts before returning to volcanoes. Based on the follow-up studies, what is the risk?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Keller et al. (2024) found that high-curiosity states impaired memory for complex, unrelated school facts presented nearby, probably because the open question captures attention. Murphy et al. (2021) showed any incidental benefit is limited to just after curiosity is raised. The grammar content is likely to suffer; the hook should lead straight into the target content.</details>

7. List Posner et al.'s four conditions and explain why a single discrepant-event demonstration often fails.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Dissatisfaction, intelligible, plausible, fruitful. A demo may create dissatisfaction but provides no intelligible, plausible, fruitful replacement. Students may also explain the anomaly away or keep school knowledge in a separate compartment. The 1992 revision adds that motivation and context matter and that many misconceptions are not well articulated.</details>

8. Classify each by Chi's type: (a) "The heart oxygenates blood"; (b) a single-loop model of circulation; (c) "heat is a substance that flows into cold things."
<details><summary>Answer</summary>(a) Belief revision; (b) mental model transformation; (c) categorical shift (heat is an emergent process of molecular interactions, not a substance). Note that Gupta, Hammer & Redish argue that substance-like reasoning is sometimes productive even for experts.</details>

9. A child says the earth is round and also that people live "on the flat part inside." What is this, what caused it, and how would you detect it at scale?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>A synthetic model (the hollow sphere): instruction ("the earth is round") fused with unrevised presuppositions (flat ground, absolute down). Detect it with generative questions (walk in a straight line for days — where do you end up?), drawings or prediction items, not recall or recognition items.</details>

10. Explain how CLT, Posner and Gruber together can justify a short "predict first" phase before explicit instruction, and what limits that justification.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Predicting activates prior conceptions (making them available to confront), creates dissatisfaction when the prediction fails (Posner), and opens an information gap that improves memory for the explanation (Gruber). CLT limits it: the phase should be short and low-search for novices, followed promptly by explicit instruction on the target content, and suited to learners' expertise. Otherwise it becomes minimally guided search.</details>

## Common misreadings of these papers
- **"CLT says lecture is best."** CLT says reduce extraneous load and build schemas. Explicit instruction can be highly interactive, and CLT supports fading guidance as expertise grows.
- **"KSC proves inquiry and PBL fail."** The paper targets *minimal* guidance for *novices*. Scaffolded inquiry, and struggle followed by instruction, are separate questions.
- **"Curiosity makes you remember everything."** The spillover to unrelated material is small and timing-bound, and it reversed for complex academic facts in 2024. The robust effect is on the answer you are curious about.
- **"The brain scans prove dopamine causes curiosity learning."** The fMRI data are correlational with dopaminergic regions. The causal dopamine story is plausible and consistent with animal work, but it was not directly tested in these studies.
- **"Conceptual change = cognitive conflict."** Conflict is one condition. Without an intelligible, plausible, fruitful alternative, and without motivation, it often fails; its own authors revised the model in 1992.
- **"ICAP means group work beats everything."** Interactive requires *both* partners to be constructive, and the strict ordering is contested. Self-explaining a good worked example can beat unproductive discussion.
- **"Children have coherent wrong theories about everything."** Coherence is disputed (Nobes et al.; diSessa). The robust part is that instruction can produce hybrids hidden by surface tests.
- **"These are school findings."** Most evidence comes from labs, adults, short timescales or single topics. They set constraints for school design; they are not proven school models.
