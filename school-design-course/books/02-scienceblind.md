## The book in one sentence
Children build coherent, causal "intuitive theories" of the physical and biological world long
before school. Those theories are systematically wrong in the same ways that pre-scientific
cultures were wrong, and they are never erased: science education builds a second theory on top
that has to *out-compete* the first every time it is used.

## The book in one paragraph
Shtulman, a cognitive developmental psychologist at Occidental College, goes through twelve
domains: matter, energy, gravity, motion, cosmos, earth, life, growth, inheritance, illness,
adaptation and ancestry. For each one he describes the intuitive theory children form, the
scientific theory that replaces it, the history of scientists holding the intuitive view, and the
experimental evidence that adults (including science teachers and professors) still carry the old
theory underneath. His central argument is that misconceptions are not isolated facts that are
missing or wrong. They are parts of a theory: an explanatory framework with its own ontology
(what kinds of things exist) and causal mechanisms. Changing that takes *conceptual change*:
differentiating concepts that were merged (heat versus temperature, weight versus density),
merging concepts that were separate (plants and animals into "living things"), and moving
concepts into new ontological categories (heat as a process, not a substance). Being told the
right answer, or learning to repeat it, does not do this. The consequence for education is that
science teaching should start from students' existing theories, expose them and compete against
them directly, give students a better explanatory mechanism rather than just the correct
conclusion, and treat intuitions as permanent competitors that must be managed, not deleted.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
**"How do I tell whether a student's mental model has actually changed, rather than just their
vocabulary, and how should the curriculum be sequenced so that it does change?"**

This is the Minds-level book on *prior knowledge as an obstacle*. How Learning Happens and
cognitive load theory treat prior knowledge mostly as an asset (schemas make learning easier).
Scienceblind is the necessary counterweight. Some prior knowledge is a competing theory, and
adding more correct information to it produces a hybrid ("synthetic model") or a student who
gives the right answer on the test and the wrong answer in life. For a school designer this
affects curriculum sequencing, assessment design, teacher knowledge, and what you count as
"mastery."

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

Each domain chapter below uses the same five slots: **Intuitive theory → Scientific theory → Why
the gap persists → Key studies → Teaching implications**.

### Ch 1 — Why We Get the World Wrong
- **Core claims**
  - Intuitive theories are *theories*. They are coherent, causal, predictive, and built from
    everyday perception, so they are not random errors. They work well enough for daily life,
    which is why nobody is pushed to revise them.
  - Children's theories often resemble earlier scientific theories: impetus physics, caloric
    heat, vitalism, Lamarckian inheritance, essentialist species. So the problem is not
    childishness. Some ideas are simply hard to reach without special tools and data.
  - Learning science is **theory change, not fact accumulation.** New facts are absorbed into the
    old framework unless the framework itself is challenged.
  - **The old theory survives.** Scientific knowledge sits on top of intuition and suppresses it
    rather than replacing it. Under time pressure, cognitive load, aging or brain injury, the
    intuition comes back.
- **Key studies**
  - Shtulman & Valcarcel (2012, *Cognition*). About 150 college-educated adults verified about
    200 statements as fast as possible. Statements whose intuitive and scientific truth values
    *conflict* (e.g., "the moon has gravity" or "air is made of matter", where intuition says
    no) were answered more slowly and less accurately than statements where they agree. This
    held across all ten domains tested. It is the book's signature finding.
  - Shtulman & Harrington (2016, *Topics in Cognitive Science*) found the same conflict cost in
    older adults, decades after schooling.
  - Goldberg & Thompson-Schill (2009, *Psychological Science*): under speeded conditions, even
    biology professors were slower and less accurate at classifying plants as "alive."
  - Kelemen, Rottman & Seston (2013, *JEP: General*): under time pressure, professional physical
    scientists endorsed unwarranted teleological explanations (e.g., "trees produce oxygen so that
    animals can breathe") more often than when unhurried.
  - Neuroimaging work (e.g., Masson, Potvin, Riopel & Brault Foisy 2014; Brault Foisy et al.
    2015) shows experts activating regions associated with inhibitory control when evaluating
    intuitively plausible but wrong physics. This is consistent with suppression, not deletion.
    (background; the book cites work in this vein)
- **So-what for a school designer.** "Mastery" of a science concept has two layers: (1) can
  produce the correct explanation, and (2) the correct model wins when intuition competes. Most
  tests measure only (1). Your assessment system has to probe (2).

### Ch 2 — Matter
- **Intuitive theory.** Matter is what you can see, touch and feel the weight of. Air, gases,
  and very small pieces are "not really matter." Children do not separate weight from density
  (heaviness for size): a tiny piece of Styrofoam "weighs nothing," and if you keep cutting
  something you eventually get pieces with no weight. Matter is continuous stuff, not particles.
- **Scientific theory.** All matter is made of particles (atoms/molecules) with mass. Mass is
  conserved when matter is divided or changes state. Weight and density are distinct quantities.
  Gases are matter.
- **Why the gap persists.** Perception gives no information about particles. Tiny weights are
  below sensory threshold. "Heavy" in everyday language merges weight and density. Particle
  theory needs an unobservable model that must be taught.
- **Key studies.** Carol Smith, Susan Carey & Marianne Wiser (1985) and Smith's later work
  (Smith 2007; Smith, Wiser, Anderson & Krajcik 2006, learning progression for matter). Children
  judge small pieces of Styrofoam to weigh nothing and have an undifferentiated weight/density
  concept. Interventions using visual "dots-per-box" models of density helped students
  differentiate the two. (background detail; the weight/density case is central to the chapter)
- **Teaching implications.** Teach a *model* (particles; density as crowdedness) rather than a
  definition. Build a learning progression across grades (weight → volume → density →
  particles) instead of introducing "atoms" as a fact in one unit. Diagnostic: "If you cut a grain
  of rice in half again and again, will the pieces ever weigh nothing?"; "Does air weigh
  anything?"

### Ch 3 — Energy
- **Intuitive theory.** Heat, sound and light are *substances* that flow, fill, get trapped and
  get used up. "Cold" is a separate substance that gets in. Heat and temperature are the same
  thing: a big pot of warm water has "more temperature."
- **Scientific theory.** Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.
  Temperature relates to the average kinetic energy of particles. Sound is a pressure wave, a
  process moving through a medium, not a thing that travels.
- **Why the gap persists.** Language ("close the door, you're letting the cold in," "heat
  rises") encodes the substance view. The substance view predicts everyday events well. The
  correct view belongs to a different *ontological category*: an emergent process, not a thing.
  Michelene Chi argues this category shift is the hardest kind of conceptual change.
- **Key studies.**
  - Wiser & Carey (1983). The seventeenth-century Florentine Experimenters (Accademia del
    Cimento) themselves failed to separate heat from temperature. This is Shtulman's key
    historical parallel: the student's confusion was once the scientist's confusion.
  - Reiner, Slotta, Chi & Resnick (2000) on substance-based conceptions of heat, light, current.
  - Chi (2005) and Chi et al. (2012) on emergent-process misconceptions (e.g., diffusion). Slotta
    & Chi (2006): teaching the "emergent process" category first improved later physics
    learning. (background; consistent with the chapter's argument)
- **Teaching implications.** Name the ontological shift explicitly ("heat is not stuff, it is
  what happens when…"). Hold off on terms that invite substance thinking until the process model
  is in place. Diagnostic: "Why does a metal spoon feel colder than a wooden spoon in the same
  room?" (Intuitive answer: metal is colder or "holds cold." Scientific answer: same
  temperature; metal conducts heat away from your hand faster.)

### Ch 4 — Gravity
- **Intuitive theory.** Objects fall because they are heavy and unsupported. Heavier objects
  fall faster. Gravity is a property of heavy objects or of Earth's surface, needs air to work,
  and is absent in space. "Down" is an absolute direction, not "toward the centre of mass."
- **Scientific theory.** Gravity is a mutual attraction between all masses. In the absence of
  air resistance, all objects fall at the same acceleration. Astronauts in orbit are in free fall,
  not in a gravity-free zone.
- **Why the gap persists.** Air resistance really does make feathers fall more slowly. Everyday
  experience confounds weight with drag. "Heavy" and "falls" are learned together. Infants and
  toddlers show a strong "gravity bias."
- **Key studies.** Bruce Hood (1995), the tubes task: toddlers search directly below the drop
  point for a ball even though it rolled through an opaque curved tube to a different spot.
  Aristotle's heavier-falls-faster view lasted about 2,000 years until Galileo's challenge. Adults
  in speeded verification hesitate on "the moon has gravity" and similar items (Shtulman &
  Valcarcel 2012). (Hood: background knowledge of the literature; very likely discussed)
- **Teaching implications.** Before explaining, get students to make a committed prediction
  (drop two balls of different mass). Separate "weight" from "air resistance" as two causes. Use
  the Moon and orbit as transfer tests. Diagnostic: "Is there gravity on the International Space
  Station? Why do astronauts float?"

### Ch 5 — Motion
- **Intuitive theory.** "Impetus": pushing an object gives it an internal force that carries it
  forward and gradually runs out. Anything moving must have a force acting in its direction of
  motion. A ball leaving a curved tube keeps curving. A dropped object falls straight down from a
  moving carrier.
- **Scientific theory.** Newton's laws. An object in motion stays in motion unless a net force
  acts on it. Force changes motion (acceleration); it does not keep motion going. Friction is the
  hidden force that makes things stop.
- **Why the gap persists.** On Earth friction is everywhere, so "things stop unless pushed" is
  true to experience. Impetus also predicts many everyday events correctly. Medieval natural
  philosophers (Philoponus, Buridan) held impetus theory explicitly.
- **Key studies.**
  - McCloskey, Caramazza & Green (1980, *Science*): college students, including many who had
    taken physics, predicted that a ball leaving a spiral tube would keep curving.
  - McCloskey (1983) described intuitive physics as a close match to medieval impetus theory.
  - Clement (1982): engineering students who had passed mechanics said a force acts upward on a
    coin that is rising after being tossed.
  - Halloun & Hestenes (1985) and the Force Concept Inventory (Hestenes, Wells & Swackhamer 1992).
    Traditional lecture courses produced small gains on conceptual items even when students could
    solve textbook problems. Hake (1998): interactive-engagement courses roughly doubled
    normalized gains. (FCI/Hake: background; the canonical evidence behind this chapter)
- **Teaching implications.** This is the clearest case of **"can repeat, hasn't changed."**
  Students recite F = ma and solve quantitative problems while predicting impetus-style
  trajectories. Assess with qualitative prediction items (the concept-inventory style), not
  calculation. Use elicit → confront → resolve sequences (predict, observe, explain) with
  low-friction demonstrations (air tracks, videos of space).

### Ch 6 — Cosmos
- **Intuitive theory.** The Earth is flat, or is round in a way that preserves flatness: a
  disc, a hollow sphere with people living inside on a flat floor, or "two earths" (a round
  planet up in the sky and the flat one we live on). The Sun moves around the Earth. Seasons are
  caused by the Earth being closer to the Sun in summer.
- **Scientific theory.** Earth is a sphere in a heliocentric system. Gravity pulls towards its
  centre, so "down" is relative. Day and night come from rotation. Seasons come from axial tilt,
  which changes the angle and duration of sunlight.
- **Why the gap persists.** Direct perception says the ground is flat and the Sun moves. Adults
  *tell* children "the Earth is round," and children fit that fact into their flat-earth theory,
  producing hybrids. The distance explanation of seasons is intuitive because closer to a fire
  means hotter.
- **Key studies.**
  - Vosniadou & Brewer (1992, *Cognitive Psychology*): children's drawings and answers revealed
    stable **synthetic models** (dual earth, hollow sphere, flattened sphere). Children blend the
    culturally transmitted "round" with the intuitive "flat and supported." Vosniadou & Brewer
    (1994) found the same for day/night.
  - *A Private Universe* (Schneps & Sadler, 1987 documentary): most Harvard graduates and faculty
    interviewed at commencement explained seasons by Earth-Sun distance. (background; widely cited
    in this literature)
- **Teaching implications.** Synthetic models are the key diagnostic concept for the whole
  school. A child who says "the Earth is round" may hold a hollow-sphere model. **Ask generative
  questions** that the memorized sentence cannot answer: "If you walked in a straight line for
  many days, what would happen? Could you fall off the edge?" "Draw where people live on the
  Earth." "It's summer here; what season is it in Australia, and why?"

### Ch 7 — Earth
- **Intuitive theory.** The ground is fixed and permanent. Continents do not move, and mountains
  were always there. Earth's history is short, on a human time scale. On climate: weather and
  climate are the same thing; global warming is caused by the ozone hole or by pollution
  "trapping heat" in a vague way; a cold winter contradicts it.
- **Scientific theory.** Plate tectonics: the lithosphere moves on a convecting mantle over deep
  time. Climate change comes from the greenhouse effect: greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit
  outgoing infrared radiation.
- **Why the gap persists.** Geological change is imperceptible at human time scales, and deep
  time resists intuition. Wegener's continental drift was rejected for decades, partly because
  it had no mechanism. The greenhouse mechanism is invisible, involves energy of a different
  wavelength, and is easily confused with other atmospheric problems. Climate involves
  stocks and flows and statistics, not individual events.
- **Key studies.** Ranney & Clark (2016, *Topics in Cognitive Science*): very few US adults could
  explain the greenhouse mechanism, and a short (roughly 400-word) mechanistic explanation
  increased acceptance of human-caused warming across the political spectrum. This supports
  Shtulman's recurring point that **understanding the mechanism drives acceptance** more than
  exhortation does. (Ranney & Clark: background knowledge; it fits the chapter closely) Sterman &
  Sweeney (2007) on stock-flow failures in reasoning about CO2 accumulation (background; may not
  be in the book).
- **Teaching implications.** Teach mechanisms, not conclusions. Explicitly address scale (time
  and quantity) with models and analogies. Diagnostic: "If emissions stopped rising and stayed
  flat, would CO2 in the atmosphere stop rising?" (No: the stock still grows while inflow exceeds
  outflow.)

### Ch 8 — Life
- **Intuitive theory.** Living means moving on its own or being active. So the sun, rivers and
  cars are "alive" and plants are not (Piaget's animism). Life is a vital force or energy that is
  absorbed from food and can run out. Death is like sleep or a journey, reversible and not
  universal, until children build a biological theory.
- **Scientific theory.** Life is a set of biological processes (metabolism, growth,
  reproduction, homeostasis) shared by plants, animals and microbes. Death is the irreversible
  breakdown of those processes.
- **Why the gap persists.** Self-directed motion is the most obvious perceptual cue to life. Plants
  do not move on human time scales. Merging plants and animals into one category "living thing"
  requires *coalescence*, a theory-based category that perception does not supply.
- **Key studies.**
  - Carey (1985), *Conceptual Change in Childhood*: children's biology emerges from a psychology
    of behaviour and is restructured roughly between ages 4 and 10.
  - Inagaki & Hatano (e.g., 1993, 2002): young children's "vitalist" biology holds that organs
    take in vital power from food.
  - Slaughter & Lyons (2003): teaching preschoolers a simple body-as-machine model of life
    (organs keep you alive) improved their understanding of death (irreversibility,
    inevitability). Mechanism supports a concept.
  - Goldberg & Thompson-Schill (2009): speeded "alive?" judgments show the plant penalty persists
    in biologists.
- **Teaching implications.** Teach "life" as a mechanism (the body as a system that keeps
  itself going) rather than as a list of traits (MRS GREN) that can be memorized without any
  change of model. Diagnostic: "Is a seed alive? Is fire alive? Why or why not?" Listen for
  "because it moves or grows" versus "because it takes in energy to maintain itself."

### Ch 9 — Growth
- **Intuitive theory.** You grow because you eat, and the food somehow becomes you, with its
  properties transmitted ("you are what you eat"). Plants get their food, and their mass, from
  the soil. Aging is wearing out or running down, like a machine or a battery.
- **Scientific theory.** Growth is cell division and the building of new tissue from nutrients
  broken into molecular parts. A plant's mass comes mostly from carbon dioxide in the air, fixed
  through photosynthesis. Aging is the accumulation of cellular damage and changes that are
  regulated genetically.
- **Why the gap persists.** Digestion and photosynthesis happen at invisible scales, and the idea
  that a solid tree is made out of gas is deeply counterintuitive. Holistic "food essence"
  reasoning is the default.
- **Key studies.** Van Helmont's willow experiment (1600s): the tree gained mass while the soil
  barely changed, and he concluded that the mass came from *water*, the right data with the wrong
  theory. MIT and Harvard graduates in the *Minds of Our Own* / *Lessons from Thin Air* videos
  said a tree's mass came from the soil (background). Nemeroff & Rozin (1989) show "you are what
  you eat" reasoning in adults (background). Inagaki & Hatano show vitalist growth reasoning.
- **Teaching implications.** Cross-disciplinary sequencing: photosynthesis needs particle
  theory of matter (Ch 2) and "gases are matter" first. A school that teaches photosynthesis
  before students accept that air has mass is building on sand. Diagnostic: "A seed weighing 1 g
  grows into a 500 kg tree. Where did the 500 kg come from?"

### Ch 10 — Inheritance
- **Intuitive theory.** Offspring resemble parents because of an inherited *essence* passed
  through blood. Traits acquired during life (strength, scars, learned skills) can be passed on
  (Lamarckian inheritance). Young children also expect adopted children to resemble adoptive
  parents or think beliefs are inherited like eye colour. Genes are treated as the essence itself:
  deterministic, one gene for one trait.
- **Scientific theory.** Genes are DNA sequences that code for proteins. Traits come from
  interactions among many genes and with the environment. Acquired traits are not inherited
  genetically (with epigenetic nuances). Inheritance is particulate, not blending.
- **Why the gap persists.** **Psychological essentialism** (Susan Gelman) is an early, robust
  cognitive default: categories have hidden essences that cause their surface features.
  "Genes" is often just a new word attached to the old essence concept.
- **Key studies.**
  - Solomon, Johnson, Zaitchik & Carey (1996): preschoolers often did not distinguish biological
    from adoptive transmission of traits. The distinction emerges around ages 6-7. Springer & Keil
    (1989) found an early preference for biological inheritance of some traits, and this debate
    is part of the literature.
  - Weissman & Kalish (1999) and others on children's Lamarckian reasoning.
  - Meyer, Leslie, Gelman & Stilwell (2013): adults believed an organ recipient might take on the
    donor's personality traits. Essentialism persists. Dar-Nimrod & Heine (2011) on genetic
    essentialism. (background; illustrative of the chapter's claims)
- **Teaching implications.** Watch for **terminology masking** where "genes" or "DNA" is a new
  label on the old essence theory. Diagnostic: "A mouse has its tail cut off. Will its babies
  have short tails?" "Two parents who are both good at piano: will their baby be born knowing
  how to play?" "Is there one gene for intelligence?"

### Ch 11 — Illness
- **Intuitive theory.** Illness is caused by being cold or wet, by bad behaviour (immanent
  justice: you get sick because you were bad), by imbalance, or by contact with anything "gross."
  Germs are treated as tiny inert contaminants, dirt, rather than living organisms that reproduce.
  Contagion applies to every illness, including non-infectious ones.
- **Scientific theory.** Germ theory: many diseases are caused by microbes, living things that
  reproduce inside the host. The immune system responds. Vaccines train that response. Many
  illnesses are not infectious (genetic, chronic, lifestyle).
- **Why the gap persists.** Microbes are invisible. Contamination and disgust intuitions are
  evolutionarily old and overgeneralize. Humoral medicine (balance of the four humours) lasted
  for centuries, and pre-germ-theory physicians resisted handwashing (Semmelweis). Supernatural
  and biological explanations coexist rather than replace each other.
- **Key studies.**
  - Kalish (1996, 1999) on children's contagion and contamination reasoning.
  - Au, Chan, Chan, Cheung, Ho & Ip (2008), "Folkbiology meets microbiology": teaching children
    that germs are *living things that reproduce* (a causal model) improved their reasoning and
    hygiene behaviour more than teaching rules such as "wash your hands."
  - Legare & Gelman (2008): adults in South Africa endorsed biological and supernatural
    (witchcraft) explanations of AIDS at the same time. Coexistence, not replacement.
  - Nyhan, Reifler, Richey & Freed (2014): pro-vaccine messages did not raise vaccination intent
    among the most hesitant parents and sometimes lowered it. (background; relevant to the
    chapter's vaccine discussion)
- **Teaching implications.** The Au et al. result is a model for the whole book: **a mechanistic
  theory beats a rule**. Teach *why* (germs are alive, multiply, travel on hands) and behaviour
  follows. Diagnostic: "Can you catch a sprained ankle from a friend? Can you catch a cold from
  being cold? Why do you need to wash for 20 seconds rather than just rinse?"

### Ch 12 — Adaptation
- **Intuitive theory.** Species have fixed essences, and all members are basically the same.
  Evolution is **transformational**: the *whole population* changes together because individuals
  need to (need-based, goal-directed). Acquired changes are passed on (the giraffe stretches its
  neck). Traits exist *in order to* serve a purpose (teleology).
- **Scientific theory.** Evolution is **variational**. Individuals vary. Variation is heritable
  and arises randomly with respect to need. Individuals with advantageous variants leave more
  offspring. Population-level trait frequencies shift over generations. There is no goal.
- **Why the gap persists.** Essentialism blocks the idea that within-species variation is the
  raw material. "Promiscuous teleology" (Kelemen) makes purpose-based explanations feel
  satisfying. Language ("the moth evolved to blend in," "adapt") encodes need-based change.
  Population thinking is statistical and not natural.
- **Key studies.**
  - Shtulman (2006, *Cognitive Psychology*): adults' evolutionary reasoning was coded as
    transformational or variational across six phenomena (variation, inheritance, adaptation,
    domestication, speciation, extinction). Most adults, including many who *accepted* evolution
    and had studied biology, reasoned transformationally. **Acceptance of evolution was barely
    related to understanding of it.**
  - Shtulman & Calabi (2013) and related work: students' understanding after college biology
    remained largely transformational. (background detail; the finding pattern is well
    established)
  - Kelemen (1999) on promiscuous teleology in children. Kelemen, Emmons, Seston Schillaci &
    Ganea (2014, *Psychological Science*): a picture-storybook about fictional "piloses" taught
    5-8-year-olds natural selection with variation, differential survival and multi-generation
    change. Children generalized the mechanism to new animals and retained it months later.
    Evidence that the mechanism can be taught early, *before* teleology hardens.
  - Bishop & Anderson (1990) on college students' natural selection misconceptions (background).
- **Teaching implications.** Sequencing argument: teach variational natural selection in early
  primary, not first in high school, while essentialist/teleological reasoning is less
  entrenched. Diagnostic items that separate "can repeat" from "model changed": "Cheetahs got
  faster over time. Explain how, starting from a population of slower cheetahs." Transformational
  answer: "they needed to run faster, so they got faster." Variational answer: "some were already
  faster; they caught more prey and had more cubs…" Note that both students may use the phrase
  "natural selection."

### Ch 13 — Ancestry
- **Intuitive theory.** Species are fixed kinds created separately or appearing as they are.
  When people accept evolution, they picture it as a **linear ladder** ("humans evolved from
  chimpanzees," "the missing link") rather than a **branching tree** of common ancestry. More
  "advanced" species sit at the top.
- **Scientific theory.** All living things share common ancestors. Speciation is branching.
  Living species are cousins, not ancestors of one another. Evolutionary relatedness is read
  from branch points (most recent common ancestor), not from the order of tips or from surface
  similarity.
- **Why the gap persists.** Essentialism, plus deep time, plus linear "march of progress"
  imagery. People read phylogenetic trees wrongly (by the order of tips, or by counting nodes).
  Creationist beliefs are also culturally transmitted and fit children's intuitive preference for
  intentional origins.
- **Key studies.** Evans (2001): children in both fundamentalist and non-fundamentalist
  communities in middle childhood favour creationist origins explanations, which shows an
  intuitive pull and not just family transmission. Tree-thinking research (Baum, Smith &
  Donovan 2005, *Science*; Catley, Novick and colleagues; Gregory 2008) shows systematic
  misreading of cladograms. (background; representative of the chapter's evidence)
- **Teaching implications.** Teach tree-reading as a skill with explicit practice. Diagnostic:
  "Which is more closely related to a frog: a lizard or a fish?" (read from the tree) and "Did
  humans evolve from monkeys?" Also separate *understanding* from *belief*: assess whether
  students can reason with the model and do not demand that they profess acceptance (see
  Institutional implications).

### Ch 14 — How to Get the World Right
- **Core claims**
  - Intuitive theories will not go away. The realistic goal is for students to hold a scientific
    theory strong enough to **override** intuition, and to know when to distrust their gut.
  - Effective teaching gets into the details of the specific concepts that must be
    **differentiated** (heat/temperature, weight/density), **coalesced** (plant + animal =
    living thing), **reanalyzed** (heat as process), or **discarded** (impetus, vital force).
    Generic "critical thinking" instruction does not do this.
  - Provide **mechanisms** and **models**, not just conclusions. Mechanistic knowledge supports
    both understanding and acceptance (greenhouse effect, germs, natural selection).
  - Use the **history of science** as a teaching tool: students' ideas are often the ideas that
    serious scientists once held, which is both respectful and diagnostic. Showing how those
    ideas were overturned models the process of theory change.
  - Science literacy means understanding science as a way of revising theories. Knowing a list
    of facts is not enough, and it offers little protection against misinformation.
- **So-what.** Curriculum should be organized around a finite, known set of high-leverage
  conceptual changes per domain, with explicit diagnostics for each.

## The core distinction: "can repeat the explanation" vs. "the model has changed"

This is the most important idea for a school designer to take from the book, so it gets its own
section.

| | Student A: *can repeat* | Student B: *model changed* |
|---|---|---|
| Familiar question ("What causes seasons?") | Correct ("the Earth's tilt") | Correct |
| Novel / transfer question ("Why is it summer in Australia in December?") | Struggles or reverts to distance | Derives answer from the model |
| Prediction before demonstration | Predicts intuitively | Predicts scientifically |
| Speeded or distracted | Reverts | Slower but mostly right |
| Explains in own words, with mechanism | Uses the term as a label | Walks through the causal chain |
| Delayed test (months later) | Old theory returns | Retained |

**Why it happens.** New information is assimilated into the existing framework. The words
change; the ontology and causal mechanism do not. The results are *synthetic models* (Vosniadou)
or knowledge that is compartmentalized ("school physics" versus "real-world physics").

**Implications for curriculum sequencing**
1. **Map prerequisites between concepts, including across subjects**, not just topic lists.
   Particles → gases are matter → photosynthesis. Heat vs. temperature → energy transfer →
   greenhouse effect. Variation within species → natural selection → common ancestry → tree
   reading.
2. **Start early where intuitions are soft.** The Kelemen storybook study suggests natural
   selection mechanisms can be taught at ages 5-8. Slaughter & Lyons suggests body-as-machine
   biology can be taught in preschool. Delaying "hard" concepts until high school lets
   misconceptions entrench.
3. **Revisit, don't cover once.** Conceptual change is slow and partial, so a spiral with
   *increasingly demanding diagnostics* is better than one unit per topic.
4. **Fewer concepts, deeper.** The Hake/FCI pattern shows that coverage-driven courses leave
   models unchanged.

**Implications for diagnostic questions** (good diagnostics share these features)
- They are **generative**: they ask about a situation the student has not been taught
  directly, so a memorized sentence is no help.
- **Wrong answers diagnose**: each distractor corresponds to a known intuitive theory
  (concept inventory design).
- They require **prediction before observation**.
- They ask for **mechanism** ("explain how, step by step"), not a label.
- They include **conflict items**, cases where intuition and science disagree (e.g., "Does the
  moon have gravity?").

**Implications for assessment**
- Use concept inventories or hinge questions with misconception-based distractors, not just
  terminology recall.
- Use delayed and transfer tests to decide whether a concept is "mastered."
- Consider **speeded** items or items under mild load as a stress test of model strength (a
  research idea in schools, not yet a validated classroom practice).
- Score explanations on a **model rubric** (intuitive / synthetic / scientific), not only
  correct/incorrect.
- Report acceptance and understanding separately. They are only weakly related.

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. **Misconceptions are theories, not gaps.** Children build coherent causal frameworks that
   explain everyday experience. Teaching must replace a *theory*, which is much harder than
   filling an empty slot.
2. **The intuitive theory is suppressed, never deleted.** Even experts are slower and less
   accurate when science contradicts intuition (Shtulman & Valcarcel 2012), and they revert under
   time pressure. Mastery means the scientific model reliably *wins*.
3. **Repeating the right answer is not the same as changing the model.** Synthetic models and
   compartmentalized knowledge are the default outcome of telling. Assessment must use
   generative, prediction and transfer questions to detect real change.
4. **Mechanism is what changes minds.** Teaching *how* something works (germs reproduce,
   greenhouse gases absorb infrared, variation plus selection) shifts reasoning, behaviour and
   even acceptance more than teaching conclusions or rules (Au et al. 2008; Ranney & Clark 2016).
5. **Conceptual change has specific forms:** differentiation, coalescence, and ontological
   reclassification (from substance to process; from essence to population). Curriculum design
   should name which one each concept requires.
6. **Understanding and acceptance are different variables.** People can accept evolution without
   understanding it and vice versa (Shtulman 2006). Schools should aim for understanding and
   measure the two separately.
7. **Kids' errors recapitulate the history of science.** Impetus, caloric, vitalism, essentialism
   and Lamarckism were held by serious thinkers. Using history respects students' reasoning and
   shows theory change in action.
8. **Sequence for conceptual prerequisites and start early.** Some mechanisms (natural selection,
   body-as-machine) can be taught to young children before intuitions entrench, and some concepts
   (photosynthesis) are unteachable without others (matter is particulate, air has mass).

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **Intuitive (folk) theory** — a coherent, pre-instructional causal framework for a domain
  (e.g., impetus physics). *Bites:* assuming students are blank slates about science.
- **Conceptual change** — restructuring a theory's concepts and relations, as opposed to adding
  facts. *Bites:* units that "cover" content without changing the model.
- **Differentiation** — splitting one undifferentiated concept into two (heat/temperature,
  weight/density). *Bites:* students using both words interchangeably.
- **Coalescence** — merging separate concepts into one (plants + animals = living things).
  *Bites:* "plants aren't alive" persisting after a life-science unit.
- **Ontological shift** — moving a concept to a different category of being: substance →
  process (Chi). *Bites:* heat, sound, electric current, diffusion, evolution.
- **Synthetic model** (Vosniadou) — a hybrid of intuitive and taught ideas (hollow-sphere
  Earth). *Bites:* students who say the right sentence while holding a hybrid.
- **Coexistence / suppression** — the intuitive theory remains and is inhibited rather than
  erased. *Bites:* reversion under stress, time pressure, or on delayed tests.
- **Impetus** — a folk "force of motion" stored inside moving objects. *Bites:* physics students
  drawing forces in the direction of motion.
- **Vitalism** — life as a force or energy absorbed from food. *Bites:* early biology, nutrition.
- **Psychological essentialism** (Gelman) — belief that categories have hidden essences causing
  their features. *Bites:* genetics, evolution, also social categories (race, gender, "smart
  kids").
- **Promiscuous teleology** (Kelemen) — a bias to explain things by purpose. *Bites:* "giraffes
  have long necks so they can reach leaves."
- **Transformational vs. variational evolution** (Shtulman 2006) — whole-population need-driven
  change vs. selection on existing variation. *Bites:* the single best diagnostic for evolution
  understanding.
- **Concept inventory** — a multiple-choice test whose distractors encode known misconceptions
  (FCI). *Bites:* the tool for assessing model change at scale.
- **Understanding vs. acceptance** — separate constructs. *Bites:* controversies around evolution
  and climate change; grading policy.

## Evidence strength & limits
- **Robust.** Specific misconceptions (impetus, flat/synthetic earth, seasons-by-distance,
  weight/density, heat/temperature, essentialism, teleology, transformational evolution) are
  among the most replicated findings in science education and developmental psychology, across
  countries and ages. That lecture-based instruction leaves them largely intact is also
  well established (FCI data across thousands of students).
- **Robust but narrower.** The speeded-verification "coexistence" effect (Shtulman & Valcarcel
  2012; Goldberg & Thompson-Schill 2009; Kelemen et al. 2013) is replicated, but effect sizes are
  in reaction time and accuracy on a lab task. That this reflects intuition "persisting" is an
  interpretation. Neuroimaging evidence for inhibition is suggestive and based on small samples.
- **Contested: theory vs. pieces.** Shtulman sits in the "theory theory" camp (Carey, Vosniadou):
  intuitive knowledge is coherent and theory-like. The rival **knowledge-in-pieces** view
  (diSessa; Smith, diSessa & Roschelle 1993) holds that intuitive knowledge is a fragmented set
  of "p-prims" activated by context, and that coherence is often an artifact of interviews. This
  matters in practice. The theory view favours confronting and replacing a framework. The pieces
  view favours building on productive intuitions and reorganizing them. The book presents the
  theory view more than it argues against the alternative.
- **Intervention evidence is thinner than descriptive evidence.** The book documents *what goes
  wrong* far better than it proves *what fixes it*. Promising interventions (Kelemen storybooks,
  Au germ curriculum, Ranney & Clark, Slaughter & Lyons, Slotta & Chi) are mostly small or short
  trials. None is a school-scale, multi-year RCT. The historical parallels (child ≈ early
  scientist) are illustrative, not evidence of a common mechanism.
- **Cognitive conflict is not a silver bullet.** The broader literature (Posner et al. 1982 and
  its critics; Limón 2001) finds that simply confronting students with anomalous data often fails:
  students explain it away. The book's emphasis on mechanism plus explicit concept work is more
  defensible than "show them they're wrong."
- **Scope.** The book covers physical and life sciences only (no chemistry beyond matter, no
  social science or mathematics misconceptions), and mostly US/Western samples, although several
  findings are cross-cultural.
- **Genre.** A trade book: clear, persuasive, selective. It synthesizes others' research plus the
  author's own and is not a systematic review.

## Institutional design implications
- Build every science unit around a named set of **target conceptual changes** (what intuitive
  theory, which change type), not a list of topics. **[E]** that specific misconceptions are
  predictable and persistent. **[H]** that planning around them at unit level improves outcomes
  at scale.
- Create a **school-wide K-12 conceptual progression map** with cross-subject prerequisites
  (e.g., particulate matter before photosynthesis; variation before selection). **[H]**
  (learning-progressions research is supportive but not decisive)
- Teach **mechanistic natural selection and body-as-machine biology in early primary**. **[E]**
  small trials show young children can learn them. **[H]** that this prevents later
  misconceptions at scale.
- **Assessment policy:** a concept is "mastered" only when the student succeeds on novel
  transfer/prediction items and on a delayed re-test, not on recall of the explanation. **[E]**
  that recall and conceptual assessments diverge. **[V]** the definition of mastery.
- Use **concept inventories and misconception-based hinge questions** as a standard
  diagnostic in every science course, pre and post. **[E]** (FCI and related instruments are
  validated; see Wiliam on hinge questions)
- Score written explanations on an **intuitive / synthetic / scientific model rubric**.
  **[H]**
- **Teacher knowledge:** hire and train for *pedagogical content knowledge*, i.e. knowing the
  common intuitive theories in each domain and how to elicit them. Teachers themselves may hold
  them. **[E]** that teachers and adults share many misconceptions. **[H]** that PCK training
  improves student conceptual change.
- Routinely use **predict-observe-explain** and elicit-before-teach sequences. **[E]**
  (interactive engagement effects, Hake 1998) **[H]** for generalization beyond physics.
- **Separate understanding from belief** in grading. Students are assessed on their ability to
  reason with evolution or climate models, never on professing acceptance. **[V]** (respect for
  conscience and family) plus **[E]** (understanding and acceptance are weakly related, and
  mechanistic understanding may raise acceptance anyway).
- **Teach the history of science as theory change**, featuring students' own intuitive theories
  as ideas once held by scientists. **[H]**
- Treat **reversion as normal**: plan spaced revisits of key conceptual changes across years.
  **[E]** for spacing and coexistence. **[H]** for the specific schedule.
- Beware of **essentialism beyond science**. The same bias shapes how students and staff think
  about ability groups and social categories, so the school's language about "smart kids" and
  fixed traits matters. **[H]** (links to growth-mindset and belonging research; the book's
  evidence is on biological kinds)

## Connections
- **00-foundational-papers.** *Strong agreement.* Posner et al. (1982) is the classic
  conceptual-change model (dissatisfaction, intelligible, plausible, fruitful). Vosniadou supplies
  the synthetic models Shtulman relies on. Chi supplies the ontological-shift account.
  *Tension with Sweller/CLT*: CLT treats prior knowledge as schema that reduces load. Scienceblind
  shows that some prior knowledge must be dismantled, and that explicit telling can produce
  synthetic models.
- **01-how-learning-happens.** Agrees on prior knowledge as the main determinant of learning

- **03-intellectual-lives-of-children.** Engel sees children's theories as evidence of rich
  intellectual life to be cultivated. Shtulman sees them as systematically wrong theories to
  overturn. Both agree children are theorizers. The design question is whether to honour or
  confront, and the likely answer is both, in sequence.
- **04-productive-failure.** Strong fit. Kapur's generate-before-instruction phase elicits

- **07-embedding-formative-assessment.** Hinge questions with misconception-based distractors are
  the operational tool for detecting "can repeat" versus "model changed" in real time.
- **12-why-we-remember.** Memory is reconstructive and competes. This fits coexistence and
  reversion under load.
- **13-learning-scientific-concepts.** The direct extension. Amin's work covers the current state

  mostly sidesteps.
- **05-10-to-25.** Weak link. Acceptance of science is also social and identity-laden. Yeager's
  respect-based framing may matter for contested topics (evolution, climate).

## Retrieval practice

1. What is the signature finding of Shtulman & Valcarcel (2012), and what does it imply about
   what happens to intuitive theories after science education?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Educated adults verified statements more slowly and less accurately when the intuitive and scientific truth values conflicted (e.g., "the moon has gravity") than when they agreed, across many domains. It implies the intuitive theory is not erased but coexists and must be suppressed each time.</details>

2. Explain the difference between a transformational and a variational understanding of
   evolution, and why the phrase "natural selection" in an answer doesn't tell you which one a
   student holds.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Transformational: the whole population changes because individuals need to, so change is goal-directed and acquired traits are passed on. Variational: individuals already vary, those with advantageous heritable variants reproduce more, and trait frequencies shift. Students of both kinds use the term "natural selection". Only a mechanistic explanation starting from a varied population reveals which model they hold.</details>

3. A 7-year-old says "the Earth is round" confidently. Give two follow-up questions that would
   reveal whether they hold a synthetic model, and name the likely synthetic models.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>"Draw the Earth and show where people live." "If you walked straight for a very long time, where would you end up / could you fall off?" "Where is the sky?" Likely synthetic models (Vosniadou & Brewer 1992): dual earth, hollow sphere (people live inside on a flat floor), flattened sphere or disc.</details>

4. Name the three types of conceptual change and give one example of each from the book.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Differentiation (heat vs. temperature; weight vs. density). Coalescence (plants and animals merged into "living things"). Ontological reclassification (heat or sound from substance to process; species from essence to variable population).</details>

5. Spot the misconception. A curriculum team writes: "Our Year 10 students scored 88% on the
   Newton's laws unit test (definitions and F = ma problems), so we can move on." What's wrong?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Definitional and quantitative success coexists with impetus beliefs (McCloskey et al. 1980; Clement 1982; FCI data). The test measured "can repeat", not model change. They need qualitative prediction and transfer items (e.g., a concept inventory), ideally with a delayed re-test.</details>

6. Why did teaching children that germs are living things work better than teaching hygiene
   rules (Au et al. 2008), and what general principle does this illustrate?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>A causal mechanism (germs are alive, reproduce, spread via hands) lets children infer new appropriate behaviours and reasons, while rules are brittle and do not generalize. General principle: mechanism beats conclusion or rule, for understanding, transfer and even behaviour and acceptance (cf. Ranney & Clark on the greenhouse effect).</details>

7. Apply. You are designing the K-8 science sequence. Using the book, argue for where
   photosynthesis should sit relative to the particulate nature of matter, and what diagnostic
   you'd use before teaching it.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>After students accept that gases (air, CO2) are matter with mass and have a particle model, because the key idea is that plant mass comes mostly from CO2 in the air. Diagnostic: "A 1 g seed becomes a 500 kg tree. Where did the mass come from?" and "Does air weigh anything?"</details>

8. Why might a biology professor be slower to say "a tree is alive" than "a dog is alive"
   under time pressure, and what does this say about expertise?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The childhood intuition that life means self-directed motion persists. Plants violate it, so the scientific classification must override the intuition, which takes time (Goldberg & Thompson-Schill 2009). Expertise adds an overriding theory and inhibitory control; it does not delete the naive theory.</details>

9. A parent objects to evolution being taught. How does the book's evidence support a policy of
   assessing understanding but never requiring acceptance, and what is the value component?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Shtulman (2006) found understanding and acceptance only weakly related, so they are distinct targets. Understanding is the educational goal and can be assessed objectively. Mechanistic understanding may raise acceptance anyway. The value component [V] is respecting conscience and family belief while not compromising scientific content.</details>

10. Explain the knowledge-in-pieces critique and how it would change a teacher's approach
    compared with Shtulman's framework-theory view.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>diSessa and colleagues argue intuitive knowledge is fragmented, context-cued elements (p-prims) that are not coherent theories. So rather than confront and replace a framework, a teacher would identify productive intuitive resources (e.g., "more effort gives more result," or balancing) and reorganize and refine them. Shtulman's view emphasizes eliciting and overturning a coherent theory. In practice, good teaching likely does both.</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- **"Kids are bad thinkers."** No. The book argues children reason like scientists with limited
  data. Their theories are coherent and match historical science. The problem is the hardness of
  the correct ideas, not childish thinking.
- **"Just tell them the right answer earlier and more clearly."** Telling tends to produce
  synthetic models and "can repeat" knowledge. Clarity helps only when it targets the specific
  intuitive theory and provides a mechanism.
- **"Show them a surprising demonstration and the misconception disappears."** Anomalies are
  often explained away. Conflict is a starting point, not the cure, and intuitions persist even
  after successful change.
- **"If adults still have intuitions, science education is pointless."** The claim is that
  education adds an overriding theory that usually wins with time and effort. Success means the
  right model wins reliably, not that the intuition is erased.
- **"This is only about science class."** The mechanisms (essentialism, teleology, synthetic
  models, coexistence) also apply to history, economics, mathematics (e.g., "multiplication makes
  bigger") and to how staff think about students' abilities.
- **"Acceptance equals understanding."** A student who "believes in" evolution or climate
  change may understand it no better than a sceptic. Measure understanding.
