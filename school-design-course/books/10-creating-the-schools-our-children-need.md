## The book in one sentence
Most popular school reforms (hiring smarter teachers, firing weak ones, merit pay, smaller classes, copying top-scoring countries, school choice) give too little benefit for what they cost, or cannot scale. Two things give the most improvement per dollar and per hour: a knowledge-rich curriculum, and sustained, school-based professional development in which teachers get better at formative assessment.

## The book in one paragraph
Wiliam writes as a hard-nosed evaluator of reforms, not as an advocate of any one of them. He starts from an economic premise. Education matters more than it used to, so schools must improve, and every improvement has to be judged by the size of its effect, what it costs, how reliably it scales, and what it pushes aside (opportunity cost). He then takes apart the favourite reforms of US policy. Teacher quality matters enormously, but you can't reliably spot good teachers before hiring them, and you can't reliably spot weak ones cheaply once they're working. Merit pay has mostly not worked in experiments. Smaller classes help a bit but cost a great deal, and at scale they force you to hire weaker teachers. Other countries' systems are bound up with their cultures ("everything works somewhere; nothing works everywhere"). School choice produces small average effects with wide variation. What remains is to "love the one you're with": improve the teachers you already have, and give them better content to teach. The second half makes the case for a knowledge-rich curriculum (grounded in cognitive science on working memory, long-term memory and background knowledge) and for teacher learning communities. These are small, regular, teacher-led groups in which each teacher commits to trying specific formative assessment techniques and reports back to colleagues. The book closes with the leader's job: protect scarce teacher time and attention by stopping good things so that the best things get done.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
This is the curriculum's **resource-allocation book**. Other books tell you what good learning looks like (01, 06, 07) or how organizations improve (11). This one asks the founder's question: *with finite money, finite teacher hours and finite leadership attention, which bets pay off most?* Read it with this question: **"If I could fund and protect only two or three things across the whole school, what should they be, and what am I willing to not do?"** For someone from a product background, it is roughly the education version of ruthless prioritization by expected value per unit cost, with a strong warning about variance across contexts.

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

### Introduction: the stance
- Core claims
  - The problem in education is not a shortage of good ideas. It is that too many good-sounding ideas compete for limited resources. The question to ask is never "does this work?" but "is this the best use of this money and time?"
  - **"Everything works somewhere, and nothing works everywhere."** Research can't tell teachers or leaders exactly what to do, because context changes the result. What research *can* do is tell you which bets have the best odds.
  - Readers should become critical consumers of research, not followers of whatever is fashionable.
- So-what: Adopt a portfolio mindset. Each initiative is a bet with an expected return, a cost and a variance, and taking on one bet displaces others.

### Section 1 · Ch 1 — Why We Need to Improve American Education
- Core claims
  - US schools are not failing by historical standards. National trend data (NAEP) show gains over decades, especially for younger students and in mathematics. The "crisis" story is overstated.
  - The case for improvement is economic and moral, not nostalgic. Jobs that paid well without much education are disappearing, and automation and offshoring hit routine work hardest. What schools used to produce is no longer enough.
  - Higher educational achievement is linked to large economic returns for individuals and nations. Wiliam draws on Hanushek & Woessmann's work tying cognitive skills (test scores) to long-run national growth.
  - Achievement gaps by income and race are large, and they mean students' life chances depend heavily on factors outside their control.
- Evidence: NAEP long-term trends; Hanushek & Woessmann (e.g., *The Knowledge Capital of Nations*, 2015) on cognitive skills and growth; labour-market polarization research (Autor and colleagues). Specific figures in the chapter are not reproduced here.
- So-what: Your school's "why" should be about what graduates can do in a changed economy and society, not about restoring a golden age. [V] Also: rhetoric about "failing schools" tends to justify drastic structural reforms that don't pay off (the rest of the book).

### Section 2 · Ch 2 — Getting Smarter People Into Teaching
- Core claims
  - Teacher quality is the biggest in-school variable. Differences between classrooms in the same school are much larger than differences between schools. Wiliam's often-repeated framing: with the most effective teachers, students learn in roughly half the time; with the least effective, it takes about twice as long. That is about a four-fold range (a rhetorical summary of value-added spreads, approximate).
  - But what we can see at hiring (degrees, certification, college selectivity, licensure test scores, even subject-matter test scores) predicts later effectiveness only weakly.
  - Raising entry bars reduces supply, and supply constraints are real in shortage subjects and hard-to-staff schools. A higher bar can mean more vacancies filled by substitutes.
  - Alternative-route programmes (e.g., Teach For America) produce effects that are at best modestly positive in some subjects, not transformative.
- Evidence: Kane, Rockoff & Staiger (2008), NYC: certified, uncertified and alternatively certified teachers differ little on average, and variation *within* each group is huge. Studies of TFA (Mathematica evaluations) show small positive maths effects. Hanushek's work on the size of teacher effects.
- So-what: Hiring matters but is noisy. You can't select your way to a great faculty. Design for **developing** whoever you hire. [E] Pre-hire signals are weak. [H] Structured work samples (teaching a demo lesson, responding to student work) may predict better than credentials. Test this in your own hiring.

### Section 2 · Ch 3 — Firing Bad Teachers
- Core claims
  - The "deselection" proposal comes from Hanushek, who estimated that replacing the bottom 5-10% of teachers with average ones would bring US achievement near top-performing countries. In principle the maths works, but in practice it breaks down.
  - **Measurement problem:** value-added estimates are noisy. Year-to-year correlations for the same teacher are modest (roughly 0.2-0.5 in the literature), so many teachers classified as "bottom" one year are not the next. Classroom observation is also unreliable unless you use multiple trained observers and multiple lessons (the Gates MET project, 2010-2013, made this point strongly).
  - **Time problem:** deselection improves the workforce only slowly, as each year's cohort turns over. Meaningful change takes a decade or more.
  - **Supply problem:** replacements have to come from somewhere, and new teachers are on average less effective than experienced ones in their first years.
  - **Cost/side-effects:** a climate of threat can drive good teachers away and discourage risk-taking and collaboration.
- Evidence: Hanushek (e.g., 2009/2011 deselection papers); MET project (Kane, Staiger et al.) on the reliability of observation and value-added; literature on early-career improvement (Rockoff 2004).
- So-what: Removing truly ineffective teachers is sometimes necessary, but it is an **HR hygiene factor, not an improvement strategy**. Don't build your improvement model on accountability-by-removal. [E] Single-year ratings are unreliable. [H] Low-stakes, frequent, developmental observation beats high-stakes annual rating.

### Section 2 · Ch 4 — Paying Good Teachers More
- Core claims
  - Individual performance pay depends on measurement that is too unreliable to be fair (see Ch 3), and it can damage collaboration.
  - Randomized trials of US merit-pay schemes mostly found little or no effect on student achievement.
  - Across-the-board pay rises may improve who enters and stays in teaching over the long term, but they are extremely expensive and slow to show up in results.
  - Some designs (e.g., incentives framed as losses, pre-paid bonuses clawed back if targets are missed) have shown effects in individual experiments, but the evidence is thin and they are hard to use at scale.
- Evidence: Springer et al. (2010), Project on Incentives in Teaching (POINT), Nashville: bonuses up to $15,000, no significant effect. Fryer (2013), NYC school-wide bonus programme: no positive effect. Fryer, Levitt, List & Sadoff (2012), Chicago Heights loss-aversion design: positive effects. Treat the exact attribution of each study to this chapter as background knowledge.
- So-what: Pay competitively for recruitment and retention [V/H], but don't expect incentive pay to raise teaching quality. The main lever is knowing *how* to improve, not motivation. [E] Most teachers already work hard. Their bottleneck is knowledge and skill, not effort.

### Section 2 · Ch 5 — Reducing Class Size
- Core claims
  - Smaller classes do help, especially in the early grades and for disadvantaged students. But the effects are modest compared with the cost, because teacher salaries are the largest budget line.
  - At scale, class-size reduction forces mass hiring, which pulls in less experienced and less qualified teachers. That cancels much of the gain and moves strong teachers toward richer schools.
  - The per-dollar comparison is the point. The same money spent on teacher development would plausibly produce much more learning. Reducing class size is also hard to reverse, because the cost recurs every year.
- Evidence: Tennessee STAR (1985-89; Finn & Achilles; Krueger 1999), a randomized trial of K-3 classes of ~13-17 vs ~22-25, found gains of roughly 0.15-0.2 SD, larger for Black and low-income students. California's 1996 statewide reduction (Jepsen & Rivkin 2009) found smaller gains, diluted by the hiring of new, often uncertified teachers. Hattie's synthesis puts class size at a small effect (~0.2). Hanushek's reviews are sceptical.
- So-what: As a founder, don't use small classes as your main value proposition, and don't spend your budget on them before investing in teacher learning. [E] Class-size effects are real but expensive. [H] Targeted small groups (early reading, intervention) may capture much of the benefit for a fraction of the cost. [V] Parents often value small classes for reasons other than achievement (being known and cared for). That is a legitimate value, but name it honestly.

### Section 2 · Ch 6 — Copying Other Countries
- Core claims
  - International comparisons (PISA, TIMSS) are useful for noticing what is possible, but policy borrowing usually fails. Features of high-performing systems (Finland, Singapore, Shanghai, South Korea, Japan) are tangled up with culture, history, teacher status, demographics, family investment (e.g., private tutoring in East Asia) and wider social policy.
  - Causation is rarely clear. A system can do well despite a policy, not because of it. Many different policy mixes produce high scores.
  - This is where the "everything works somewhere, nothing works everywhere" principle applies with the most force.
- Evidence: PISA and TIMSS rankings. The frequent citing of Finland's teacher-selectivity and low-testing model and of Shanghai's teacher research groups. Wiliam's argument is that the evidence is correlational and confounded. (He does draw on one import elsewhere, the Japanese "lesson study" idea of collaborative professional learning, but he treats it as a principle to adapt, not a policy to copy.)
- So-what: Don't design your school by copying Finland or Singapore. Borrow **mechanisms** that have causal evidence and test them in your context. [E] Cross-national correlations are weak evidence for any single policy. [H] Collaborative teacher inquiry (lesson study) transfers as a mechanism if you adapt it.

### Section 2 · Ch 7 — Expanding School Choice
- Core claims
  - Charter schools, vouchers and other choice mechanisms produce small average effects on achievement, with **very wide variation** between schools. Some urban charter networks produce large gains. Many charters do no better or worse than district schools.
  - Structural change (who runs the school, how funding follows students) doesn't in itself change what happens in classrooms. Effective choice schools are effective because of what they do inside the classroom (curriculum, instruction, culture, teacher development), not because they are choice schools.
  - Because variation between schools is small compared with variation within schools, reshuffling students across schools has a limited upper bound.
  - Choice can also increase sorting and stratification.
- Evidence: CREDO national charter studies (2009, 2013): average effects near zero, with gains in some urban sectors. Lottery-based studies of Boston charters and KIPP (Angrist, Pathak, Walters, Dobbie & Fryer) found large positive effects. Voucher studies are mixed, and some (e.g., Louisiana, around 2013-15) showed negative first-year maths effects.
- So-what: This is especially important for a founder. **Being a new or independent school confers no advantage by itself.** Autonomy only helps if you use it to do the high-leverage classroom things. [E] Structural reforms show small average effects with high variance. [V] Whether choice is good is partly a values question about equity and community.

### Section 3 · Ch 8 — Moving Forward
- Core claims
  - The book pivots here: having ruled out the popular levers, Wiliam sets out how to judge reforms and names the two priorities.
  - **How to evaluate an initiative:** (1) size of effect on student learning; (2) cost (money *and* teacher time); (3) how reliably it scales, meaning whether it works in ordinary schools with ordinary teachers, not just with enthusiasts; (4) opportunity cost, meaning what it displaces; (5) time to impact.
  - **Effect sizes need careful reading.** They depend on student age (young children gain more per year, so the same effect size means more months of learning for older students), on the kind of test (researcher-designed tests close to the intervention give inflated effects compared with standardized tests), and on sample restrictions (studying a narrow group inflates effect sizes). League tables of effect sizes (e.g., Hattie's) compare things that can't be compared.
  - A year's growth is itself a small effect size at secondary level (roughly 0.25-0.4 SD at secondary, larger in the early grades; Bloom, Hill, Black & Lipsey 2008). So an "effect of 0.2" can mean anything from a few weeks to most of a year's extra learning depending on age.
  - Conclusion: the biggest *system-wide* gains come from improving the teachers already in classrooms ("love the one you're with") and improving what they teach.
- Evidence: Bloom, Hill, Black & Lipsey (2008) on benchmarks for interpreting effect sizes. Wiliam's broader critiques of meta-analysis (also in his other writing), which anticipate later methodological critiques (e.g., Simpson 2017).
- So-what: Build an **initiative evaluation rubric** for your school: effect in months of learning, cost per student per year including teacher hours, scalability, and what it displaces. [E] Effect sizes can't be compared across different populations and measures. [H] A simple benefit/cost screen, applied every time, prevents initiative overload.

### Section 3 · Ch 9 — Why Curriculum Matters
- Core claims
  - Curriculum (the content and its sequence, and the materials teachers use) is a major and under-used lever. Choosing better materials costs little compared with most reforms, because schools buy materials anyway.
  - Distinguish the **intended** curriculum (what is specified), the **implemented/enacted** curriculum (what is taught) and the **achieved** curriculum (what is learned). Improvement means aligning all three.
  - Curriculum and pedagogy can't be fully separated. How content is sequenced and represented shapes what teachers can do.
  - Too much curriculum "coverage" leads to shallow learning. Choosing what to leave out is part of curriculum design.
- Evidence: Research showing that choosing among maths textbooks gives achievement differences comparable to more expensive reforms at near-zero marginal cost (e.g., Agodini et al. 2010 randomized comparison of elementary maths curricula; Chingos & Whitehurst 2012, "Choosing Blindly", Brookings). Treat the exact inclusion of these studies as background knowledge.
- So-what: For a founder, **curriculum choice is one of the highest-return early decisions.** Choose or build coherent, sequenced, content-rich materials before spending on anything else in instruction. [E] Materials matter and are cheap to change. [H] Shared, school-wide materials also make teacher collaboration (Ch 12) far more productive, because everyone is working on the same lessons.

### Section 3 · Ch 10 — A Knowledge-Rich Curriculum
- Core claims
  - Knowledge makes thinking possible. Working memory is severely limited. Long-term memory is effectively unlimited. Expertise mostly consists of large, well-organized knowledge in long-term memory that frees working memory for thinking.
  - "Critical thinking", "problem solving" and "creativity" are not generic, transferable skills that can be taught without content. They depend on domain knowledge. Teaching generic skills in isolation gives weak transfer.
  - Reading comprehension depends heavily on background knowledge. Once students can decode, knowledge of the topic often predicts comprehension better than general "reading skill".
  - Minimal guidance (pure discovery) is inefficient for novices. Explicit instruction, worked examples and step-by-step guidance work better while knowledge is thin. Guidance can fade as expertise grows.
  - The distinction between **biologically primary** knowledge (learned naturally, like speaking) and **biologically secondary** knowledge (school subjects, which need deliberate teaching) explains why "natural" learning approaches that work for speech fail for algebra.
  - Knowledge-rich curriculum is also an equity issue. Advantaged children pick up background knowledge at home, so a school that doesn't teach knowledge explicitly widens gaps.
- Evidence: Cognitive load theory (Sweller); Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006); Recht & Leslie (1988) baseball study (weak readers with high baseball knowledge outperformed strong readers with low knowledge on a baseball passage); expertise research (Chase & Simon 1973, chess); Geary on primary/secondary knowledge; E. D. Hirsch's work on core knowledge; Willingham's accessible syntheses.
- So-what: Put a **specified, sequenced, cumulative knowledge curriculum** at the centre of the school, including the elementary years (science, history, geography, arts, not only literacy and numeracy blocks). [E] Background knowledge drives comprehension. [E] Guidance is superior for novices. [V] *Which* knowledge to include is a values choice. Make it explicitly, with your community.

### Section 3 · Ch 11 — Improving the Teachers We Have
- Core claims
  - Because selection and deselection are weak levers, the scalable path is continuous improvement of the existing workforce. Teachers improve a great deal in their first few years. Evidence on later growth is mixed: some studies show a plateau, others show continued growth, especially in supportive schools.
  - Most professional development as currently delivered (one-off workshops, generic inspiration, courses away from school) has little measurable effect on student achievement.
  - The highest-leverage focus for teacher development is **classroom formative assessment**: using evidence of student learning minute by minute and day by day to adjust teaching. This has large effects in research, low cost, and applies across subjects and ages.
  - The five key strategies (from Wiliam & Thompson): (1) clarifying, sharing and understanding learning intentions and success criteria; (2) engineering effective discussions, tasks and activities that elicit evidence of learning; (3) providing feedback that moves learners forward; (4) activating students as learning resources for one another; (5) activating students as owners of their own learning. All of them serve one big idea: use evidence of learning to adapt instruction to meet student needs.
  - Formative assessment is about **short-cycle** adjustments (within and between lessons), not about more frequent tests or data dashboards. Long-cycle "benchmark assessment" data systems add little.
  - Teacher change is **habit change**. Knowing what to do is not the problem. Changing ingrained routines in a complex, fast environment is. So PD must be sustained, practice-based and supported.
- Evidence: Black & Wiliam (1998), "Inside the Black Box" and the review "Assessment and Classroom Learning", which reported effect sizes typically 0.4-0.7 (Wiliam has since emphasized that these are rough and that realistic school-wide effects are smaller but still large per unit cost). The King's-Medway-Oxfordshire Formative Assessment Project (KMOFAP; Black, Harrison, Lee, Marshall & Wiliam 2003) found gains of roughly 0.3 SD, which Wiliam translates to roughly half a year of extra learning per year. TNTP's *The Mirage* (2015) found PD spending high and effects hard to detect. Research on teacher experience includes Rockoff (2004) and Papay & Kraft (2015); Kraft & Papay (2014) found that growth continues longer in supportive professional environments.
- So-what: Make formative assessment the **single, long-term focus** of teacher development. It is the "what" of your PD. [E] Classroom formative assessment has strong evidence of meaningful effects at low cost. [E] Typical PD has little effect. [H] A small number of techniques practised deeply beats a long menu adopted shallowly.

### Section 3 · Ch 12 — Creating a Learning Environment for Educators
- Core claims
  - This is the "how" of teacher learning. The recommended structure is **school-based teacher learning communities (TLCs)**.
  - Typical design (as in Wiliam & Leahy's *Embedding Formative Assessment* pack): groups of about 8-12 teachers; monthly meetings of about 75-90 minutes; at least two years' duration; a fixed agenda (introduction/check-in, a "how's it going?" round where each teacher reports on what they tried, new learning, personal action planning for the next month, a review of learning); and teachers observing each other with a focus the *observed* teacher chooses.
  - Five principles for teacher change: **choice** (teachers choose which techniques to develop), **flexibility** (adapt techniques to their context, but without "lethal mutations" that remove the active ingredient), **small steps** (one or two changes at a time), **accountability** (commit publicly to peers to try things and report back; accountability for trying and reflecting, not for outcomes), and **support** (time, peer help, leadership backing).
  - Leaders create the conditions: time in the timetable, removing other demands, stable groups, and treating the work as a multi-year priority.
  - "Tight but loose": be tight on the core ideas (the active ingredients of formative assessment and the TLC structure) and loose on local adaptation.
- Evidence: Wiliam and colleagues' ETS work on TLCs (mid-2000s; Thompson & Wiliam 2008 "Tight but loose"); lesson study as an analogous mechanism; the Embedding Formative Assessment programme later tested in an English EEF randomized trial (Speckesser et al., 2018), which found a small positive effect of roughly two months' extra progress on GCSE Attainment 8. That trial is background knowledge. Whether the book reports it is unverified, since the book and the trial came out in the same year.
- So-what: This is a **design spec you can adopt directly**: fixed monthly TLC slots for every teacher, protected for at least two years, with a standard agenda and peer observation. [E] Sustained, collaborative, practice-focused PD works better than one-offs. [H] The exact TLC parameters (size, frequency) are sensible defaults to tune, not research-derived optima.

### Section 3 · Ch 13 — Pulling It All Together
- Core claims
  - The recommended strategy is two-pronged: (1) a knowledge-rich curriculum, (2) teacher development through TLCs focused on formative assessment. Almost everything else is lower priority.
  - Leaders' central problem is **allocation**. Money, teacher time and leadership attention are all scarce. The binding constraint is usually *teacher time*, not money.
  - So the leadership skill is **stopping things**: "de-implementing" good-but-not-best initiatives to free time and focus for the best. Wiliam repeatedly argues that the problem is not bad ideas but too many good ones.
  - Improvement should be judged over years, not terms. Changing initiatives every year guarantees that none of them embed.
  - Leaders should model being learners, protect teachers from external noise, and hold the line on the priority.
- So-what: Your founding strategy document should name **two or three priorities** and a **"not doing" list**. Every new proposal must name what it replaces. [H] Evidence on de-implementation is thinner than the logic for it. [V] Choosing depth over breadth of initiative is a leadership value.

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. **Judge every initiative by benefit per unit cost, including teacher time and opportunity cost, not by whether it "works."** Almost everything works a little somewhere. The question is whether it is the best use of the scarce resource it consumes.
2. **Improve the teachers you have ("love the one you're with").** Teacher quality is the biggest in-school lever, but selection, deselection and incentives are weak, slow or costly ways to change it. Sustained development of existing teachers is the scalable path.
3. **The content of teacher development should be classroom formative assessment:** short-cycle use of evidence of learning to adjust teaching, organized around five strategies and one big idea.
4. **The process of teacher development should be school-based teacher learning communities:** monthly, multi-year, teacher-chosen techniques, small steps, peer accountability, protected time.
5. **A knowledge-rich, specified curriculum is a cheap, powerful lever.** Knowledge in long-term memory is what makes thinking, reading comprehension and "skills" possible, and schools that don't teach it widen gaps.
6. **"Everything works somewhere; nothing works everywhere."** Borrow mechanisms, not policies. Expect variation and test in your own context.
7. **Structural reforms (choice, governance, class size, pay) change the container, not the contents.** Being a new, autonomous school guarantees nothing. Your advantage exists only if you use autonomy on classroom-level levers.
8. **Read effect sizes with suspicion.** They depend on age, type of test and sample, so league tables that compare them across studies mislead.
9. **The leader's hardest job is stopping good things.** Teacher time is the binding constraint, and initiative overload is the default failure mode.

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **Benefit/cost ratio (of an initiative)**: learning gained per dollar and per teacher-hour. Bites when choosing between smaller classes and a PD programme, or between a new app and curriculum materials.
- **Opportunity cost**: the value of the best alternative that an initiative pushes out. Bites every time a new initiative is added to a full teacher week.
- **"Everything works somewhere, nothing works everywhere"**: context-dependence of educational effects. Bites when someone says "It worked at School X / in Finland."
- **Scalability**: whether an effect survives in ordinary schools with ordinary teachers without the original designers. Bites when piloting with enthusiasts and rolling out to everyone.
- **Deselection**: removing the least effective teachers to raise the average. Bites in HR policy. It is an unreliable measure and a slow effect.
- **Value-added model (VAM)**: statistical estimate of a teacher's contribution to test score growth. It is noisy year to year. Bites in teacher evaluation and pay.
- **Effect size (standardized mean difference)**: difference between groups in SD units. It depends on age, measure and sample. Bites when reading any research summary or vendor claim.
- **Intended / enacted / achieved curriculum**: what's planned, what's taught, what's learned. Bites in curriculum audits: the gaps between them are where improvement lives.
- **Knowledge-rich curriculum**: specified, sequenced, cumulative content across subjects. Bites in elementary timetables, where literacy "skills blocks" crowd out science and history.
- **Biologically primary vs secondary knowledge (Geary)**: what humans evolved to learn naturally vs what must be taught deliberately. Bites in debates about discovery vs explicit instruction.
- **Formative assessment (Wiliam's definition)**: assessment is formative to the extent that evidence of achievement is elicited, interpreted and used to make better decisions about next steps in instruction. Bites because "formative" is about *use*, not test type.
- **Five key strategies**: learning intentions; eliciting evidence; feedback that moves forward; peers as resources; learners as owners. Bites as the shared vocabulary of your PD and observation.
- **Short-cycle vs long-cycle assessment**: minute-to-minute/day-to-day vs termly/annual. Short cycles have the most impact. Bites when you're tempted to buy a data platform instead of developing teachers.
- **Teacher learning community (TLC)**: a small, stable, monthly peer group with a fixed agenda and personal action plans. Bites as the backbone of your PD calendar.
- **Choice, flexibility, small steps, accountability, support**: design principles for teacher change. Bites when designing any adult-learning programme.
- **Tight but loose**: tight on active ingredients, loose on local adaptation. Bites when balancing fidelity with teacher autonomy.
- **Lethal mutation**: an adaptation that removes the active ingredient (e.g., "traffic lights" used for self-report without the teacher acting on the information). Bites during rollout.
- **De-implementation**: deliberately stopping practices to free capacity. Bites in annual planning.

## Evidence strength & limits
- **Robust**
  - Teacher effects are large, and pre-hire credentials predict effectiveness weakly (many studies, including Kane, Rockoff & Staiger 2008).
  - Single-year teacher value-added and single-observer ratings are unreliable (MET project).
  - Randomized US merit-pay trials mostly show null effects (POINT 2010; Fryer 2013).
  - Class-size reduction has modest positive effects that are expensive, and scaled implementations dilute them (STAR vs California).
  - Charter and choice effects vary widely, with small average effects (CREDO; lottery studies).
  - Background knowledge strongly affects comprehension. Novices benefit from guidance (cognitive science consensus, e.g., Recht & Leslie 1988; CLT).
  - Effect sizes depend on age, measure and sample (Bloom et al. 2008; later Simpson 2017; Kraft 2020).
- **Contested or weaker**
  - **Size of formative assessment effects.** The early 0.4-0.7 range from Black & Wiliam (1998) comes from a narrative review of heterogeneous studies. Kingston & Nash (2011) estimated a much smaller average (~0.2), and methodological disputes followed. Scaled randomized trials (e.g., the EEF trial of Embedding Formative Assessment, 2018) show smaller but positive effects. Wiliam's cost-effectiveness case survives these smaller estimates better than his "doubling the speed of learning" rhetoric does.
  - **Teacher learning communities** as the specific delivery model rest mostly on Wiliam's own development work plus one decent-sized trial. The general principles of effective PD (sustained, practice-based, collaborative) have broader support. Later syntheses of PD (e.g., Kennedy 2016; Sims et al. 2021 EEF review of PD mechanisms) broadly support practice-focused, sustained designs. Sims et al. postdate the book.
  - **Knowledge-rich curriculum at whole-school scale**: the cognitive mechanism is well supported, and causal evidence for whole curricula is growing (e.g., Core Knowledge charter lottery study, Grissmer et al. 2023, which postdates the book). In 2018 Wiliam argued mainly from mechanism.
  - **Arguing vs showing.** The book is a policy argument using others' evidence, not new research. The comparative cost-effectiveness claims (e.g., PD beats class size per dollar) are reasoned estimates, not results of head-to-head trials.
- **Known critiques / limits**
  - US-centric context (policy debates of the 2000s-2010s).
  - Some readers see a double standard: other reforms get tough scrutiny, while formative assessment (the author's own field) gets a more generous reading. Wiliam acknowledges the conflict and argues on cost-effectiveness.
  - Little on non-academic outcomes, student wellbeing, motivation or school culture (see 05-10-to-25, 03-intellectual-lives-of-children).
  - Little on the improvement *methodology* (how to test changes locally). That is 11-improvement-in-action's territory.
  - Critics of "knowledge-rich" approaches worry about narrow or culturally partial canons and about neglect of inquiry. Wiliam's position is that guidance should fade as expertise grows, not that inquiry is never appropriate.

## Institutional design implications
- **Adopt an initiative-evaluation rubric** (effect in months of learning, cost in dollars and teacher-hours, scalability, displacement, time to impact) and require it for every proposal. [H]
- **Name two or three priorities and publish a "stop doing" list** every year. Any new initiative must name what it replaces. [V/H]
- **Treat teacher time as the scarcest resource.** Budget it explicitly (e.g., hours per teacher per month for PD, planning, meetings), as you would money. [H]
- **Hire on job-relevant work samples**, but don't expect hiring to do the heavy lifting. Plan for development from day one. [E: credentials predict weakly; H: work samples help]
- **Do not make value-added or single observations the basis of high-stakes decisions.** Use multiple measures over multiple years for any consequential judgement. [E]
- **Do not use individual merit pay as an improvement lever.** Pay competitively and fairly for recruitment and retention. [E on merit pay nulls; V on fairness]
- **Don't lead with small class sizes.** Invest in teacher learning and curriculum first. Use targeted small-group intervention where it matters most (early reading, catch-up). [E/H]
- **Choose or build a coherent, sequenced, knowledge-rich curriculum across all subjects and grades before launch.** Shared materials school-wide. [E on materials and background knowledge; V on content selection]
- **Protect elementary time for science, history, geography and the arts** as knowledge-building, not just literacy/numeracy skills blocks. [E/V]
- **Make formative assessment the multi-year focus of all teacher development**, with the five strategies as shared language for planning, observation and coaching. [E/H]
- **Run teacher learning communities**: groups of 8-12, monthly, 75-90 min, at least two years, standard agenda, personal action plans, peer observation on the observed teacher's chosen focus. [H: parameters are defaults; E: sustained collaborative PD beats one-offs]
- **Keep peer observation developmental and low-stakes**, separate from evaluation. [H]
- **Be tight but loose.** Specify the non-negotiable active ingredients, allow local adaptation, and watch for lethal mutations. [H]
- **Don't buy data systems for long-cycle assessment as a substitute for classroom-level formative practice.** [E/H]
- **Don't copy "what Finland does."** Import mechanisms with causal evidence and pilot them. [E]
- **Use your autonomy as a new school for classroom-level levers.** Structure alone won't produce results. [E]
- **Commit to a 3-5 year horizon on the core priorities** and resist annual pivots. [V/H]

## Connections
- **07-embedding-formative-assessment** (Wiliam & Leahy): the operational companion. This book says *why* formative assessment and TLCs deserve scarce resources; 07 says *how* (techniques and the TLC pack). Read 07 next.
- **01-how-learning-happens** and **00-foundational-papers** (Sweller CLT; Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006): the cognitive science behind Ch 10's knowledge-rich argument. Strong agreement.
- **06-beyond-the-science-of-reading** (Wexler): strong agreement on background knowledge and on knowledge-building elementary curriculum. Wexler supplies the classroom and literacy detail that Wiliam only sketches.
- **11-improvement-in-action** (Bryk): complementary, with some tension. Both reject silver bullets and stress variation ("everything works somewhere" is close to Bryk's "what works, for whom, under what conditions"). Bryk offers a *method* (networked improvement, PDSA cycles) for local testing. Wiliam offers a *bet* (formative assessment + curriculum). Tension: Wiliam is readier to name the answer in advance.
- **09-in-search-of-deeper-learning** (Mehta & Fine): partial tension. Mehta & Fine prize depth, identity and project work, and are sceptical of knowledge-transmission cultures. Wiliam would ask about cost, scale and novices' need for guidance. Both agree that structure-only reform fails and that teacher learning environments are decisive.
- **04-productive-failure** (Kapur): tension with Ch 10's "guidance for novices" line. Kapur argues that structured problem-solving *before* instruction can help conceptual understanding. The two can be reconciled: productive failure is still followed by explicit instruction, and it's a designed sequence, not minimal guidance.
- **08-extraordinary-learning-for-all** (Samouha et al.): shares the focus on adult learning systems and coherent instructional models at scale. Compare their school-design approach with Wiliam's allocation logic.
- **03-intellectual-lives-of-children** (Engel) and **05-10-to-25** (Yeager): cover what Wiliam largely skips (curiosity, motivation, adolescent status and respect). They are useful counterweights to a purely achievement-per-dollar lens.
- **02-scienceblind** (Shtulman) and **13-learning-scientific-concepts** (Amin): knowledge-rich doesn't mean fact-list. Conceptual change research shows that knowledge must restructure intuitive theories. This refines Ch 10.
- **12-why-we-remember** (Ranganath): memory science that supports the long-term-memory argument.

## Retrieval practice

1. (Recall) Name the six popular reforms that Section 2 argues "won't help much."
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Getting smarter people into teaching; firing bad teachers; paying good teachers more; reducing class size; copying other countries; expanding school choice.</details>

2. (Recall) What are the two priorities Wiliam recommends instead?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>(1) A knowledge-rich curriculum; (2) improving the teachers we already have through sustained, school-based professional development (teacher learning communities) focused on classroom formative assessment.</details>

3. (Explain-why) Teacher quality is the biggest in-school variable. Why, then, does Wiliam argue against building a strategy on teacher selection and deselection?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Because we can't measure teacher quality reliably enough, either before hiring (credentials predict weakly) or afterwards (value-added and observation ratings are noisy year to year). Deselection also works slowly, depends on the supply of replacements (who are often novices), and can damage climate and retention. Improving existing teachers reaches the whole workforce now.</details>

4. (Explain-why) Tennessee STAR found real gains from smaller classes. Why did California's statewide class-size reduction disappoint?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Scaling it up required hiring large numbers of new teachers quickly, many of them inexperienced or uncertified. Stronger teachers moved to more advantaged schools, and facilities were stretched. The loss in teacher quality offset much of the class-size benefit, and the recurring cost was very large.</details>

5. (Apply) A board member proposes cutting class sizes from 24 to 18 across your school, costing about the same as a three-year, school-wide TLC programme plus new curriculum materials. Using Wiliam's criteria, how would you evaluate this?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Compare expected effect in months of learning, cost (including recurring salary cost), scalability and opportunity cost. Class-size effects at this range are modest and mostly strongest in early grades. The cost recurs forever, and it requires hiring more teachers (possibly weaker ones). TLCs + curriculum are cheaper per unit of expected learning and improve every class. Wiliam would favour PD + curriculum, perhaps with targeted small groups for early reading. Name honestly any non-achievement value parents place on smaller classes.</details>

6. (Spot the misconception) "Formative assessment means giving more frequent quizzes and tracking the data in a dashboard."
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Wrong. Assessment is formative when evidence of learning is actually *used* to adjust teaching or learning, mostly in short cycles (within and between lessons). Frequent tests whose data aren't acted on in class, or long-cycle benchmark dashboards, capture little of the benefit.</details>

7. (Explain-why) Why does Wiliam treat a knowledge-rich curriculum as an equity measure?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Comprehension and thinking depend on background knowledge. Advantaged children pick up much of that knowledge at home, so a school that relies on generic "skills" and doesn't teach knowledge systematically leaves the knowledge gap, and therefore the achievement gap, in place or wider.</details>

8. (Apply) You're launching TLCs. List the five principles for teacher change and one design feature that puts each into practice.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Choice: each teacher picks techniques from a menu. Flexibility: adapt techniques to the class while keeping the active ingredient. Small steps: one or two techniques per month in a personal action plan. Accountability: report back at the next meeting in the "how's it going" round. Support: protected monthly time, peer observation, leadership backing.</details>

9. (Spot the misconception) "Our charter status and freedom from district rules will make us better than the local district schools."
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Structure doesn't determine results. Choice schools show small average effects and very high variance. Successful ones succeed because of what they do in classrooms (curriculum, instruction, teacher development). Autonomy is only an advantage if you spend it on those levers.</details>

10. (Explain-why / effect sizes) A vendor says its programme has an effect size of 0.4 while "class size is only 0.2", so it must be twice as good. What's wrong with this?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Effect sizes can't be compared across different studies. They depend on student age (a year of growth is a much smaller SD for teenagers than for young children), on the test (researcher-designed, closely aligned tests inflate effects) and on the sample (restricted-range samples inflate SD-based effects). Cost and scalability are also missing from the comparison. You need comparable measures and a benefit-per-cost judgement.</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- **"Wiliam says teacher quality doesn't matter."** The opposite. He says it matters most, and that the weak levers are *selection, removal and incentives*, not development.
- **"Class size doesn't matter."** It matters modestly. His claim is about cost-effectiveness and what happens at scale, not zero effect.
- **"Knowledge-rich means rote memorization of facts."** He means organized, cumulative knowledge that makes thinking and understanding possible, taught with explicit guidance that fades as expertise grows.
- **"Formative assessment = more testing / data systems."** It means short-cycle, in-the-moment adaptation of teaching, plus peer and self-assessment practices.
- **"Just run a PD day on formative assessment."** He argues one-off PD fails. The model is multi-year, monthly, collaborative habit change.
- **"Ignore research because nothing works everywhere."** No. Use research to pick the best-odds bets, then adapt with fidelity to the active ingredients and watch results in your context.
- **"It's a book about US policy, so it doesn't apply to a single new school."** The allocation logic (effect × scale ÷ cost, opportunity cost, stopping things) applies directly to a founder's budget and calendar.
- **"The two priorities are all a school needs."** They are the highest-return *academic-improvement* bets. Wiliam isn't arguing against safety, culture, wellbeing or motivation. He just doesn't focus on them (see 05, 03, 09).
