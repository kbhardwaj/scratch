## The book in one sentence
Six real school systems show that schools improve reliably when they treat their own inconsistent results as the thing to study. That means naming a specific problem, working out which parts of the system produce it, testing small changes quickly with practical measures, and learning together across a network. Buying a new program and hoping does not do this.

## The book in one paragraph
Bryk led the Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching from 2008 to 2020. His starting point is that American education reform keeps repeating one cycle: adopt a big solution, roll it out everywhere, get results that vary widely and are mediocre on average, give up, and try the next big solution. His alternative is *improvement science*, adapted from industrial quality management (Deming, Juran) and from healthcare improvement (the Institute for Healthcare Improvement, Berwick, Langley et al.'s *Model for Improvement*). *Learning to Improve* (2015) set out six principles and a toolkit. *Improvement in Action* (2020) shows the method working in six organizations: Fresno Unified School District, New Visions for Public Schools (New York City), Summit Public Schools, High Tech High, the National Writing Project, and the School District of Menomonee Falls, Wisconsin. The problems include graduation rates, college access, credit accumulation, chronic absenteeism, writing instruction and whole-district culture. Each chapter tells the story and then draws general lessons. The final chapter argues that to spread this kind of practice, the field needs a support system: analytic hubs, networked improvement communities, leaders who model inquiry, and new roles for research and policy.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
Every other book in this curriculum tells you *what* good learning looks like: retrieval practice, formative assessment, knowledge-rich curriculum, deeper learning, adolescent motivation. This book answers a different question: **once you know what should happen, how do you make it happen reliably, in every classroom, for every student, year after year, as staff change?** Read it with this question in mind: *What is the operating system of my school, meaning how it notices problems, tests fixes, measures, and spreads what works, and how do I build that in from day one instead of adding it later?*

For a founder this may be the most useful book on the list. A new school will ship something imperfect. What decides whether it becomes world-class is how fast and how well it learns afterwards.

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

### Part A — The method (from *Learning to Improve*, 2015, recapped in the Introduction)

#### The diagnosis: why reform keeps failing
- **Core claim.** Schools are complex systems. Many interacting parts (schedules, roles, curricula, data, norms, relationships) jointly produce outcomes. Reforms usually change one part and assume the rest will adjust. They don't, so the same intervention works in some places, fails in others, and averages out to "small effect."
- **Core claim.** Most reforms fail because of *implementation*, not because of their *ideas*. The field has plenty of good ideas and not much ability to make them work reliably in different contexts. Bryk's summary: the question is not "what works?" but **"what works, for whom, under what conditions?"**
- **Core claim.** The usual research-to-practice pipeline, where academics design, randomized trials prove, and schools adopt, is too slow, and it answers the wrong question. An RCT gives you an average effect. A school leader needs to know whether it will work *here*, and what to adjust.
- **Key phrase (a saying from the healthcare improvement movement, usually credited to Paul Batalden):** every system is perfectly designed to get the results it gets. If your results are bad, look at the design, not at the individuals.
- **So-what.** Assume from the start that whatever you design will vary in how it gets carried out, and build ways to detect and learn from that variation.

#### The six principles
The core of the method. For each principle: what it means, how it maps onto software practice, and where that mapping stops holding.

**1. Make the work problem-specific and user-centered.**
- Start with a precisely stated problem, such as "38% of our ninth-graders fail at least one core course in fall semester," and not with a solution ("let's adopt a tutoring program"). Understand the problem as the people who live it experience it: students, teachers, parents. Talk to them, shadow them, map their journey.
- *Software analogy (a good fit):* product discovery, jobs-to-be-done, user interviews before building. The discipline of "fall in love with the problem, not the solution" is the same.
- *Where it breaks down:* in product work, users pick your product and can leave. In schools, the "users" (students) are required to attend, teachers are both users of the system and the people delivering it, and "what the user wants" is not always the goal. A 14-year-old may want less homework. You serve their long-term interests, and that takes judgement a satisfaction survey can't give you.

**2. Focus on variation in performance.**
- The key question is not "does X work on average?" but "why does X work well in some classrooms and badly in others?" Variation is where the information is. Look at the tails: the classrooms, teachers or students where things go unusually well (*positive deviants*) or badly.
- The aim is **quality reliably at scale**, which means reducing variation in outcomes (especially the bad tail), not only raising the mean.
- *Software analogy (a very good fit):* reliability engineering. You care about p99 latency, not mean latency. An intervention with a good mean and a fat left tail is a service that is "fast on average" and times out for 5% of users, and in schools that 5% is disproportionately the students who are already most disadvantaged.
- *Where it breaks down:* teacher variation is not configuration drift you can fix by reverting to a known-good build. Human variation comes from skill, context, relationships and student mix. You reduce it by building capability and giving support, not by enforcing a spec.

**3. See the system that produces the current outcomes.**
- Before changing anything, map how the current outcome is produced. Tools: process maps, root-cause (fishbone) analysis, systems diagrams, and conversations with the people involved. Most "people problems" turn out to be process problems.
- *Software analogy (a strong fit):* a blameless post-incident review. You don't ask "who broke production?" You ask "what about our system made this failure likely, and why didn't our safeguards catch it?" Improvement science brings exactly this attitude to schools: a failing student is an incident, and you look for contributing factors in the system.
- *Where it breaks down:* in software you can usually reconstruct the causal chain from logs. In schools the causes are spread out, often invisible (a student's home situation, a peer relationship), and slow to show up, so your root-cause analysis is always a *hypothesis*.

**4. We cannot improve at scale what we cannot measure.**
- You need measures of outcomes, and also measures of the *processes and drivers* you think lead to those outcomes. Those need to be collected often enough to guide weekly decisions. Annual state test results come too late and are too coarse to improve anything with.
- This is where **practical measures** come in (see below).
- *Software analogy (a strong fit):* observability. The annual test is like quarterly revenue, a lagging indicator. You need the equivalent of request-level metrics and traces: leading indicators close enough to the work to show whether this week's change did anything.
- *Where it breaks down:* (a) Measuring people changes how they behave (Campbell's law / Goodhart's law), and teachers and students react to being measured in ways servers don't. (b) Each measurement uses up instructional time and goodwill, so it has a real cost. (c) Many things you care about, such as understanding, belonging and curiosity, can only be measured noisily.

**5. Anchor practice improvement in disciplined inquiry (PDSA cycles).**
- Improvement moves through repeated **Plan–Do–Study–Act** cycles. Plan: state a change, a *prediction*, and how you'll measure it. Do: try it at small scale. Study: compare the result with the prediction. Act: adopt, adapt or abandon. Start very small (one teacher, one class, one week). Widen the conditions as confidence grows. Scale only once it holds up across contexts.
- The organizing frame is the *Model for Improvement* (Langley, Nolan, Nolan, Norman & Provost, *The Improvement Guide*), which asks three questions: What are we trying to accomplish? How will we know a change is an improvement? What change can we make that will result in improvement?
- Bryk's slogan: **"learning fast to implement well."** Fail fast and small so you don't fail slow and large.
- *Software analogy (partly a fit):* iterative development, feature flags, canary releases. Roll a change out to 1%, watch the metrics, widen the rollout. The *ramp logic* carries over well.
- *Where it breaks down, and this matters:* **a PDSA cycle is not an A/B test.** An A/B test uses randomization and large samples to estimate a causal effect with statistical confidence. A PDSA cycle usually has n = 1 classroom and no control group. It builds practical knowledge by accumulating many small, predicted, observed tests over time, and it relies on run charts and judgement. It is closer to a developer iterating on a local build against a clear spec than to a controlled experiment. Its weakness is that you can fool yourself: regression to the mean, a novelty effect, or a good week that had nothing to do with the change. Writing the prediction down beforehand is the main protection against this. Also, students can't be rolled back. A failed test used real students' learning time, which is why the tests start so small.

**6. Accelerate learning through networked improvement communities (NICs).**
- A single school learns slowly because it has few cases and little variation to learn from. A **network** of schools working on the *same* problem, with a *shared* aim, a *shared* theory (the driver diagram), *shared* measures, and a hub that coordinates and pools the data, learns far faster, because the tests run in many places at once show what holds up across contexts.
- NICs are "scientific communities" of practitioners, researchers, designers and data analysts. The concept draws on Douglas Engelbart's idea of improving the improvement process itself.
- *Software analogy (a reasonable fit):* an open-source project with a common roadmap, a shared test suite, and maintainers who merge contributions. Or a platform team whose telemetry lets each product team learn from the others.
- *Where it breaks down:* NICs depend on trust and voluntary participation across organizations with different bosses, calendars and incentives. Coordination costs are very high and hubs need long-term funding. None of this is as cheap as a pull request.

#### The core toolkit (background knowledge, standard Carnegie/IHI practice)

**Aim statement.** Specific, measurable, time-bound, and tied to a population. For example: "By June 2027, 90% of our 9th-graders will pass all core courses, with no gap across racial or income subgroups." It works like an OKR, except it names who and how many, not only "improve X."

**Fishbone (Ishikawa / cause-and-effect) diagram.** The problem goes at the "head." Major categories of cause form the "bones" (in schools, often: students, teachers/instruction, curriculum, policies/structures, family/community, data and systems). Specific contributing causes hang off each bone. It is built *with* the people who do the work, and it should be *checked* against data and user interviews, not guessed. It is often combined with the "5 Whys." It is the equivalent of a post-incident review's contributing-factors section. Common failure: fishbones full of causes the school can't control ("poverty," "parents don't care"). A good facilitator steers the group toward causes inside the system's control, without pretending the outside causes don't matter.

**Pareto chart.** Rank the causes by how often they occur. Often a few categories account for most of the failures (for example, most course failures come from missing assignments rather than failed tests). This tells you where to act first, the same way you triage bugs by frequency times severity.

**Process map.** Draw the actual step-by-step flow of what happens (how a failing grade becomes an intervention, how a student gets enrolled in college). Find the handoffs, the waiting, and the places where nobody owns a step. Fresno's "leaky pipeline" is a process map of the route from high school to college. It is like a request trace across microservices, and the leaks are the dropped requests.

**Driver diagram.** This is the *working theory of improvement*, and the most important tool to learn. Read from left to right:
- **Aim** → what you are trying to achieve (measurable).
- **Primary drivers** → the few (typically 3-5) high-level areas that you believe must change to reach the aim. Example for 9th-grade course passing: *students' academic mindsets*, *quality of instruction in core courses*, *early-warning and response system*, *transition supports*.
- **Secondary drivers** → the specific structures, processes and norms that act on each primary driver. Example under "early-warning system": *timely grade data visible to teachers*, *a weekly team meeting that reviews at-risk students*, *a clear menu of responses*.
- **Change ideas** → specific, testable changes that act on the secondary drivers. Example: "Advisory teachers send a same-day text to families when a student misses a major assignment."

Two things matter most. First, a driver diagram is a **hypothesis, meant to be revised**. You update it as PDSA cycles show which drivers actually move the aim. Second, **every driver should have a measure**, which gives you the measurement system: outcome measures at the aim, driver measures in the middle, process measures at the change ideas. The software analogue is a metrics tree or an OKR cascade connected to a causal hypothesis. It is closest to an architecture decision record that also carries its own instrumentation.

**Four kinds of measures** (IHI tradition):
- *Outcome* — did the aim move? (graduation rate, course pass rate). Lagging.
- *Driver* — did the hypothesized lever move? (students' sense of belonging, % of teachers using the early-warning data).
- *Process* — was the change actually carried out as planned? (% of eligible families contacted within 24h).
- *Balancing* — did we break something else? (did teacher workload go up, did other courses' grades go down). This is the equivalent of guardrail metrics in an experiment, and it is the type most often left out.

**Practical measures.** Short, quick, low-burden instruments embedded in daily work, designed specifically to guide improvement, not to hold people accountable or to support research. The key reference is Yeager, Bryk, Muhich, Hausman & Morales (2013), *Practical Measurement* (Carnegie Foundation). Example: the Carnegie Math Pathways used a handful of short survey items on "productive persistence" (students' mindsets and skills around persisting in math) that took seconds to answer, were given at key moments, and flagged students at risk early. The design criteria:
- **Specific to the change** being tested (not a general construct).
- **Fast and cheap**: a few items, a few minutes at most, repeated frequently.
- **Predictive**: shown to relate to the outcome you care about.
- **Actionable**: results come back quickly, in a form the practitioner can act on this week.
- **Low stakes**: not used to evaluate people, or practitioners will game them.
Practical measures sit between *accountability measures* (annual tests, meant for judging) and *research measures* (long validated scales, meant for publishing). Most schools have plenty of the first two and none of the third.

**Run charts / statistical process control.** Plot a measure over time with the change marked on the chart, and use simple rules (for example, a run of six or more points above the median) to tell a real signal from ordinary noise. This is Deming's distinction between *common-cause* and *special-cause* variation. The analogy is a time-series dashboard with anomaly detection, and it breaks down in the same way: small n and noisy classroom data make false signals easy to see.

#### The two flagship examples from *Learning to Improve* (background)
- **Carnegie Math Pathways (Statway / Quantway).** A NIC across community colleges attacking very high failure rates in developmental (remedial) math. It redesigned the pathway into one-year, credit-bearing statistics and quantitative reasoning courses, and paired it with "productive persistence" supports and practical measures. It is the showcase for NICs and practical measurement. Carnegie reported substantially higher success rates than the traditional sequence; I don't give exact figures here.
- **Building a Teaching Effectiveness Network (BTEN).** Worked with districts on supporting beginning teachers, with a focus on the *feedback* new teachers receive. It illustrates problem-specific framing and seeing the system: new-teacher attrition was treated as partly a failure of the feedback and support process rather than of the individual teachers.

### Part B — The cases (*Improvement in Action*, 2020)

The same structure applies throughout. Each chapter tells how the organization came to improvement work, what problem it took on, which tools it used, and what changed, and then closes with Bryk's general lessons. The narratives are largely based on interviews with the organizations' leaders, and all of them had some connection to Carnegie. That matters for evidence quality (see below).

#### Introduction
- Restates the reform problem and the six principles (Part A above).
- Positions the book as proof of concept: the principles are not only theory, and real organizations with ordinary budgets have used them to reduce long-standing inequities.
- **So-what:** the cases differ on purpose (a large urban district, a nonprofit support hub, two charter networks, a national teacher network, a small suburban district). The point is that improvement is an *organizational capability* that can take root in many structures, not a program that belongs to one type of organization.

#### Ch 1 — Attacking Inequities in Postsecondary Opportunities (Fresno Unified)
- **Problem.** Fresno Unified is a large, high-poverty district in California's Central Valley. Its students were dropping out of the path to college at multiple points: not finishing the "A–G" course sequence required for California's public universities, not applying, not completing financial aid, not enrolling. The chapter describes this as a **leaky pipeline** (verified via review extracts).
- **Core claims.**
  - *Mapping the whole pipeline* turned a vague aim ("more college-going") into a series of specific, measurable leaks, each with its own owners and fixes. This is principle 3 in action.
  - The district used its own data to find where students were lost, and found that many of the losses were *procedural*, such as forms, deadlines, missing information and course placement, not a lack of ability or ambition. Those problems can be fixed through better processes *(background; details unverified)*.
  - Equity is framed operationally. Gaps are variation in outcomes across subgroups, and closing them is a design problem.
- **So-what for a school designer.** Map every student's path to the outcomes you promise (graduation, postsecondary, whatever your mission says) as a funnel with conversion rates at each step, broken out by subgroup, from day one. Many "achievement gaps" at the end of the funnel are really process gaps upstream, and much cheaper to fix. This is the most direct analogy to product funnel analytics in the book, and it holds up well.

#### Ch 2 — Supporting Improvement Through an Analytic Hub (New Visions for Public Schools)
- **Problem.** New Visions is a New York City nonprofit that supports a network of NYC public high schools and also runs its own charter schools. The problems were high school graduation, credit accumulation, passing the state Regents exams, and **chronic absenteeism** (verified in review extracts).
- **Core claims.**
  - Individual schools lack the data capacity to track every student's progress toward graduation in real time. A central **analytic hub** can build tools and routines that give counselors and teachers a live, student-by-student view: who is off-track on credits, who is missing Regents requirements, whose attendance is slipping.
  - The hub's job is not to produce reports for leadership. It builds *tools for practitioners*, then studies how those tools actually get used and improves them. The data work is itself improved through user-centered iteration *(background: New Visions is known for building lightweight spreadsheet-based tracking tools for schools; the specific tool names are not verified here)*.
  - Data does nothing on its own. It helps only when it is built into regular routines (who looks at it, when, and what they do next).
- **So-what.** Build the student-information and early-warning layer as a *product with internal users* (advisors, counselors, teachers), and staff it that way: an analyst or data-product owner whose customers are practitioners. In software terms this is a platform or internal-tools team. The analogy works well, including the classic failure mode of building dashboards nobody opens.

#### Ch 3 — Becoming a Learning Organization (Summit Public Schools)
- **Problem.** Summit is a charter network that moved *away from a traditional high school structure* toward a personalized-learning model (verified in review extracts). That is a large redesign with many unknowns.
- **Core claims** *(background; details unverified)*.
  - A radical redesign has to be treated as a *continuous learning process*, not a launch. The first version of any new model will have failures nobody anticipated. The organization's ability to spot and fix them quickly matters more than the quality of the original blueprint.
  - Summit is the case where improvement is developed as an organizational identity ("learning organization"): routines, roles and norms where the adults inquire into their own practice the way they ask students to.
  - Tension shown in the case: innovation (big new designs) versus improvement (making designs work reliably). Bryk's view is that they are complementary. Innovation creates variation, and improvement disciplines it.
- **Caveat worth knowing (background, outside the book):** Summit's personalized-learning platform (Summit Learning, spread to many other schools with technology-sector backing) later attracted public controversy and pushback in some adopting districts. The book is about Summit's internal learning practices and does not settle whether the model works at scale elsewhere.
- **So-what.** As a founder, you are Summit: you are launching a new design. Expect v1 to be wrong in ways you can't predict, and budget time, roles and data for learning about it from the first term.

#### Ch 4 — Infusing Improvement into a Charter Network (High Tech High)
- **Problem.** High Tech High is a San Diego network known for project-based learning, and it also runs its own graduate school of education. Its aim was college success for all students, and it wanted to use data more intelligently to find and solve problems (verified in review extracts).
- **Core claims.**
  - Even schools with a strong pedagogical identity and a good reputation have inequitable outcomes that stay hidden until you break the data out. Having a strong design is not the same as getting reliable results.
  - HTH brought improvement science into both its schools and its teacher-education programs, turning improvement into something teachers learn to do, not only something administrators run *(background; supported by public accounts of HTH teachers running improvement projects in mathematics, e.g., weekly student-agency surveys, which match the practical-measures idea)*.
  - Adding improvement to a culture that values autonomy and creativity takes care. The approach has to be presented as professional inquiry, not compliance.
- **So-what.** A strong pedagogical vision (you will have one) does not protect you against inequitable outcomes. Measure outcomes by subgroup no matter how good the design feels. And train teachers in improvement methods as part of how they grow as professionals.

#### Ch 5 — The National Writing Project (College-Ready Writers Program)
- **Problem.** Helping teachers teach *argument writing* well, in the period when the Common Core writing standards were being introduced. The work happened through NWP's network of university-based sites working with high-need, largely rural districts (verified in review extracts and in public accounts of the College-Ready Writers Program).
- **Core claims.**
  - This is the clearest example in the book of a **networked improvement community** tackling an *instructional* problem at the classroom level, rather than a structural one such as attendance or credits. It shows that the method can reach what happens inside the classroom, not only logistics.
  - The program combined teacher professional learning, shared instructional resources and formative-assessment tools with network-wide improvement routines.
  - **Evidence of note (verified independently):** an SRI Education randomized controlled trial of the College-Ready Writers Program found positive effects on teachers' practice and on a validated measure of students' argument writing. This matters because it is one of the few places in the improvement literature where an improvement-style program also has RCT evidence of impact.
- **So-what.** Improvement methods and strong instructional content complement each other. Adopt proven instructional approaches (for example, from Wiliam & Leahy on formative assessment) and then use improvement methods to make them work reliably in *your* classrooms.

#### Ch 6 — Transforming a School District (Menomonee Falls, Wisconsin)
- **Problem.** Under superintendent **Pat Greco**, a small suburban district facing a suspension rate far above the state average (public accounts put it at roughly seven times higher), gaps by income and race, cost overruns and missed accountability targets decided to become a continuous-improvement organization *across the whole district*.
- **Core claims.**
  - The most complete "whole-system" case. *Everyone* was trained in improvement methods, including teachers, support and operations staff, board members, leadership, and students. Improvement became how the district runs, not a project inside it.
  - Executive leadership was decisive. The superintendent modeled inquiry, built the infrastructure (training, coaching, time, data), and protected the work while people learned.
  - Culture changed from blame to learning. Problems are raised as data about the system, not as failures of individuals.
- **So-what.** For a founder, this is the closest model of an improvement culture that runs through the whole organization. It suggests (not proves) that *leadership behavior* and *universal training* are what make the method stick. It also shows the risk: if the whole thing depends on one strong leader, it may not survive that leader leaving.
- *Software analogy:* an organization-wide DevOps/SRE transformation, where every team owns its reliability, blameless reviews are universal, and leadership invests in tooling. The analogy fits well, and so does the lesson that this is mostly about culture, not tools.

#### Ch 7 — Charting Paths Forward
- **Core claims** *(themes drawn from the book's framing; specific wording unverified)*:
  - Across the cases, success depends less on any single tool and more on developing **organizational capabilities**: seeing the system, using data for learning, disciplined inquiry routines, and leadership that makes it safe to surface problems.
  - Improvement needs **infrastructure** that most US education systems don't have: analytic hubs, facilitators and improvement coaches, time in the schedule, and data systems designed for practitioners.
  - **Leadership** means creating the conditions for inquiry, not issuing mandates, and leaders are the ones who model the learning stance.
  - The field needs new relationships between research and practice. Researchers should work *with* practitioners on practitioners' problems, and policy should make room for learning and not only reward compliance with accountability rules.
- **So-what.** The final chapter is a checklist of institutional investments. A new school gets to build them in rather than retrofit them, and that is a real advantage for a founder.

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. **Improvement is an organizational capability, not a program.** What makes schools good over time is a built-in routine for noticing problems, testing fixes and spreading what works. Design that routine as carefully as you design the curriculum.
2. **Aim for reliability at scale, which means attacking variation.** Ask "why does this work here and fail there?" Measure outcomes by subgroup and by classroom, and treat the bad tail as the main problem to solve.
3. **The system produces the result.** Before blaming teachers or students, map the process that produces the outcome. Most failures are process leaks (Fresno) or missing information loops (New Visions).
4. **Hold your theory of action as a driver diagram, and revise it.** Aim → primary drivers → secondary drivers → change ideas, with a measure at every level. It is a hypothesis you keep updating, not a plan you defend.
5. **Test small, predict first, learn fast.** PDSA cycles with a written prediction, starting with one classroom. They are not A/B tests. They build practical, local knowledge, and they need safeguards against fooling yourself.
6. **Practical measures are the missing middle.** Short, frequent, low-stakes measures, tied to specific changes and returned to teachers within days. Without them you are looking at lagging annual results.
7. **Networks learn faster than lone schools.** A school on its own has too little variation to learn from. Join or build a networked improvement community around a shared problem.
8. **Leadership decides whether this works.** Leaders make it safe to surface failure, give time and roles to the work, and model inquiry (Menomonee Falls). Improvement run as compliance turns into paperwork.
9. **Improvement complements good content and does not replace it.** It helps you implement proven practices reliably (NWP). It won't produce a good curriculum out of weekly cycles.

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **Improvement science** — a disciplined method, from industrial quality and healthcare, for getting better at getting better. *Bites:* it is how your school runs from week to week, not a special project.
- **Quality reliably at scale** — good outcomes for *every* student in *every* classroom, not only on average. *Bites:* it changes the success metric from means to distributions.
- **Aim statement** — how much, by when, for whom. *Bites:* it forces precision, and "improve reading" is not an aim.
- **Problem of practice** — a specific, high-leverage problem that practitioners own. *Bites:* start from this and not from a solution you have already chosen.
- **Fishbone / root-cause analysis** — a structured list of causes contributing to a problem, grouped by category. *Bites:* stops the jump to solutions, and it has to be checked against data.
- **Process map** — the actual steps of a workflow. *Bites:* shows handoffs, gaps and waiting. Most leaks happen at handoffs.
- **Driver diagram** — aim → primary drivers → secondary drivers → change ideas. *Bites:* your working theory, the basis for measurement, and a shared language across a network.
- **PDSA cycle** — Plan (including a prediction) → Do (small) → Study (compare with the prediction) → Act (adopt, adapt, abandon). *Bites:* the unit of learning, done weekly and not yearly.
- **Ramps of PDSAs** — a series of cycles that grows in scale and variety of conditions. *Bites:* one teacher, then three, then a department, then the whole school.
- **Practical measure** — a short, frequent, change-specific, low-stakes instrument. *Bites:* the thing most schools lack.
- **Outcome / driver / process / balancing measures** — did the aim move / did the lever move / did we do the change / did we break something else. *Bites:* balancing measures (teacher workload!) are the ones people forget.
- **Common-cause vs. special-cause variation** (Deming) — the system's normal noise vs. a real, assignable signal. *Bites:* stops you from reacting to every weekly wobble.
- **Positive deviance** — people or units that do unusually well under the same constraints. *Bites:* your best source of change ideas is usually inside your own building.
- **Networked improvement community (NIC)** — a network with a shared aim, theory, measures and hub. *Bites:* faster learning. Consider founding or joining one.
- **Analytic hub** — a central team that builds data tools and routines for practitioners (New Visions). *Bites:* a role you should budget for.
- **Learning fast to implement well** — Bryk's slogan for the whole approach. *Bites:* small, fast failures instead of slow, large ones.
- **Solutionitis** (Carnegie usage) — jumping to a favorite fix before understanding the problem. *Bites:* the default failure of school leadership teams.

## Evidence strength & limits
- **What is robust.** The *diagnosis* is well supported. Implementation variation is widespread, average effects hide large context-dependence, and large-scale reforms often fade out; a long line of implementation research makes this point (e.g., the RAND Change Agent studies of the 1970s onward). The *methods* have a strong track record in manufacturing and a substantial one in healthcare, though healthcare's evidence is itself mixed. Systematic reviews of PDSA use in healthcare find that it is often done without rigor (poor documentation, no prediction, cycles that don't build on each other).
- **What the book shows vs. argues.** *Improvement in Action* is a book of **case narratives**, not evaluations. The cases were chosen because they went well (selection on the outcome), the organizations were Carnegie partners, and the accounts rely largely on the organizations' own reports and leaders. There are no comparison groups. The book *shows* that improvement practices can be adopted and linked to improving trends. It does not *prove* that improvement methods caused those trends, and it cannot tell you how often such efforts fail.
- **The one strong external check.** NWP's College-Ready Writers Program has independent RCT evidence (SRI Education) of positive effects on argument writing. It is a combined program (content, professional development and improvement routines), so the RCT supports the whole package, not the improvement component separately.
- **Contested points.**
  - *Scalability and measurability.* Critics (e.g., an *Education Next* piece on obstacles to a culture of improvement) ask whether the approach can work at national scale and produce measurable gains beyond motivated early adopters.
  - *Dependence on leaders.* Menomonee Falls and others depend heavily on exceptional leaders. It is unclear whether the practices survive turnover.
  - *Method drift.* In many districts "continuous improvement" has become a compliance label (PDSA templates filled in after the fact), which empties out the discipline Bryk describes. The book warns against this but can't prevent it.
  - *Weak inference from small tests.* PDSA cycles with n = 1 are open to confirmation bias and regression to the mean. The method depends on run charts, predictions and replication across a network to make up for this, and that works only if the discipline is kept.
  - *Opportunity cost.* Improvement routines use up adult time, which competes with instruction, planning and rest.
- **Scope note.** The cases are mostly **secondary-school, structural outcomes** (graduation, credits, attendance, college access), and those are relatively easy to measure. Classroom-level learning (NWP aside) and elementary-level cases get much less coverage. Don't assume the method transfers equally easily to hard-to-measure goals such as deep understanding or curiosity.

## Institutional design implications
- **Name a small number of improvement aims each year** (2-3), each with a precise aim statement broken out by subgroup. Don't chase ten. [E] for focus as a general principle in improvement practice; [H] for the specific number.
- **Build a student-level funnel and early-warning system from day one** (attendance, course performance, credits or mastery progress, postsecondary steps), visible to advisors weekly. [E] early-warning indicators such as attendance and course failure predict later outcomes well (Chicago Consortium "on-track" research, which Bryk helped establish); [H] that your specific version will improve outcomes.
- **Staff an analytic/improvement role** (a data-product owner or improvement lead serving teachers as internal users), not only a data clerk producing compliance reports. [H], supported by the New Visions and Menomonee Falls cases.
- **Protect weekly inquiry time** in the master schedule for teacher teams to run PDSA cycles and review practical measures. [H] (case-based; expensive in adult time, so measure the cost).
- **Use practical measures, and keep them low-stakes.** Never use improvement data in teacher evaluation. [E] that high-stakes use corrupts indicators (Campbell's law, widely documented); [V] a commitment to trust over surveillance.
- **Always track balancing measures,** especially teacher workload and student wellbeing, alongside each change. [V] with [H].
- **Run blameless reviews of student failures** (course failure, disengagement, dropout) as system incidents, looking for contributing factors. [H] (transfers well from SRE and healthcare; evidence in schools is case-based).
- **Maintain a living driver diagram** for the school's central mission, reviewed each term, with a measure attached to every driver. [H].
- **Train all staff, and eventually students, in basic improvement methods**, as Menomonee Falls did. [H] (single-case evidence).
- **Join or found a networked improvement community** with peer schools working on the same problem. Don't try to learn alone. [H] (strong theoretical argument, mixed empirical base).
- **Pair improvement with proven content.** Adopt evidence-backed practices (retrieval practice, formative assessment, knowledge-rich curriculum) and use improvement to implement them reliably. Don't try to discover pedagogy from scratch through PDSA cycles. [E] for the instructional practices (see other books); [V] for the stance.
- **Treat inequity as variation to design out:** report every key measure by subgroup and by classroom by default. [V] (equity commitment) with [E] that aggregate measures hide gaps.
- **Plan for leadership succession** by building the improvement system into roles, routines and documents so it doesn't depend on one person. [H].

## Connections
- **10-creating-the-schools-our-children-need (Wiliam 2018)** — *strong agreement, useful tension.* Both are skeptical of fashionable reforms and focused on what reliably works. Wiliam puts more weight on choosing high-effect practices (especially teacher formative assessment and teacher learning communities). Bryk puts more weight on the organizational process for implementing anything. A founder needs both: Wiliam's "what," Bryk's "how."
- **07-embedding-formative-assessment (Wiliam & Leahy)** — *deep structural rhyme.* Formative assessment is PDSA at the level of a single lesson: elicit evidence, interpret, adjust. Teacher learning communities are small NICs. Improvement science is formative assessment applied to the institution.
- **09-in-search-of-deeper-learning (Mehta & Fine 2019)** — *productive tension.* Mehta & Fine find deep learning mostly at the edges (electives, clubs) and argue that the "grammar of schooling" resists it. Improvement science is strong on reliability of measurable outcomes and weaker on hard-to-measure depth. Question to carry: can you improve what you can only measure roughly? Mehta & Fine warn that a narrow measurement culture can push out depth.
- **08-extraordinary-learning-for-all (Samouha, Wetzler, Henry Wood)** — *agreement.* That book is about the institutional conditions (coherent design, adult learning, systems) that make excellent learning common rather than rare. Bryk supplies the operating method for building and maintaining those conditions.
- **05-10-to-25 (Yeager 2024)** — *direct link.* David Yeager is a co-author of the Carnegie *Practical Measurement* paper, and his work on mindset and belonging supplied the "productive persistence" drivers used in the Carnegie Math Pathways. His respect-based approach to adolescents fits "user-centered" design when the users are teenagers.
- **06-beyond-the-science-of-reading (Wexler 2025)** and **01-how-learning-happens** — *complementary.* These give the evidence-based content and instructional choices that improvement cycles should *implement*. Tension: improvement enthusiasts sometimes treat local PDSA results as evidence on a par with decades of cognitive science. They aren't equivalent. Settled science should constrain which change ideas you test.
- **04-productive-failure (Kapur)** — *parallel stance.* Productive failure for students and PDSA for adults both treat well-designed failure as data. Different levels, same attitude.
- **00-foundational-papers (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006)** — *tension to manage.* Adult inquiry (PDSA) is good for experienced professionals. Don't carry "discovery" over to novice teachers without structure. New staff need worked examples of improvement work, which is itself a cognitive-load point.

## Retrieval practice

**1. (Recall) Name the six improvement principles from *Learning to Improve*.**
<details><summary>Answer</summary>(1) Make the work problem-specific and user-centered; (2) focus on variation in performance; (3) see the system that produces the current outcomes; (4) we can't improve at scale what we can't measure; (5) anchor improvement in disciplined inquiry (PDSA cycles); (6) accelerate learning through networked improvement communities.</details>

**2. (Recall) What are the four columns of a driver diagram, from left to right?**
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Aim → primary drivers → secondary drivers → change ideas. Each should have an associated measure (outcome measures at the aim, driver measures in the middle, process measures at the change ideas).</details>

**3. (Explain why) Why does Bryk put *variation* at the center instead of average effects?**
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Because the same intervention works in some contexts and fails in others, the average hides the most useful information. The goal is quality reliably at scale, which means understanding and shrinking the bad tail (often the most disadvantaged students), and asking "what works, for whom, under what conditions."</details>

**4. (Spot the misconception) "A PDSA cycle is basically an A/B test for teachers."**
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Wrong. An A/B test uses randomization and a large sample to estimate a causal effect. A PDSA cycle is usually n = 1 with no control group. It is a small, *predicted* test that builds practical knowledge through repetition, run charts and replication across contexts. It relies on writing the prediction down beforehand and building up evidence over many cycles to guard against self-deception, not on statistical power.</details>

**5. (Recall) What makes a measure "practical" in Carnegie's sense?**
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Specific to the change being tested, quick and cheap to collect, taken frequently, predictive of the outcome, returned fast enough to act on, and low stakes (not used to evaluate people). It sits between accountability measures and research measures.</details>

**6. (Apply) Your school's 9th-grade course-failure rate is 30%. A board member says "buy an online tutoring platform." What would Bryk have you do first?**
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Resist the premature fix ("solutionitis"). Break the failures out by course, teacher, subgroup and reason, and do a Pareto on the causes (missing work? failed tests? absences?). Interview students and teachers, build a fishbone and process map of how a struggling student is (or isn't) noticed and helped, then write an aim statement and a driver diagram. Only then test change ideas small, which might or might not include tutoring.</details>

**7. (Explain why) Why do networked improvement communities learn faster than single schools?**
<details><summary>Answer</summary>A single school has few cases and little variation, so it learns slowly and can't tell local luck from real effects. A network with a shared aim, theory, measures and hub runs many tests in parallel across different contexts, pools the data, and can see what holds up across contexts and what depends on local conditions.</details>

**8. (Recall) Which case illustrates an "analytic hub," and what did it do?**
<details><summary>Answer</summary>New Visions for Public Schools (NYC). It built data tools and routines that let counselors and teachers track each student's credits, Regents progress and attendance in real time, and it treated practitioners as the users of those tools, tackling graduation and chronic absenteeism.</details>

**9. (Spot the misconception) "*Improvement in Action* proves that improvement science raises graduation rates."**
<details><summary>Answer</summary>It doesn't prove it. The book is a set of case narratives chosen because they went well, with no comparison groups, written about Carnegie partners and based largely on the organizations' own accounts. It shows that the approach is feasible and associated with improving trends. The NWP College-Ready Writers Program has separate RCT evidence, but for the whole program package.</details>

**10. (Apply) You roll out a new advisory curriculum and attendance rises. What balancing measure should you check, and why?**
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Examples: teacher workload or burnout (advisors' time), instructional time lost from core classes, student stress or wellbeing, or whether grades in other courses dropped. Balancing measures catch the case where improving one metric quietly breaks something else. They work like guardrail metrics in an experiment.</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- **"Improvement = collecting more data."** Data is only useful inside routines where someone looks at it, predicts, acts and learns. Dashboards with no routines behind them are the common failure.
- **"PDSA is a form to fill in."** In many districts it has become a compliance template filled in after the fact. The discipline (prediction first, small scale, honest study, a documented decision) is the whole point.
- **"Improvement replaces research-based practice."** It doesn't. It is a way to implement good ideas reliably in your context. Don't use PDSA to rediscover what cognitive science already settled. Use it to make those findings work here.
- **"It's a bottom-up, teacher-led thing, so leaders can step back."** Every case shows leadership as decisive: setting aims, giving time and roles, making it safe to report failure, modeling inquiry.
- **"Focusing on variation means standardizing teachers."** The goal is to reduce variation in *outcomes*, and particularly to lift the bad tail. It is not uniformity of practice for its own sake. Positive deviants (teachers doing unusually well) are a source of ideas, not something to be flattened.
- **"The software analogy is exact."** It helps in many places (blameless reviews, observability, funnels, staged rollouts, internal tools), but students can't be rolled back, measurement changes behavior, samples are tiny, and the "users" didn't choose to be there. Use the analogy to design the system, not as a guarantee of how it will behave.
- **"This is only for big districts."** Menomonee Falls is small, and the cases include a single network of charter schools. For a founder, the method probably works best at small scale, where routines can be built in from the start.
- **"The cases prove it works."** See evidence limits: the cases show feasibility, not causal efficacy.
