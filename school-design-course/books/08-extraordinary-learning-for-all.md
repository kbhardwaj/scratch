## The book in one sentence
A school worth building is not copied from a model or imposed by experts but designed *by and with* its own community — students, families, educators, leaders, and outside experts — through an iterative, evidence-informed design journey aimed at a shared vision of "extraordinary" outcomes and student experiences, and sustained by the right community conditions, leadership, and policy environment.

## The book in one paragraph
The authors run **Transcend**, a US nonprofit (founded 2015 by Samouha and Wetzler; Henry Wood is its Chief Learning Officer) that supports schools and districts in redesigning learning. The book is their field manual. Part I defines the target: **Graduate Aims** in three areas (intellectual prowess, wayfinding, well-being), synthesized from 30+ outcome frameworks, and ten **Leaps** — shifts in the *student experience* from "industrial-era" schooling toward "equitable, 21st-century learning" (e.g., from irrelevance to relevance, from passive compliance to active self-direction). It then introduces **community-based design**: a **design journey** unique to each community, driven by a **design team**, iterating through a four-stage **design cycle**, producing a **school design blueprint** (aims and guiding concepts → student experience → school/system "elements"), watched over by attention to **community conditions** and usually supported by an external **design partner**. Part II tells four design-journey stories: Van Ness Elementary (DC Public Schools), Northern Cass (rural North Dakota district), Brooklyn STEAM Center (NYC career and technical education), and Intrinsic Public Schools (Chicago charter). Part III is a how-to: **Map It** (high-level vision for the whole school), **Zoom In** (detailed design of one part of the student experience), **Test-Drive** (pilot and refine), **Look Ahead**, then scale from test-drive to full speed, re-zoom, re-map, and do routine maintenance. Part IV argues that **learner-centered leadership** and the **policy environment** (accountability, seat time, funding, credentialing) decide whether designs survive. The conclusion ("You Are Not Alone") points readers toward a growing movement and network. It is persuasive, practical, and well organized — but it is a **practitioner framework written by the organization that sells the process**, and its evidence is mostly design rationale, case narrative, and correlational survey data rather than causal outcome evaluations.

## Why it's in this curriculum (the question to read it with)
Every other book in this curriculum tells you *what* should happen in a learner's head or a classroom. This one answers: **"How do you get a whole community — with its politics, legacy habits, and competing goals — to agree on, design, pilot, and sustain a different kind of school?"** For a founder, it is the closest thing in the curriculum to a product-development methodology for schools (discovery → vision → spec → prototype → iterate → scale).

Read it with this question: *"Which parts of this process are genuine design discipline that would raise the odds my school works, and which parts are value commitments or organizational branding that I should adopt deliberately, not by default?"* And a second: *"Where does community voice improve design, and where must it be bounded by what the learning sciences (books 00-07, 12-13) say actually works?"*

## Chapter-by-chapter: the salient knowledge

Sourcing note: Chapter 1 and 2 claims marked (V) are confirmed by Wiley's chapter abstracts. Leaps wording is confirmed by Transcend materials surfaced in search. Case-school facts are confirmed against independent sources where cited. ### Introduction
- **Core claims:** The dominant US school model was designed roughly a century ago for an industrial economy: age-graded cohorts, seat-time, standardized pacing, teacher-at-the-front, sorting. It "works" for some students and fails many, and the failures fall predictably along lines of race and income. Tinkering at the edges (a new curriculum, a new program) leaves the underlying model intact.
- The authors frame the present as a moment of unusual urgency: chronic absenteeism and disengagement, a youth mental-health crisis, declining enrollment, and fast-changing work (including AI) — and unusual opportunity, because communities are more open to rethinking school than before.
- Their answer is neither "adopt this model" nor "let every teacher innovate alone," but **community-based design**: communities build their own vision and school, with help.
- **So-what:** The book positions school design as *system redesign*, not program adoption. For a founder building from scratch, you have the rare advantage of not needing to dismantle an existing model — but you still need the community-building half of the process, because families, staff and students must own the model for it to survive the first hard year.

### Ch 1 — Extraordinary Experiences and Outcomes
- **Core claim (V):** "Extraordinary learning for all" is defined by two linked things: the **outcomes** young people need to thrive and "transform their worlds," and the **experiences** (activities and interactions in school) that build those outcomes.
- **Graduate Aims (V):** From a review of 30+ outcome frameworks (e.g., deeper-learning competencies, SEL frameworks, 21st-century skills lists, portrait-of-a-graduate documents), the authors group outcomes into three areas:
  - **Intellectual prowess** — deep knowledge and the ability to use it: literacy, numeracy, content knowledge, critical thinking, problem solving, creativity.
  - **Wayfinding** — the ability to navigate one's own path: sense of purpose and identity, self-direction, agency, career and life navigation, the ability to act on the world (including civic and social contribution).
  - **Well-being** — physical, mental, emotional and social health; relationships; resilience.
- **The Leaps (V — framework; wording from Transcend materials):** Ten shifts in the student *experience*, each named as "from [industrial-era pattern] to [21st-century pattern]," which Transcend says are grounded in the science of learning and development:
  1. From Narrow Focus → **Whole-Child Focus**
  2. From Rote Activity → **Rigorous Learning**
  3. From Irrelevance → **Relevance**
  4. From Assimilation & Marginalization → **Affirmation of Self & Others**
  5. From Reinforcement of the Status Quo → **Social Consciousness & Action**
  6. From Isolation → **Connection & Community**
  7. From Inflexible Systems → **Customization**
  8. From Passive Compliance → **Active Self-Direction**
  9. From Siloed Schooling → **Anytime, Anywhere Learning**
  10. From Unequal Expectations & Opportunities → **High Expectations with Unlimited Opportunities**
  (Transcend first published the Leaps in 2020. Minor wording differs across versions and translations.)
- **Key point:** The Leaps are not a model; they are a *design language*. A community prioritizes a few Leaps (nobody leaps all ten at once) and designs toward them. Transcend's **Leaps Student Voice Survey** (launched 2021, with technical reports in 2022 and 2024) measures how students experience each Leap, turning the vision into something you can track.
- **Evidence cited:** The "science of learning and development" (SoLD) literature — e.g., the Darling-Hammond, Cantor, Osher et al. synthesis papers (c. 2018–2020) that the Transcend team draws on — plus research on belonging, relevance, agency, and culturally affirming practice. The *direction* of each Leap is supportable; the *package* is a synthesis, not a tested intervention.
- **So-what:** Write your outcomes (Graduate Aims) and your student-experience targets (your version of the Leaps) *before* you design structures. Then everything downstream (schedule, staffing, assessment, space) is judged by whether it produces those experiences. Watch for conflict with the Minds-level books: "Rigorous Learning" and "Active Self-Direction" must be reconciled with cognitive load and the novice/expert distinction (see Connections).

### Ch 2 — Community-Based Design
The load-bearing chapter for process. Its five named components (all V from Wiley's abstract):
- **Design journey** — the whole multi-year arc. Every community's journey is unique, shaped by its history, conditions and needs. The book rejects a single recipe even while offering a structure.
- **Design cycle** — a **four-stage iterative process** that refines ideas over time and produces better implementation. (The stage names are not visible in the sources I could reach. From Transcend's broader materials, the cycle runs roughly: *understand/learn* (research the need and the evidence) → *envision/design* (generate and choose ideas) → *try/test* (prototype, pilot) → *reflect/refine* (learn from data and iterate). Treat these labels as my reconstruction, not the book's wording.) The same cycle is repeated at every scale: whole-school vision, one component, one pilot.
- **School design blueprint** — the product of design, with three layers:
  1. **Goals and guiding concepts** — Graduate Aims, prioritized Leaps, core beliefs about learning.
  2. **Student experience** — what a day, week, year actually looks and feels like for a learner.
  3. **School and system "elements"** — the enabling structures: schedule, staffing and roles, curriculum and instruction, assessment and grading, culture and routines, space, technology, family and community partnerships, budget, operations, and policy.
  The logic runs top-down: aims → experience → elements. The common failure is designing elements first (a new schedule, a device rollout) with no clear experience they are meant to produce.
- **Community conditions** — "guideposts" for judging how healthy the soil is for new ideas to take root. (Exact list not visible to me; Transcend's materials emphasize things like shared urgency and vision, trust and relationships, inclusive participation, leadership commitment, capacity and time, and a supportive policy/resource context. Treat as reconstruction.) Conditions are monitored and actively cultivated, not assumed.
- **Design partner** — an outside organization or person (Transcend's own role, obviously) who keeps the community on track, brings research and examples from elsewhere, facilitates, and holds the process. Note the conflict of interest: the book is partly an argument for the service its authors provide.
- **Design team** (background knowledge; consistent with Transcend's public descriptions): a diverse group including school leaders, teachers, students, caregivers, and community members, which runs the journey, conducts empathy research (interviews, focus groups, surveys, shadowing students), studies research and exemplar schools, and makes design decisions.
- **So-what:** This is recognizably human-centered/design-thinking product development applied to schools, with two additions that matter: (1) an explicit **equity lens** — those historically least served get the most voice, and (2) explicit tending of **community conditions** — because a school is a social system, not a product; a technically good design that the community does not own will be quietly reverted.

### Part II — Stories from Four Community Design Journeys (Ch 3–6)
All four are real Transcend partner communities. The chapters are narrative case studies: the starting point, who was on the team, what they learned, which Leaps they chose, what they designed and piloted, what went wrong, and what they are seeing. The case facts below are verified against independent sources where noted; details of *how the book tells each story* come from my background knowledge and should be treated as approximate.

#### Ch 3 — DC Public Schools' Van Ness Elementary | Washington, DC
- **Context (verified: CZI case study, Transcend/Edutopia pages):** A neighborhood DCPS elementary school closed for years and reopened (2015) in response to community demand in a gentrifying neighborhood. Founding principal Cynthia Robinson-Rivers co-designed the vision with families.
- **Design focus:** The **Whole-Child Model** — every child greeted each morning by an adult, sets a goal for the day, eats breakfast, and takes part in community-building and self-regulation practices (morning meeting); explicit teaching of emotional regulation, stress management and conflict skills; restorative approaches; strong family partnership. Transcend helped **codify** the model so other schools could adopt it; it has been adopted by schools in several states.
- **Leaps emphasized:** Whole-Child Focus; Connection & Community; Affirmation of Self & Others.
- **So-what:** A public district school (not a charter, not a private school) can run a genuine design journey inside ordinary constraints. And codification — turning tacit practice into written, teachable routines — is what makes a model spread. For a founder: your culture and routines are part of the design, and they need a spec, not just a feeling.

#### Ch 4 — Northern Cass | Hunter, North Dakota
- **Context (verified: Aurora Institute, KnowledgeWorks, District Administration):** A small rural PK-12 district (~660 learners, ~25 miles from Fargo) led by Superintendent Cory Steiner that moved to **personalized, competency-based learning**: learners advance on demonstrated mastery rather than seat time; grade levels being phased out; no percentage or letter grades; students decide when they are ready for assessment; flexible small groups regrouped every few weeks. Steiner also testified to the North Dakota legislature for policy flexibility — a live example for Ch 11.
- **Design focus:** Customization; Active Self-Direction; High Expectations. Big structural moves (grading, progression, scheduling) rather than a single program.
- **So-what:** Competency-based progression is the deepest structural change in the book, and it collides with the whole accountability and transcript system. Rural districts can move fast because the community is small and trust is high — but they also show how much work sits in the *elements* layer (reporting to families, college transcripts, teacher workload, tracking mastery). Evidence caution: competency-based education overall has a mixed and thin causal evidence base (see Evidence section).

#### Ch 5 — Brooklyn STEAM Center | Brooklyn, New York
- **Context (verified: Brooklyn Navy Yard/Transcend pages):** A shared career-and-technical-education (CTE) site in the Brooklyn Navy Yard, a partnership between the Navy Yard Development Corporation and NYC Department of Education. Juniors and seniors from several home high schools spend half the day in industry-aligned pathways (e.g., culinary, film and media, computer science, design and engineering, construction), with industry professionals and internships. After about two years of operation it joined the **New Schools Design Collaborative** (Transcend + NewSchools Venture Fund) for a ~10-month design journey; one visible result is **workplace immersion** — learning organized like a real workplace. Stated aim: economic mobility and wealth creation for predominantly Black and Latinx students.
- **Leaps emphasized:** Relevance; Anytime, Anywhere Learning; High Expectations with Unlimited Opportunities; Wayfinding outcomes.
- **So-what:** Design can happen at the level of a *program or site*, not just a whole school. Employer partners are co-designers, not just placement hosts. For a founder: if relevance and career navigation are in your Graduate Aims, the structure (half-day blocks, off-site space, industry staff) must be designed for it from day one.

#### Ch 6 — Intrinsic Public Schools | Chicago, Illinois
- **Context (verified: Transcend, The 74, EdWeek, architecture coverage):** A Chicago charter network (opened 2013, grades 7–12) built for **blended learning** in a converted lumberyard with large open "pods" instead of classrooms; humanities and STEM halves; students move among different learning environments (small-group instruction, collaborative work, independent work with playlists). The community prioritized customization of focus, pace and sequence; relevance; and whole-child focus. Transcend reports a 95% graduation rate and 70% college enrollment for the class of 2024 (organization-reported, uncontrolled).
- **So-what:** The shortest case chapter, and the one where **space** is most visibly a design element: the building embodies the model. It also shows a charter that already had an innovative model re-entering the design cycle to evolve it — i.e., design is continuous, not a one-time founding act. The 74's coverage framed its lesson as innovation *plus* rigorous instruction — a useful corrective to "innovation alone."

**Cross-case lessons the book draws** (my synthesis of its argument): start from community-defined aims; prioritize a few Leaps; pilot small before going school-wide; codify what works; expect setbacks in year one; protect time for the design team; bring students into design roles, not only as interview subjects.

### Ch 7 — Launching Your Design Journey
- **Core content:** How to start: assemble a diverse design team and define roles; secure leadership commitment and time; understand the need (student, family and staff voice; data; the history of the community, including past reforms that failed); learn from research and exemplars; then **Map It (V)** — craft a *high-level vision for the entire school or system*: Graduate Aims, prioritized Leaps, and a first sketch of the blueprint. (Only the Map It heading is verified; the preceding steps are my reconstruction of Transcend's standard sequence.)
- **Participation mechanics** (background knowledge; consistent with Transcend's public materials): empathy interviews and focus groups with students and families; "learning journeys" (visits, real or virtual, to other schools); community sessions to react to draft visions; deliberate recruitment of voices that are usually absent (students who are struggling or disengaged, families who don't attend meetings, non-English speakers).
- **So-what:** Map It is your founding charter document. Keep it short enough to guide daily decisions; broad enough to cover all of Graduate Aims; specific enough to reject ideas that don't fit.

### Ch 8 — Deepening Your Design Journey
- **Zoom In (V):** Pick *one specific aspect of the student experience* (e.g., the first 30 minutes of the day; how students get feedback; a ninth-grade advisory; a math block) and design it in detail against the chosen Leaps. The book's logic: you cannot redesign everything at once, so choose a high-leverage entry point where the need is strongest and conditions are best.
- **Test-Drive (V):** Pilot the zoomed-in design with a small group; collect data (including student-experience survey data and observation); refine. A pilot has explicit learning questions and success measures set in advance.
- **Look Ahead (V):** Reflect on what the pilot taught, decide what to keep, change, or drop, and plan the next cycle; prepare the community for the next stage.
- **So-what:** This is the minimum-viable-product move. For a founder, it argues for launching with a few fully designed, well-run signature experiences rather than a hundred half-built innovations.

### Ch 9 — Advancing and Sustaining Your Design Journey
- **From Test-Drive to Full Speed (V):** Scaling a successful pilot across grades or the whole school — which exposes new constraints (staffing, schedule, budget, training).
- **Zoom In again (V):** Design additional components, one at a time.
- **Map It again (V):** Refine the high-level vision in light of what has been learned — the vision is a living document.
- **Routine maintenance (V):** Build ongoing habits — regular data review, student feedback loops, onboarding new staff into the model, protecting the model against drift — so that the design survives leadership and staff turnover.
- **So-what:** The authors treat sustainability as a design problem. This chapter pairs naturally with Bryk's improvement science (11): Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles, networks, and measuring variation.

### Ch 10 — Your Leadership Matters
- **Core claim (V):** Redesign is "a special kind of challenge" that needs a special kind of leadership — **learner-centered leadership** (V: "What learner-centered leaders do").
- **Content** (background knowledge, approximate): Leaders model learning themselves; share power with the design team and students; hold the vision while tolerating ambiguity; create psychological safety for experimentation and failure; manage the pace of change against staff capacity; and buffer the design team from pressures (e.g., test-prep panic). This echoes the adaptive-versus-technical leadership distinction (Heifetz) — whether the book cites Heifetz directly I could not verify.
- **So-what:** In founding a school, the leader's job is less "chief architect" and more "chief facilitator of a design process" — but with the authority to make calls when the community stalls.

### Ch 11 — The Policy Environment Around You Matters
- **Core claim:** Local design runs into state and district policy: seat-time and Carnegie-unit rules, standardized testing and accountability systems, teacher certification and staffing rules, funding formulas, graduation requirements and transcripts, and procurement. The authors urge communities to know which rules are actually binding (many are assumed), use waivers and innovation zones, and advocate for policy that rewards learner-centered design (e.g., competency-based credit, broader accountability indicators, funding for design time). (Chapter title verified; content from background knowledge.)
- **Example:** Northern Cass leaders working with the North Dakota legislature on flexibility for competency-based learning (verified in legislative testimony records).
- **So-what:** For a founder, map your regulatory envelope early — charter law, private-school regulations, accreditation, graduation credits, testing obligations — because it constrains your blueprint's elements layer.

### Conclusion — You Are Not Alone
- A call to join a growing movement: other communities, networks and tools exist (Transcend's design resources and Leaps survey; the Canopy project mapping innovative schools, which Transcend co-leads with the Center on Reinventing Public Education). Encouragement to start small and start now.

## The 5-10 ideas you must carry out of this book
1. **Design top-down through three layers: aims → student experience → school elements.** Define what graduates should be able to do and what students should experience every day before choosing schedules, staffing, tech or space; judge every element by whether it produces the target experience.
2. **The student experience is the unit of design and measurement.** The Leaps turn values into observable experiences, and student-experience surveys give a near-real-time leading indicator long before test scores move. (Transcend's own data link better-reported experiences with better grades, attendance and discipline — correlational.)
3. **Community ownership is a design requirement, not a courtesy.** Models imposed from outside get reverted; a diverse design team — including students and the families least served — surfaces needs experts miss and builds the coalition that sustains the school.
4. **Iterate: Map It → Zoom In → Test-Drive → Look Ahead → repeat.** Don't redesign everything at once. Pilot a few high-leverage experiences, measure, refine, then scale and re-map.
5. **Tend the soil (community conditions) as deliberately as the design.** Trust, urgency, shared vision, leadership commitment and capacity determine whether a good design takes root.
6. **Codify what works.** Van Ness spread because its whole-child practices were written down as teachable routines; tacit culture doesn't survive turnover.
7. **Leadership and policy are part of the design.** Learner-centered leaders share power and protect experimentation; policy (seat time, testing, credits, funding) sets the envelope, so map it and push on it.
8. **Outcomes are broader than academics — but academics are one of three pillars.** Intellectual prowess, wayfinding and well-being are all Graduate Aims; the framework is "and," not "instead of."

## Mental models & vocabulary
- **Extraordinary learning for all** — the target state: every student, not just the advantaged, reaches ambitious outcomes through high-quality experiences. *Bites:* makes equity a design criterion for every decision.
- **Graduate Aims** — the book's three outcome areas: intellectual prowess, wayfinding, well-being. *Bites:* your "portrait of a graduate"; it drives assessment and curriculum choices.
- **Intellectual prowess** — deep knowledge plus the capacity to use it critically and creatively. *Bites:* where the Minds books (knowledge, cognitive load) plug in.
- **Wayfinding** — the ability to find and navigate one's path: purpose, identity, agency, life and career navigation. *Bites:* advisory, career-connected learning, student-led conferences.
- **Well-being** — physical, mental, social-emotional health and relationships. *Bites:* schedule, counseling, culture, discipline.
- **Leaps (for Equitable, 21st-Century Learning)** — ten from→to shifts in student experience. *Bites:* choose 2-4 to prioritize; they become your experience specs and survey measures.
- **Industrial-era model** — the inherited age-graded, seat-time, one-pace school. *Bites:* the default you drift back to unless you design against it.
- **Community-based design** — a local, collaborative process in which students, families, educators, administrators and experts design learning together. *Bites:* your founding and annual planning process.
- **Design journey** — the multi-year, community-specific arc of redesign. *Bites:* set expectations that the school is never "done."
- **Design team** — the diverse group that runs the journey. *Bites:* composition decides whose problems get solved.
- **Design cycle** — the four-stage iterative loop used at every scale. *Bites:* the rhythm for pilots and yearly review.
- **School design blueprint** — the three-layer spec (aims and guiding concepts; student experience; school/system elements). *Bites:* your founding document and the thing you hand new hires.
- **Elements** — the enabling structures (schedule, staffing, curriculum, assessment, culture, space, tech, partnerships, budget, policy). *Bites:* where most money and conflict lives.
- **Community conditions** — the "soil health" factors that decide whether new ideas take root. *Bites:* diagnose before launching major change.
- **Design partner** — outside facilitator/expert that keeps the journey on track. *Bites:* useful, but watch for dependence and for vendor bias.
- **Map It / Zoom In / Test-Drive / Look Ahead** — the practical moves: whole-school vision; detailed design of one component; pilot and refine; plan the next cycle.
- **Full speed / routine maintenance** — scaling a successful pilot; the ongoing habits that prevent drift.
- **Learner-centered leadership** — leadership that models learning, shares power, and protects experimentation. *Bites:* hiring your head of school.
- **Codification** — turning practice into written, teachable routines. *Bites:* the difference between a heroic school and a replicable one.
- **Leaps Student Voice Survey** — Transcend's validated survey of student experience on each Leap (Pulse Check plus deep-dive scales). *Bites:* a ready-made leading indicator.

## Evidence strength & limits
**What kind of book this is.** A practitioner framework and advocacy book by the leaders of the organization that runs this process for a living. It argues and illustrates far more than it demonstrates. That's not a flaw for its purpose — it's a how-to — but a founder should weigh its claims accordingly.

**Relatively robust:**
- The *general* claim that students' experiences of belonging, relevance, relationships and agency relate to engagement, attendance and achievement is supported by a large research literature (e.g., the science of learning and development syntheses; belonging and "wise intervention" research — see Yeager, 05). [E, correlational to moderate-causal depending on construct]
- The survey instrument: the Leaps Student Voice Survey has technical reports (2022, 2024) with factor analyses, reliability and bias (differential item functioning) checks. It is a reasonable *measurement* tool. [E for psychometrics]
- The implementation-science point that reforms imposed without local buy-in and capacity tend to fade or be reverted is consistent with decades of implementation research (and with Bryk, 11). [E]

**Thin or contested:**
- **No causal evaluation of the design process itself.** The book does not present randomized or strong quasi-experimental evidence that schools doing community-based design outperform comparable schools. Transcend reports that 70%+ of partner schools "demonstrably improve student experiences associated with outcomes" within three years — an organization-reported metric about *experiences*, without a comparison group.
- **The "Beyond Fine" report (Transcend, Sept 2025;** published after the book; ~70,000 student responses) finds better-reported experiences are associated with higher test scores, GPA and attendance and fewer disciplinary incidents, and that schools that regularly use student feedback have better experiences and outcomes. It's large and useful, but **correlational and self-published**; selection (more functional schools both listen to students *and* get better results) is an obvious alternative explanation.
- **Case studies are success stories selected by the authors**, told with partner cooperation. Outcomes reported (e.g., Intrinsic's 95% graduation / 70% college enrollment for 2024) are uncontrolled, and we don't hear about Transcend partners where journeys stalled.
- **Specific practices endorsed in the cases have mixed evidence:** competency-based education (Northern Cass) has a limited, mixed causal literature (e.g., RAND evaluations of personalized learning, c. 2015–2017, found modest and variable effects); blended "playlist" models vary widely in quality; CTE with work-based learning has some positive quasi-experimental evidence (e.g., career academies, P-TECH-type evaluations), which supports Brooklyn STEAM's direction more than the others.
- **The Leaps package** is a values-plus-research synthesis. Several Leaps ("Social Consciousness & Action," "Affirmation of Self & Others") are primarily value commitments. "Active Self-Direction" and "Anytime, Anywhere Learning" are in tension with evidence that novices learn best with explicit guidance and structure (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006; 00, 01). The book does not deeply work through that tension.
- **Community voice ≠ correct design.** Communities can converge on popular but ineffective ideas (e.g., learning styles, unstructured discovery). The book relies on the design partner and research-review steps to catch this, but offers little on how to adjudicate when community preference conflicts with evidence.
- **Cost and time:** A UK review (ICT in Education) noted the collaboration, time and funding required act as barriers to entry; the book is lighter on budget and staffing realities than on vision.

**Bottom line:** Treat it as a strong *process* and *vocabulary* for founding and evolving a school, and a reasonable *measurement* approach for student experience — not as proof that any particular model or the process itself produces better learning.

## Institutional design implications
- Write Graduate Aims across all three areas (intellectual prowess, wayfinding, well-being) before any structural decisions. [V]
- Make academic knowledge and skill an explicit, non-negotiable part of "intellectual prowess" so broader aims don't crowd out core learning. [V, with E support from the Minds books]
- Choose 2-4 priority Leaps for your first years rather than claiming all ten. [H]
- Specify the target *student experience* (a concrete day/week) as the middle layer of your blueprint, and derive schedule, staffing and space from it. [H]
- Measure student experience every term with a validated instrument (e.g., the Leaps survey or equivalent) as a leading indicator, alongside academic measures. [E for measurement validity; H that it predicts outcomes causally]
- Form a founding design team that includes students and families, deliberately recruiting those least likely to show up. [V; H that it improves design quality; E that buy-in aids sustainment]
- Bound community voice with an evidence review: every major design choice gets checked against the learning-science consensus before adoption. [H — this is our addition, not the book's emphasis]
- Launch with a few fully designed signature experiences and pilot them with explicit success measures before scaling. [H; consistent with E from improvement science]
- Codify routines and culture into written playbooks for onboarding and for protecting against drift. [H]
- Budget protected time for design and review (e.g., design team hours, summer design sprints). [H]
- Hire and develop learner-centered leaders who share power but can make decisive calls. [H]
- Map your policy envelope (charter/private regs, accreditation, graduation credits, testing, seat time) before finalizing elements such as competency-based progression. [E that policy constrains; the specific design is H]

## Connections
- **11-improvement-in-action (Bryk 2020)** — strongly aligned: iterative testing, local ownership, networks, measuring what matters. Bryk is the more rigorous methodology for *improvement*; this book is stronger on *vision and community design*. Use Bryk's PDSA discipline inside Transcend's Test-Drive step.
- **09-in-search-of-deeper-learning (Mehta & Fine 2019)** — overlap on the vision (mastery, identity, creativity) and on why deeper learning is rare; Mehta & Fine are more skeptical and show how pockets of deep learning coexist with conventional structures. Their finding that the best learning often happens at the "periphery" (electives, clubs) supports designing the whole experience, as Transcend urges.
- **10-creating-the-schools-our-children-need (Wiliam 2018)** — tension: Wiliam argues schools should prioritize the few things with the strongest evidence (teacher quality, formative assessment, knowledge-rich curriculum) and is skeptical of many innovation agendas. Read both to calibrate how much innovation to attempt.
- **07-embedding-formative-assessment (Wiliam & Leahy)** — a high-evidence practice that fits any blueprint; it is what "Test-Drive" should look like at classroom level.
- **06-beyond-the-science-of-reading (Wexler 2025)** — tension/corrective: Wexler's emphasis on knowledge-building curriculum and explicit writing instruction checks the risk that "relevance" and "self-direction" dilute content.
- **00-foundational-papers / 01-how-learning-happens** — tension: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) and cognitive load theory warn against minimally guided learning for novices; "Active Self-Direction" and "Anytime, Anywhere Learning" must be designed with strong guidance.
- **04-productive-failure (Kapur 2024)** — partial bridge: structured problem-solving before instruction offers a principled way to deliver "rigor" and "agency" without abandoning explicit instruction.
- **05-10-to-25 (Yeager 2024)** — aligned: status, respect, purpose and belonging give the psychological mechanism behind several Leaps (relevance, connection, self-direction); Yeager's mentor mindset is a good "learner-centered" practice.
- **03-intellectual-lives-of-children (Engel 2021)** — aligned on curiosity and purposeful intellectual activity as design goals.
- **02-scienceblind, 13-learning-scientific-concepts** — reminder that "intellectual prowess" includes hard conceptual change, which requires carefully sequenced instruction, not just relevant experiences.

## Retrieval practice

1. What are the three areas of the book's Graduate Aims?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Intellectual prowess, wayfinding, and well-being — synthesized from a review of 30+ outcome frameworks.</details>

2. Name the three layers of the school design blueprint, in order.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>(1) Goals and guiding concepts (aims, Leaps, beliefs); (2) the student experience; (3) school and system elements (schedule, staffing, curriculum, assessment, space, etc.). Design flows from aims to experience to elements.</details>

3. Why do the authors insist on community-based design rather than adopting a proven model from elsewhere?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Because each community has its own history, conditions and needs; imposed models lack ownership and tend to be reverted; and the people closest to the problem (students, families, teachers) surface needs outside experts miss. The book pairs this with learning from research and exemplars, so it's "adapt with evidence," not "invent from nothing."</details>

4. Explain the difference between "Map It" and "Zoom In."
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Map It creates a high-level vision for the whole school or system (aims, priority Leaps, a sketch of the blueprint). Zoom In designs one specific aspect of the student experience in detail, to be test-driven.</details>

5. Scenario: Your founding team wants to launch with personalized playlists, a competency-based transcript, a new advisory, project weeks and a 1:1 laptop program — all in year one. What would the book advise?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Don't redesign everything at once. Use Map It to set the vision, then Zoom In on the highest-leverage one or two components, Test-Drive them with clear success measures, learn, and scale. Also check community conditions and capacity; and (from the curriculum's other books) check each idea against evidence before piloting.</details>

6. Spot the misconception: "The Leaps are a school model — adopt all ten and you have a 21st-century school."
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The Leaps are a design language describing shifts in student experience, not a model. Communities prioritize a few and design their own structures to achieve them; nobody makes all ten leaps at once.</details>

7. What is the strongest evidence the book (and Transcend) can point to, and what kind of evidence is it?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Student-experience survey data (the psychometrically validated Leaps Student Voice Survey and the later ~70,000-student "Beyond Fine" analysis) showing better-reported experiences correlate with better grades, test scores and attendance, plus case narratives. It is correlational and self-reported by the organization; there's no causal evaluation of the design process itself.</details>

8. Which case school best illustrates codification, and why does codification matter?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Van Ness Elementary: its whole-child practices (morning greeting, goal-setting, breakfast, community-building, self-regulation) were codified into the Whole-Child Model, which other schools adopted. Codification turns tacit culture into teachable routines, making it survive staff turnover and spread.</details>

9. Scenario: Your family focus groups strongly favor letting students learn "in their own learning style." How should a design team using this book handle it?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Take the underlying need seriously (students feel instruction doesn't fit them — a Customization/Relevance concern), but run the idea through the research-review step: learning-styles matching is not supported by evidence. Redesign toward evidence-based customization (pace, formative feedback, choice of context) instead. Community voice sets needs and priorities; evidence constrains solutions.</details>

10. Why does the book give a whole chapter to policy, and what's one policy that commonly constrains design?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Because local designs hit state/district rules that can block or erode them. Examples: seat-time/Carnegie-unit credit rules, testing and accountability, certification rules, graduation requirements, funding formulas. Northern Cass sought legislative flexibility for competency-based learning.</details>

## Common misreadings of this book
- **"It's anti-academic."** No — intellectual prowess is one of the three Graduate Aims, and "Rigorous Learning" and "High Expectations" are Leaps. The risk is in implementation, where broad aims can crowd out knowledge building; guard against it deliberately.
- **"Community design means the community votes on pedagogy."** The process combines community voice with research, exemplars and a design partner. Voice defines needs and priorities; it doesn't validate every solution.
- **"The four schools prove the approach works."** They are selected, narrated success stories without comparison groups. They show feasibility and texture, not effectiveness.
- **"It's a one-time founding process."** The book stresses re-mapping, re-zooming and routine maintenance; design is permanent.
- **"The Leaps are a checklist."** They are directions of travel to prioritize, and several are value commitments, not empirical findings.
- **"You need Transcend to do this."** The book promotes design partners (its authors' business), but the process — team, vision, blueprint, pilots, iteration — can be run in-house if you build the capacity.
- **"Student-experience data is a substitute for learning outcomes."** It's a leading indicator that correlates with outcomes; you still need direct measures of what students know and can do.
